# Alerting Strategy: SLI → SLO → SLA Framework — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is an SLI, and what makes a good one?**
A: A Service Level Indicator is the actual measured signal that reflects user experience — a quantitative metric you track continuously. Examples: request success rate, p99 latency, checkout completion rate. A good SLI is:
- **User-centric** — measures something a user actually feels, not internal noise like CPU%.
- **Actionable** — when it degrades, it points you toward a problem worth fixing.
- **Measurable with low noise** — stable enough to distinguish signal from random variation.
- **Scoped correctly** — measured at the right boundary (e.g., from the load balancer, not just one microservice, so it reflects end-to-end experience).

**Q2. What is an SLO, and how does it relate to an SLI?**
A: An SLO (Service Level Objective) is the target value or range you set for an SLI, measured over a defined window. Where the SLI is the measurement ("our success rate is 99.3%"), the SLO is the commitment ("our success rate must stay above 99.5% over a 30-day rolling window"). SLOs are internal targets — they're the engineering team's promise to the product/business, and they serve as the basis for alerting and prioritization decisions.

**Q3. What is an SLA, and how does it differ from an SLO?**
A: An SLA (Service Level Agreement) is an external, contractual commitment to customers — typically with financial or legal consequences for breach (credits, refunds, contract termination). The SLA is almost always set *looser* than the SLO intentionally: if your SLO is 99.9% availability, your SLA might commit to 99.5%. This gap gives the engineering team a private safety margin — you can breach your SLO, investigate, and remediate before you ever breach the SLA and trigger customer penalties.

**Q4. What's the relationship between the three — how do they connect in practice?**
A: They form a cascade of increasing consequence:
- **SLI** — the raw measurement: "what is the system actually doing?"
- **SLO** — the internal target: "is it doing well enough for our users?"
- **SLA** — the external contract: "have we failed our customers badly enough to owe them something?"

A healthy system has SLIs being monitored constantly, SLOs being consumed slowly without breach, and the SLA never being touched. Alerting fires on SLO risk (burning through error budget), not on SLA breach — by the time you breach the SLA, it's already a serious incident.

**Q5. Why not just alert on raw infrastructure metrics like CPU, memory, or disk instead of SLIs?**
A: Infrastructure metrics are *symptoms*, not causes of user pain — they're several steps removed from what the user experiences. CPU at 90% may not be hurting users at all (maybe the workload is batch, not latency-sensitive). Conversely, a disk-full event on a logging partition won't appear in CPU metrics but will crash your service. SLI-based alerting cuts through this indirection: if your success rate is dropping, users are definitely affected, regardless of what the underlying infra metric looks like. Infrastructure metrics are still useful for diagnosis once an SLI alert fires, but they make poor primary alert triggers.

## Section 2: Defining SLIs

**Q6. What are the most common SLI categories, and which services do they apply to?**
A: Google's SRE book defines four classic SLI categories:
- **Availability** — proportion of requests that succeeded. Applies to nearly every service.
- **Latency** — proportion of requests served below a threshold (e.g., < 200ms). Applies to user-facing and API services.
- **Throughput** — rate of data/work processed successfully. Applies to data pipelines, streaming, batch jobs.
- **Error rate** — proportion of requests that returned an error. Overlaps with availability; often tracked separately for finer classification (5xx vs 4xx, client vs server errors).
- **Freshness / staleness** — how recent the data is. Applies to caches, feeds, reporting pipelines ("data must be no more than 5 minutes stale").
- **Correctness** — whether the output is right. Applies to data processing jobs, ML inference, and financial calculations.

**Q7. How do you decide which SLIs to track for a service?**
A: Start with the user journey. Ask: "What does a user do with this service, and what would they notice if it broke?" For an e-commerce checkout API: the user submits an order and expects a confirmation. The SLIs that capture that experience are: request success rate (did the order go through?), latency (did it respond in time?), and maybe correctness (was the inventory correctly decremented?). For a backend pipeline the user never directly touches, freshness and throughput may matter more than latency.

**Q8. Should 4xx client errors count against your availability SLI?**
A: Usually not entirely — it depends on the error type. A 400 Bad Request caused by a malformed user payload is a client error, not a service failure; counting it against availability would let a flood of bad clients degrade your SLI unfairly. However, a 404 on a valid deep link that used to work is a server-side regression and *should* count. In practice, teams either exclude 4xx entirely or include only specific codes (e.g., 429 Too Many Requests may or may not count depending on whether rate limiting is expected behavior). The rule: if the user did everything right and still got an error, count it.

**Q9. What is a "good event" vs "valid event" in SLI framing?**
A: A valid event is one that should be counted in the SLI denominator — a real user request, not a health check ping or a pre-flight OPTIONS request. A good event is a valid event that was also handled successfully — returned a 2xx, completed in time, or produced correct output. Your SLI = `good events / valid events`. Getting the denominator right matters: including health checks inflates your apparent availability; excluding legitimate user traffic understates risk.

## Section 3: Setting SLOs

**Q10. How do you decide what number to set as an SLO target?**
A: Several inputs should inform the decision:
- **Historical baseline** — look at what your service has actually achieved over the last 90 days. Setting an SLO tighter than your historical performance guarantees you're already in breach.
- **User research / product requirements** — what latency or reliability level is the product actually built around? A banking app has different expectations than a social media feed.
- **Business impact** — how much does failure actually cost? High-revenue, high-sensitivity services get tighter SLOs; internal tooling gets looser ones.
- **Cost of improvement** — engineering a 99.9% → 99.99% jump is often 10× harder and more expensive than 99% → 99.9%. The SLO should reflect the point where additional reliability has diminishing returns.
- **Dependency chain** — your SLO can't exceed the reliability of your critical dependencies. If your payment processor has a 99.9% SLO, your end-to-end checkout SLO can't safely be set above that.

**Q11. What is an error budget, and how is it calculated?**
A: An error budget is the allowed amount of unreliability within your SLO window — the "room to fail" before you breach your objective. It's calculated as:

`Error Budget = 1 − SLO target`

For a 99.9% availability SLO over 30 days:
- Error budget = 0.1%
- In time: 0.001 × 30 × 24 × 60 = **43.2 minutes** of allowed downtime per month.
- In requests: at 1,000 RPS, that's 0.001 × 1,000 × 30 × 86,400 = **2,592,000 allowed failures** per month.

The error budget is the key bridge between the SLO and your alerting strategy.

**Q12. What is an SLO window, and what are the trade-offs between rolling vs calendar windows?**
A: The SLO window is the time period over which compliance is measured.
- **Rolling window (e.g., trailing 30 days)** — continuously moves forward; old events drop off as time passes. Gives a stable, always-current view of health. Harder to communicate to customers ("you were at 99.91% over the last 30 days" vs "you were down on November 3rd").
- **Calendar window (e.g., calendar month)** — resets on the 1st of each month. Simpler for SLA billing and customer reporting, but creates perverse incentives (teams tolerate outages late in the month if the budget is nearly exhausted, knowing the counter resets soon).

Most engineering teams prefer rolling windows for operational alerting; calendar windows for customer-facing SLAs.

**Q13. Can an SLO be too tight? What happens?**
A: Yes — an SLO tighter than the system can reliably deliver causes constant breach, which leads to alert fatigue, burned-out on-call engineers, and eventually the team ignoring the alerts entirely. If your SLO fires every week but the team can't fix the underlying causes, the SLO is set wrong. A useful heuristic: if you're in SLO breach more than once per quarter on a mature service, either the SLO is unrealistic or there's a systemic reliability problem worth investing in.

**Q14. What does it mean to have an SLO that's "too loose"?**
A: A loose SLO means you're committing to reliability well below what you actually deliver — the error budget is never at risk, so it provides no useful signal. If your service achieves 99.97% availability but your SLO is 99%, you'll never alert, never burn budget, and the SLO tells you nothing actionable. The SLO should be tight enough to matter: set at a point where a real degradation would actually consume budget and eventually breach it without intervention.

## Section 4: Error Budgets

**Q15. What is the operationally important thing you can do with an error budget?**
A: Use it as a policy tool, not just a math number. The key insight: if you have budget remaining, you can move fast (deploy frequently, experiment, take calculated risks). If you're burning budget quickly, you slow down (freeze feature deployments, focus on reliability). This removes the subjective "are we stable enough to deploy?" debate — the error budget answers it objectively. Google's SRE model formalizes this: if the budget is exhausted, the team's focus automatically shifts from features to reliability work until it recovers.

**Q16. What is budget burn rate, and why is it a better alert signal than a raw threshold?**
A: Burn rate is how fast you're consuming your error budget relative to the rate that would exhaust it exactly by the end of the window. A burn rate of 1× means you're on track to use exactly 100% of your budget by the window end. A burn rate of 10× means you'd exhaust it in 1/10th of the window (e.g., 3 days instead of 30).

Why it's better than threshold alerts:
- A 5% error rate for 2 minutes might be acceptable noise; a 5% error rate sustained for 2 hours is catastrophic budget consumption.
- Burn rate alerts are proportional to the speed of budget depletion, so they naturally distinguish "blip" from "incident."
- They eliminate the need to tune static thresholds for every service.

**Q17. Explain multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting. Why use it?**
A: The Google SRE Workbook recommends alerting on two time windows simultaneously to balance detection speed against false positives:
- **Fast burn, short window** (e.g., 14.4× burn rate over 1 hour + 5 minutes) — catches severe, fast-moving incidents quickly. High precision; low recall on slow burns.
- **Slow burn, long window** (e.g., 1× burn rate over 3 days + 6 hours) — catches gradual degradation that a short window misses. Avoids alert fatigue on transient spikes.

The alert fires only when *both* conditions are true, which significantly reduces false positives. The logic: a high burn rate for 5 minutes but not 1 hour is probably a transient spike; a high burn rate for both 1 hour and 5 minutes is a real problem.

**Q18. What happens when you run out of error budget before the window ends?**
A: The team has a few options depending on your organization's SLO policy:
1. **Freeze feature deploys** — all engineering effort shifts to reliability until budget recovers.
2. **Escalate** — engage stakeholders to prioritize reliability work over roadmap.
3. **Revise the SLO** — if the budget is exhausted every month and the service is genuinely working as designed, the SLO may be miscalibrated.
4. **Accept and document** — for planned maintenance or known infrastructure events, pre-allocate budget and communicate it.

The key point: an exhausted error budget isn't just a number — it's a forcing function to have a real conversation about reliability investment.

## Section 5: Alerting Strategy

**Q19. What are the two main failure modes of alerting systems?**
A: 
- **Too noisy (alert fatigue)** — too many alerts fire, many of them not actionable. On-call engineers learn to ignore alerts, and when a real incident fires, it gets dismissed like the others. The danger: a genuine outage gets missed.
- **Too quiet (silent failures)** — the alerting system misses real incidents because thresholds are too loose, the wrong signals are monitored, or edge cases aren't covered. The danger: users are suffering and nobody knows.

Both failure modes are dangerous. The goal is alerts that are *actionable* — every alert that fires should require and justify a human response.

**Q20. What makes an alert "actionable"?**
A: An alert is actionable when:
- It is **urgent** — ignoring it would lead to user harm or SLO breach within a predictable time frame.
- It is **novel** — it requires human judgment to resolve; it can't be automatically self-healed.
- It is **diagnostic** — the alert title and body give the responder enough context to start investigating immediately.
- It has a **runbook** — there's documented guidance on what to check and how to respond.

If an alert fires and the on-call engineer's first thought is "this can wait until morning" or "I see this every week, it always resolves itself," it's not a good alert.

**Q21. What is the difference between a "symptom-based" and a "cause-based" alert? Which is better?**
A: 
- **Symptom-based** — alert on what the user experiences: "p99 latency > 1 second" or "success rate < 99.5%." These are SLI-derived alerts.
- **Cause-based** — alert on the internal reason: "DB connection pool at 95% capacity" or "disk at 80% full."

Symptom-based alerts are almost always better as primary alerts because they directly correlate to user impact and are cause-agnostic (they fire regardless of *why* the service is slow). Cause-based alerts are useful as *diagnostic aids* — they help explain the symptom — but as primary alerts they produce false positives (disk at 80% often doesn't immediately affect users) and false negatives (the service can be down for a reason not covered by any cause-based rule). Best practice: alert on symptoms, investigate causes.

**Q22. How do you handle flapping alerts (alerts that rapidly toggle on and off)?**
A: Flapping usually means the alert threshold is too close to normal operating variance, or the evaluation window is too short. Approaches:
- **Increase the evaluation window** — require the condition to hold for 5–10 minutes before firing, smoothing out transient spikes.
- **Add hysteresis** — set a higher threshold to trigger and a lower threshold to resolve (e.g., fire at 95%, resolve at 90%). This prevents rapid re-firing.
- **Re-examine the SLI** — if latency naturally oscillates across the threshold, the metric may be too noisy to alert on directly; consider alerting on burn rate instead.
- **Increase the bucket** — if you're alerting on a percentile computed over 1 minute, extend the window; percentiles over short windows are inherently noisy.

**Q23. What is alert routing, and why does it matter for on-call health?**
A: Alert routing determines who gets paged for which alert. Poor routing means the wrong team gets paged (slowing resolution, causing toil), or every engineer gets paged for everything (alert fatigue). Good routing practices:
- Route by **service ownership** — the team that owns the service gets paged for its SLO alerts.
- Route by **severity** — page the on-call engineer for critical/high severity; post to a Slack channel for warning/low severity.
- Route by **time of day** — use escalation policies so if on-call doesn't acknowledge within N minutes, the secondary is paged.
- Route **infrastructure alerts** to platform/SRE teams, not application developers.

## Section 6: SLA Design & Consequences

**Q24. How do you set an SLA given an SLO?**
A: The SLA is set *below* the SLO by a meaningful margin — typically the margin is sized to account for:
- **Measurement uncertainty** — SLIs are sampled; the SLA should account for measurement error.
- **Incident recovery time** — even after a fix is deployed, the rolling window takes time to recover.
- **Seasonal or tail risk** — the SLO is the median-case commitment; the SLA must hold even in bad months.

A common pattern: if your SLO is 99.9% over 30 days, your SLA commits to 99.5% — giving you a 0.4-percentage-point buffer (about 2.9 additional hours of allowed downtime per month). This buffer exists so a single bad incident doesn't simultaneously breach both your internal target and your contractual commitment.

**Q25. What are common SLA consequences, and how do they affect engineering behavior?**
A: Typical consequences include service credits (discounts on future billing, often 10–30% of monthly spend), priority support access, and in enterprise contracts, contract renegotiation rights. These consequences drive engineering behavior in both useful and counterproductive ways:
- **Useful** — SLA pressure forces reliability investment and prioritization.
- **Counterproductive** — teams may game measurement (e.g., exclude certain error types from the SLI), delay incident declarations to avoid the SLA clock starting, or cut corners on features to preserve uptime.

Good SLA design avoids measurement ambiguity and aligns incentives so the "game" is to actually be reliable, not to appear reliable.

**Q26. What is "planned maintenance" and how do SLAs typically handle it?**
A: Planned maintenance is scheduled downtime for upgrades, migrations, or patching — distinct from an unplanned outage. Most SLAs explicitly exclude pre-announced planned maintenance from availability calculations, provided it falls within agreed maintenance windows (e.g., "weekly 2–4 AM Sunday maintenance window"). This is why modern services aim to eliminate the need for maintenance windows through zero-downtime deployments — rolling updates, blue/green deploys, and database migrations that don't require locks. If your architecture requires regular planned downtime, you're implicitly accepting reliability constraints that tighter SLOs can't accommodate.

## Section 7: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q27. "A product manager asks you to set an SLO of 99.99% uptime for a new user-facing service launching next month. How do you respond?"**
A: I'd push back with data. 99.99% over 30 days is only 4.3 minutes of allowed downtime — that's extremely aggressive for a new service with no production history, no established reliability track record, and likely still undergoing frequent deploys. I'd ask: what's the business case for 99.99%? Is there a contractual requirement? What does the dependency stack look like — if we depend on a cloud provider with a 99.95% SLA, we can't structurally commit to 99.99%. I'd propose starting with 99.9% (43 minutes of budget), measuring actual performance for a quarter, and tightening it once we've proven we can hold it. An aspirational but unreachable SLO helps no one.

**Q28. "Your error budget is 50% consumed after only 10 days of a 30-day window. What do you do?"**
A: That's a 15× burn rate — at this pace, the budget is exhausted by day 20. Immediate actions:
1. Check if there's an ongoing incident or a recent deploy that introduced the degradation.
2. If it's a known ongoing issue, escalate and prioritize a fix above feature work.
3. Freeze non-critical deploys until the burn rate drops back to ≤1×.
4. Review the incident timeline — was there a single large event consuming budget, or steady degradation? A single event points to a specific failure; steady degradation points to a systemic issue.
5. Communicate to stakeholders: "we're on track to breach our SLO; here's our plan."
6. If the budget is already gone, the SLO is in breach — initiate a postmortem and begin the reliability investment conversation.

**Q29. "You're designing alerting for a payment processing service with a 99.95% SLO. What alerts would you set up, and why?"**
A: I'd implement multi-window burn rate alerting:
- **Page immediately (P1):** 14.4× burn rate over 1 hour (would exhaust monthly budget in ~2 days). This catches fast-moving severe incidents.
- **Page urgently (P2):** 6× burn rate over 6 hours (budget exhausted in ~5 days). Catches sustained medium-severity degradation.
- **Ticket / Slack (P3):** 3× burn rate over 3 days (budget exhausted in ~10 days). Catches slow degradation trends that need investigation but aren't emergencies.
- **Informational:** 1× burn rate over the full window — the SLO dashboard turns red; no page, just visibility.

Beyond burn rate, I'd add hard-threshold guards: any 5-minute window where error rate exceeds 10% (regardless of budget state) pages immediately, because fast catastrophic failures can briefly look "small" in a burn rate calculation.

**Q30. "A junior engineer says 'we should alert on p99 latency > 500ms.' What's your feedback?"**
A: Good instinct, needs refinement. A few things to address:
- **Static threshold vs budget consumption** — a single spike above 500ms will fire this constantly; burn rate alerting on a latency SLO is more robust.
- **Which p99?** — p99 of what? All endpoints? A specific critical path? The checkout endpoint has a very different latency profile and tolerance than an admin dashboard endpoint.
- **500ms — is that user-validated?** — where did that number come from? If the SLO says p99 < 300ms, 500ms is already in breach. If the SLO says p99 < 1000ms, a 500ms alert may be premature.
- **Short window noise** — p99 computed over 1 minute is very noisy; require the condition to persist for 5+ minutes, or better yet, alert on burn rate against a latency SLO.

The right response isn't to reject the alert but to have the SLO-first conversation: define the latency SLO first, then derive the alert from it.

## Framework Cheat Sheet

| Concept           | Definition                                   | Who Owns It         | Consequence of Breach       |
|---                |---                                            |---                  |---                          |
| SLI               | The measured signal (e.g., success rate)     | Engineering         | None — just a measurement   |
| SLO               | Internal target for the SLI                  | Engineering / SRE   | Error budget consumed; policy action (freeze deploys, reliability sprint) |
| SLA               | External contractual commitment               | Business / Legal    | Financial penalty, customer churn, reputational damage |
| Error Budget      | Allowed room to fail within SLO window       | Engineering / SRE   | Exhaustion triggers reliability-first mode |
| Burn Rate         | Speed of budget consumption vs sustainable rate | SRE / Alerting    | High burn rate triggers pages |

---

**Tip for the interview:** Senior interviewers at 5 YOE level don't want textbook definitions — they want to see that you've felt the tension between "we want to move fast" and "we can't break things." The SLI → SLO → SLA framework is most impressive when you tie it to real decisions: how it changed what you deployed and when, how it resolved an argument with a PM about whether something was "good enough," or how an error budget made a reliability investment conversation with leadership concrete instead of abstract. The framework's value is that it replaces gut-feel arguments with math.
