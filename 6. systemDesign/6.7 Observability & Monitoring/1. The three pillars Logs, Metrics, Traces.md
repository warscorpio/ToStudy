# Observability & Monitoring — The Three Pillars: Logs, Metrics, Traces — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is observability, and how is it different from monitoring?**
A: Monitoring is the practice of collecting and alerting on predefined signals — you know in advance what to watch (CPU > 80%, error rate > 1%) and set up dashboards and alerts around those known failure modes. Observability is a property of a system — the degree to which you can understand its internal state from its external outputs alone. An observable system lets you answer questions you didn't think to ask in advance: "why is this specific user's checkout slow?" or "which downstream call is causing this p99 spike?" Monitoring tells you *that* something is wrong; observability helps you figure out *why*. In practice: monitoring is a subset of what observability enables.

**Q2. What are the three pillars of observability?**
A:
- **Logs**: Discrete, timestamped records of events that occurred — what happened, when, and with what context. Best for understanding the exact sequence of events during an incident, debugging specific requests, and auditing.
- **Metrics**: Numeric measurements aggregated over time — counters, gauges, histograms. Best for alerting, trending, capacity planning, and SLO tracking. Cheap to store and query at scale.
- **Traces**: A record of the end-to-end journey of a single request through a distributed system — spans across services, timing breakdowns, parent-child relationships. Best for understanding latency sources, identifying which service or call in a chain is slow, and visualising distributed request flows.

Each pillar answers a different question: logs answer "what happened?", metrics answer "how much / how often?", traces answer "where did the time go?"

**Q3. Why aren't logs alone sufficient for observability in a microservices system?**
A: In a monolith, logs from a single process tell a coherent story. In microservices, a single user request touches 5–15 services, each writing logs independently with no shared context. You can't reconstruct the full request journey from logs alone without a correlation ID threaded through every service — and even then, correlating logs across services is slow, manual, and doesn't give you timing breakdowns. Metrics tell you something is wrong at the aggregate level but not where. Traces are the only pillar that natively represents the causal chain across services with timing — which is why distributed tracing is non-negotiable in a mature microservices stack.

**Q4. What is the difference between a metric and a log?**
A: A log is a raw event record — unstructured or structured text describing something that happened at a specific moment ("order 42 failed validation at 14:32:01.203, reason: card_declined"). A metric is a numeric measurement sampled or aggregated over time ("payment failure rate: 0.3% over the last 5 minutes"). Logs are high-cardinality and expensive to store and query at scale; metrics are low-cardinality aggregations that compress enormous amounts of behaviour into a handful of numbers. You alert on metrics (fast, cheap to evaluate); you investigate with logs (rich, slow to query).

**Q5. What is cardinality, and why does it matter for metrics and logs?**
A: Cardinality is the number of unique values a label or field can take. In metrics, high-cardinality labels (like `user_id`, `order_id`, `trace_id`) cause a combinatorial explosion of time series — a metric with 1 million unique user IDs creates 1 million separate time series, which is prohibitively expensive for time-series databases like Prometheus or Datadog. This is why metrics use low-cardinality labels (`endpoint`, `status_code`, `region`) and logs handle the high-cardinality detail (`user_id`, `request_id`). Traces sit in between — they're per-request but sampled, so cardinality is managed by sampling rate.

## Section 2: Logs

**Q6. What is structured logging, and why does it matter?**
A: Structured logging emits log entries as machine-parseable data (JSON, logfmt) rather than free-form strings. Each field is a named key-value pair:
```json
{
  "timestamp": "2024-01-15T14:32:01.203Z",
  "level": "ERROR",
  "service": "payment-service",
  "trace_id": "abc123",
  "order_id": "ord_789",
  "user_id": "usr_42",
  "event": "payment_failed",
  "reason": "card_declined",
  "duration_ms": 234
}
```
Structured logs are directly queryable by field without regex parsing — `level=ERROR AND service=payment-service AND reason=card_declined` works instantly in Elasticsearch or Loki. Unstructured logs require fragile regex extraction that breaks when the log format changes. At scale, structured logging is non-negotiable.

**Q7. What fields should every log entry contain?**
A: A baseline set for distributed systems:
- `timestamp` — ISO8601 with millisecond precision, always UTC.
- `level` — `DEBUG`, `INFO`, `WARN`, `ERROR`, `FATAL`.
- `service` — name of the emitting service.
- `trace_id` / `span_id` — for correlating with traces (essential in microservices).
- `correlation_id` / `request_id` — for grouping all logs from a single user request.
- `event` or `message` — what happened, as a short, consistent string (not `"Error processing request"` but `"payment_failed"`).
- `duration_ms` — for any operation with meaningful latency.
- `error` / `stack_trace` — on error logs only.
Domain-specific fields (like `order_id`, `user_id`, `payment_id`) are added per log line as needed. Never log PII (card numbers, passwords, raw PAN data) — mask or omit at the logging layer.

**Q8. What are log levels, and how do you use them in production?**
A: Log levels control verbosity and signal severity:
- `DEBUG`: Fine-grained diagnostic data — variable values, branch decisions, internal state. **Disabled in production** by default; enable dynamically per service when debugging.
- `INFO`: Normal operational events — request received, payment processed, job started. The production baseline.
- `WARN`: Unexpected but handled situations — retry attempted, fallback triggered, rate limit approaching. Investigate if sustained.
- `ERROR`: A failure that affected a specific operation but the service is still running — payment failed, DB query timed out. Should alert if sustained above baseline.
- `FATAL`: Unrecoverable — service is about to crash. Always alerts.

In production: INFO and above by default. Dynamic log level adjustment (without restart) is a key operational capability — implemented via a config flag or a runtime API endpoint.

**Q9. What is log sampling, and when should you use it?**
A: Log sampling emits only a fraction of log entries for high-volume, low-signal events — for example, logging 1% of successful health check requests instead of 100%. Without sampling, high-throughput services (10,000 req/sec) generate enormous log volumes that are expensive to store, index, and query, and where successful requests add almost no diagnostic value. Rules for safe sampling:
- **Never sample errors or warnings** — every failure event is potentially diagnostic.
- **Sample predictably** — use consistent hash sampling on `request_id` so all logs from a given request are either all sampled or all dropped (not randomly half-present).
- **Log the sampling rate** in the retained entries so query results can be extrapolated.
- **Don't sample traces** at the log level — let the tracing system handle sampling independently.

**Q10. What is a log aggregation pipeline, and what are the common components?**
A: In a distributed system, logs from dozens of services across hundreds of nodes must be collected, enriched, and centralised for querying. A typical pipeline:
1. **Emitter**: Application writes structured JSON to stdout (in containerised environments, always log to stdout — never to files).
2. **Collector/shipper**: A sidecar or DaemonSet agent (Fluent Bit, Filebeat, Vector) tails stdout, buffers, and forwards logs.
3. **Aggregator** (optional): A central aggregation layer (Logstash, Vector) that enriches, filters, and routes logs before indexing.
4. **Storage/indexing**: Elasticsearch (ELK stack), OpenSearch, Loki (log lines with label-based indexing, cheaper), or Cloudwatch Logs.
5. **Query/visualisation**: Kibana (for Elastic), Grafana (for Loki), Cloudwatch Insights.

Key operational concern: back-pressure. If the storage layer is slow, the shipper must buffer or drop logs — configure buffer size and drop policies explicitly rather than letting the agent crash the application.

## Section 3: Metrics

**Q11. What are the four core metric types?**
A:
- **Counter**: A monotonically increasing number that only goes up (or resets on restart). Used for counting events: `http_requests_total`, `payment_failures_total`. You derive rates from counters: `rate(http_requests_total[5m])`.
- **Gauge**: A value that can go up or down, representing a snapshot of a current state: `active_connections`, `queue_depth`, `memory_used_bytes`. You read gauges directly — no rate calculation.
- **Histogram**: Samples observations and counts them in configurable buckets, also tracking sum and count. Used for latency and request size distributions: `http_request_duration_seconds{le="0.1"}` — the number of requests that completed in under 100ms. Allows percentile calculation (p50, p95, p99) server-side via `histogram_quantile()`.
- **Summary**: Similar to histogram but calculates configurable quantiles on the client side. Less flexible for aggregation across instances; histograms are preferred in most modern stacks.

**Q12. What is the RED method, and what does it measure?**
A: RED is a framework for service-level metrics, defined by Tom Wilkie:
- **Rate**: How many requests per second is the service handling? (`rate(http_requests_total[1m])`)
- **Errors**: What fraction of those requests are failing? (`rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5.."}[1m]) / rate(http_requests_total[1m])`)
- **Duration**: How long are requests taking, at what percentiles? (`histogram_quantile(0.99, http_request_duration_seconds)`)

RED metrics are the minimum viable monitoring set for any service. If you instrument nothing else, instrument these three. They answer the key question at all times: "is this service healthy right now?"

**Q13. What is the USE method, and how does it differ from RED?**
A: USE (Brendan Gregg) applies to infrastructure and resource health, not service-level behaviour:
- **Utilisation**: What percentage of the resource's capacity is being used? (CPU: 72%, disk: 45%)
- **Saturation**: Is the resource being asked for more than it can handle? (CPU run queue length, disk I/O wait)
- **Errors**: Is the resource experiencing errors? (disk read errors, network packet drops)

RED answers "is my service healthy?" from the user's perspective. USE answers "is my infrastructure healthy?" from the resource's perspective. Use RED for every service; use USE for every host, database, queue, and network device. They're complementary, not competing.

**Q14. What are SLIs, SLOs, and SLAs, and how do they relate to metrics?**
A:
- **SLI (Service Level Indicator)**: A specific metric that measures one aspect of service reliability — the raw measurement. Example: `availability = successful_requests / total_requests`.
- **SLO (Service Level Objective)**: A target value for an SLI — the goal you're trying to hit. Example: "availability SLI ≥ 99.9% over a rolling 30-day window."
- **SLA (Service Level Agreement)**: A contractual commitment to a customer with defined consequences for breach. SLAs are usually less ambitious than internal SLOs — you build SLOs with enough headroom that breaching an SLO is a warning before you breach an SLA.

SLIs are implemented as metrics and metric queries. SLOs are tracked via error budgets — the acceptable amount of unreliability in a time window. Metrics drive SLO dashboards and error budget burn rate alerts.

**Q15. What is an error budget, and how do you use it operationally?**
A: An error budget is the maximum amount of downtime or failure a service is allowed while still meeting its SLO. If SLO is 99.9% availability over 30 days, the error budget is 0.1% of 30 days = 43.2 minutes of allowed downtime per month. The error budget is consumed by incidents, failed deployments, and any event that causes user-facing failures. Key operational uses:
- **Deployment gate**: If error budget is nearly exhausted, freeze non-essential deployments until it replenishes.
- **Reliability investment signal**: If the error budget is consistently exhausted, the team should invest in reliability over new features. If it's never touched, the SLO may be too lenient.
- **Chaos engineering permission**: Some teams use remaining error budget to authorize chaos experiments — "we have 30 minutes of budget left, let's run a controlled experiment."

**Q16. How does Prometheus scrape and store metrics, and what are its scaling limits?**
A: Prometheus uses a pull model — it scrapes HTTP `/metrics` endpoints exposed by services at a configured interval (typically 15–60 seconds). Metrics are stored as a local time-series database (TSDB) with efficient compression. Scaling limits: Prometheus is a single-node system by design — it doesn't shard or replicate natively. At high scale (millions of time series, many months of retention), teams use:
- **Thanos or Cortex**: Adds long-term storage (S3), global query view across Prometheus instances, and deduplication.
- **Prometheus federation**: A top-level Prometheus scrapes aggregated metrics from lower-level instances.
- **Managed alternatives**: Datadog, Grafana Cloud, AWS Managed Prometheus — offload storage and scaling concerns to a vendor.

## Section 4: Traces

**Q17. What is distributed tracing, and what problem does it solve?**
A: Distributed tracing tracks the end-to-end journey of a single request as it propagates across multiple services, recording the timing and causal relationship of each operation. Without tracing, diagnosing latency in a microservices call chain is guesswork — you know from metrics that p99 is high, but not which of the 8 services in the chain is responsible. A trace answers: "this request took 2.3 seconds — 1.8 seconds was spent in the inventory service waiting for a DB query on an unindexed column." That's the signal that drives the fix.

**Q18. What is a span, and how do spans compose into a trace?**
A: A span represents a single unit of work — a service call, a DB query, a cache lookup — with a name, start time, duration, status, and key-value attributes. Spans have parent-child relationships: the root span represents the incoming request; child spans represent downstream calls made during that request. Together, all spans sharing the same `trace_id` form the trace — a directed acyclic graph (usually a tree) of operations. The visual representation is a waterfall (Gantt chart) showing each span's timing relative to the trace start, making it immediately obvious which span caused the overall latency.

**Q19. How is trace context propagated across service boundaries?**
A: Via HTTP headers (for synchronous calls) or message metadata (for async Kafka messages). The W3C TraceContext standard defines two headers:
- `traceparent`: Contains `version-trace_id-parent_span_id-flags` — the trace ID ties all spans together; the parent span ID establishes the parent-child relationship.
- `tracestate`: Vendor-specific trace state (e.g., sampling decision).

When Service A calls Service B, it injects these headers into the outgoing HTTP request. Service B extracts them, creates a child span with Service A's span as the parent, and propagates them to any further downstream calls. For Kafka, the same values are stored in message headers. OpenTelemetry SDKs handle injection and extraction automatically once configured.

**Q20. What is sampling in distributed tracing, and what are the common strategies?**
A: Recording every trace in a high-traffic system is prohibitively expensive — at 10,000 req/sec, storing full traces for every request would require enormous storage and processing capacity. Sampling reduces the volume:
- **Head-based sampling**: The sampling decision is made at the root span (the entry point) before the request is processed. Simple, low overhead — but may drop interesting traces (a rare error that happens to fall in the unsampled fraction). Common rates: 1–10% of traffic.
- **Tail-based sampling**: The sampling decision is made *after* the trace is complete — collect all spans, and then decide which traces to keep based on their outcome (always keep errors, slow traces, or traces from new deployments; sample the rest). More accurate but requires buffering all spans until the trace is complete — complex to implement (Jaeger, OpenTelemetry Collector support it).
- **Rate-limited sampling**: Keep N traces per second regardless of overall traffic volume — useful for ensuring representation of low-traffic services.
- **Always-on for errors**: Many teams combine 1% head sampling with "always keep if any span has an error" — the common pattern for catching rare failures without storing everything.

**Q21. What is OpenTelemetry, and why has it become the standard?**
A: OpenTelemetry (OTel) is a CNCF project that provides vendor-neutral SDKs, APIs, and a collector for generating and exporting telemetry data (traces, metrics, logs). Before OTel, each observability vendor (Datadog, Jaeger, Zipkin, New Relic) had its own instrumentation SDK — switching vendors required re-instrumenting every service. OTel decouples instrumentation from the backend: instrument once with the OTel SDK, export to any backend by changing the exporter config. The Collector acts as a local agent that receives telemetry from services and routes it to one or more backends — Jaeger for traces, Prometheus for metrics, Loki for logs — simultaneously, from a single instrumentation layer.

**Q22. What is auto-instrumentation vs manual instrumentation in tracing?**
A: Auto-instrumentation uses agent-based or bytecode injection (Java agent, Node.js `--require` hook) to automatically create spans for known frameworks and libraries — HTTP calls, DB queries, Redis operations, Kafka producers/consumers — without any code change. It gives you 80% of the value immediately. Manual instrumentation adds custom spans and attributes to your business logic:
```javascript
const span = tracer.startSpan('validate_payment_rules');
span.setAttribute('payment_id', paymentId);
span.setAttribute('rule_count', rules.length);
try {
  const result = await validateRules(payment, rules);
  span.setStatus({ code: SpanStatusCode.OK });
  return result;
} catch (err) {
  span.recordException(err);
  span.setStatus({ code: SpanStatusCode.ERROR });
  throw err;
} finally {
  span.end();
}
```
Start with auto-instrumentation for infrastructure calls; add manual spans for business-critical operations (payment validation, BRE rule evaluation, fraud scoring) where you need custom attributes and business context in the trace.

## Section 5: Connecting the Three Pillars

**Q23. How do you correlate logs, metrics, and traces in practice?**
A: The key is shared identifiers — specifically `trace_id` and `span_id` embedded in every log line and linked from every metric exemplar:
- **Logs → Traces**: Every structured log line includes `trace_id` and `span_id`. In Grafana, clicking on a log line jumps to the associated trace in Jaeger/Tempo. You go from "this ERROR log at 14:32:01" to "the full distributed trace showing every service call that produced this error."
- **Metrics → Traces**: Prometheus histograms support exemplars — individual samples attached to a metric data point, carrying a `trace_id`. When you see a p99 spike on a dashboard, you click the spike and see exemplars — traces from that exact time window that represent the slow requests. You go from "p99 spiked to 3s at 14:32" to "here are 3 actual traces from that spike."
- **Traces → Logs**: From a trace, click a span and jump to all logs emitted during that span's duration from that service. Grafana + Loki + Tempo implement this natively via derived fields.

This three-way linkage — sometimes called the "observability correlation" pattern — transforms observability from three separate tools into a single investigation workflow.

**Q24. What is the difference between the "known unknowns" that monitoring covers and the "unknown unknowns" that observability covers?**
A: Monitoring handles known unknowns — you know CPU can spike, so you alert on it; you know error rate can rise, so you track it. These are anticipated failure modes. Observability handles unknown unknowns — failure modes you didn't anticipate and therefore didn't build alerts for. A novel failure (a specific combination of user data that triggers a race condition in a new code path, affecting 0.01% of users) won't trigger any existing alert — but with high-cardinality tracing and structured logs, you can ask arbitrary questions ("show me all traces where the BRE evaluation step took > 500ms for NACH payments above ₹50,000") and discover the pattern. Monitoring is reactive to known risks; observability enables exploration of unknown failure modes.

**Q25. What is an observability-driven development practice?**
A: Instrumenting code *before* deploying it — treating telemetry as a first-class deliverable alongside the feature itself. Concretely: every new service or feature ships with RED metrics, structured logging with a `trace_id`, and custom spans for business-critical paths. The PR isn't complete without a dashboard showing the feature's SLI metrics. This is the equivalent of TDD for observability — don't ship code you can't observe in production. Teams that do this catch production anomalies in minutes; teams that add observability after the fact debug production issues for hours.

## Section 6: Tools & Infrastructure

**Q26. What is the ELK/EFK stack, and when do you use it?**
A: ELK = Elasticsearch + Logstash + Kibana. EFK replaces Logstash with Fluent Bit/Fluentd (lighter-weight, better for Kubernetes). Use cases: full-text log search, complex log analysis, high-cardinality log field queries. Strengths: powerful query DSL, rich aggregations, battle-tested at scale. Weaknesses: expensive to operate at large log volumes (Elasticsearch storage and indexing cost), complex cluster management, cardinality limits on indexed fields. Alternative for lower cost: Grafana Loki, which indexes only labels (low cardinality) and stores log lines as compressed chunks — much cheaper, but query performance degrades on high-volume full-text search.

**Q27. What is the Prometheus + Grafana stack, and how does alerting work?**
A: Prometheus scrapes and stores metrics; Grafana queries and visualises them. Alerting: Prometheus evaluates alert rules (PromQL expressions with `for` duration) on every scrape cycle; when a rule fires, it sends the alert to Alertmanager. Alertmanager handles deduplication, grouping, silencing, and routing — sending notifications to PagerDuty, Slack, email, or webhooks. Key concepts:
- **Alert rule**: `ALERT PaymentErrorRateHigh IF rate(payment_errors_total[5m]) / rate(payment_requests_total[5m]) > 0.01 FOR 2m` — fires if error rate exceeds 1% for 2 consecutive minutes.
- **`for` clause**: Prevents flapping — the condition must hold for the duration before the alert fires.
- **Alertmanager routing**: Different alert severity routes to different channels — `critical` wakes on-call via PagerDuty; `warning` posts to Slack.

**Q28. What is Jaeger or Tempo, and how do they fit into the tracing stack?**
A: Jaeger (CNCF) and Grafana Tempo are distributed trace storage and query backends. Services instrument with OpenTelemetry SDKs and export spans to the OTel Collector; the Collector forwards to Jaeger or Tempo for storage and querying. Jaeger provides a UI for searching traces by service, operation, tags, and duration, and visualising the waterfall. Tempo is designed to be storage-efficient and integrates natively with Grafana — you query traces by `trace_id` (usually arrived at via a log or metric link) rather than by tag search, which dramatically reduces storage cost (no inverted index on span attributes). For most teams: Tempo + Grafana if you're already in the Grafana ecosystem; Jaeger if you need tag-based trace search without a Grafana dependency.

**Q29. What is the OpenTelemetry Collector, and why run it as a sidecar or DaemonSet?**
A: The OTel Collector is a vendor-agnostic proxy that receives telemetry data from services (via OTLP, Jaeger, Zipkin, Prometheus protocols), processes it (batching, filtering, attribute enrichment, tail sampling), and exports it to one or more backends. Running it as a sidecar (one per pod) or DaemonSet (one per node) keeps telemetry export off the critical path — the service writes to a local Collector over loopback (fast, reliable), and the Collector handles retries, batching, and backend connectivity independently. If the backend is temporarily unavailable, the Collector buffers and retries without affecting the service. It also allows backend changes (swap Jaeger for Tempo) with zero service code changes — just update the Collector config.

## Section 7: Scenario / System Design Questions

**Q30. "Your payment service p99 latency spiked from 200ms to 2.3s for 8 minutes at 3pm. Walk through how you'd investigate this with all three pillars."**
A: A structured investigation using each pillar:
1. **Metrics first** (what happened and when): Confirm the spike on the p99 latency histogram — pinpoint start/end time. Check RED metrics: did error rate rise alongside latency? Check USE on the payment service's DB: did connection pool saturation or DB CPU spike at the same time? Check downstream dependency metrics: did the JusPay/Razorpay gateway latency increase? Narrow down: is this the payment service itself, its DB, or a downstream?
2. **Traces next** (where did the time go): Use metric exemplars to pull traces from the spike window. Open 3–5 slow traces from that period. The waterfall shows which span consumed the 2.3 seconds. If `db_query: SELECT * FROM payments WHERE user_id = ?` shows 2.1 seconds in every slow trace, the bottleneck is that query — missing index, lock contention, or table scan.
3. **Logs to confirm** (what exactly happened): Filter logs from 3pm–3:08pm on the payment service with `level=WARN OR ERROR`. Look for: slow query warnings, connection pool exhaustion messages, retry events, lock timeout errors. Correlate by `trace_id` — find the slow trace IDs from step 2 in the logs to get the full event narrative including any error messages not surfaced in the trace attributes.
4. **Hypothesis and fix**: DB query missing index on `user_id`. Temporary fix: add index. Root cause investigation: why did this only start at 3pm? Check deployment events and DB `pg_stat_activity` for long-running transactions that held locks.

**Q31. "Design the observability stack for a NestJS microservices payment platform with Kafka, Postgres, and Redis."**
A: Layer by layer:

**Metrics (Prometheus + Grafana)**:
- Instrument every NestJS service with `prom-client` — expose RED metrics per endpoint and per Kafka consumer group.
- Custom business metrics: `payments_processed_total{status, gateway}`, `nach_debit_attempts_total{result}`, `bre_rule_evaluations_total{rule, outcome}`.
- DB metrics via `pg_exporter` (pool saturation, query duration, replication lag), Redis metrics via `redis_exporter`, Kafka metrics via JMX exporter or Kafka's native Prometheus endpoint.
- Alert rules: payment error rate > 0.5% for 2 min → PagerDuty; Kafka consumer lag > 10,000 messages for 5 min → Slack.

**Logs (Fluent Bit + Loki + Grafana)**:
- All services log structured JSON to stdout. Fluent Bit DaemonSet collects and ships to Loki.
- Mandatory fields on every log line: `trace_id`, `span_id`, `service`, `correlation_id`, `event`, `duration_ms`.
- Never log PAN data, card numbers, or Aadhaar numbers — mask at the NestJS logger middleware level.
- Log sampling: 100% for WARN/ERROR; 10% for INFO on high-volume health check endpoints.

**Traces (OpenTelemetry + Tempo + Grafana)**:
- Auto-instrument NestJS HTTP, `pg`, `ioredis`, `kafkajs` via `@opentelemetry/auto-instrumentations-node`.
- Manual spans for BRE rule evaluation, NACH debit orchestration, and Account Aggregator calls — these are the business-critical paths where custom attributes (`payment_id`, `rule_id`, `aggregator_response_code`) are needed.
- OTel Collector as a DaemonSet — services export OTLP to local Collector; Collector batches and sends to Tempo.
- Tail-based sampling: keep 100% of traces with errors or duration > 1 second; 1% of successful fast traces.

**Correlation**: Grafana as the single pane — Loki for logs, Tempo for traces, Prometheus for metrics. Derived fields in Loki: `trace_id` in log lines links to Tempo traces. Exemplars in Prometheus histograms link to slow traces. One click from a metric spike to the exact trace, one click from the trace to the logs.

**Q32. "An on-call engineer gets a PagerDuty alert: 'NACH debit failure rate > 5%'. What observability data do they check first, and in what order?"**
A: A runbook-style investigation sequence:
1. **Metrics dashboard** (30 seconds): Open the NACH payments Grafana dashboard. Confirm the failure rate and check when it started. Check the breakdown by `failure_reason` label — is it `gateway_timeout`, `insufficient_funds`, `invalid_account`, or something new? Check if it's a specific bank or all banks. Narrow the blast radius immediately.
2. **Downstream dependency metrics** (1 minute): If `gateway_timeout` is the top failure reason, check the payment gateway (NACH sponsor bank / NPCI) latency metrics. Is their p99 elevated? Check circuit breaker state metrics — is the circuit open?
3. **Traces** (2 minutes): Pull traces from the last 5 minutes filtered to `status=error` on the NACH consumer service. Open 3 failed traces. The waterfall shows exactly where each failure occurred — is it timing out at the gateway call span, failing at the DB write span, or erroring at the BRE evaluation span?
4. **Logs** (3 minutes): From the failed traces, jump to logs via `trace_id`. Look for error messages, stack traces, or retry exhaustion logs that give the specific error from the gateway or DB. This is where you find the exact error code from NPCI or the specific DB constraint that's failing.
5. **Decision**: With the failure reason, affected scope, and root cause identified from steps 1–4, the engineer decides: is this a recoverable blip (circuit breaker will re-close), a config fix (wrong routing number), or an escalation (NPCI system outage)?

**Q33. "How would you add observability to a new BRE rule evaluation feature without slowing down the team?"**
A: Observability as part of the definition of done — not a separate task added later:
1. **Metrics (30 min)**: Add a `bre_rule_evaluations_total` counter with labels `{rule_id, outcome: pass|fail|error}` and a `bre_evaluation_duration_ms` histogram. Register with the existing Prometheus client — two metric definitions, no infrastructure change.
2. **Structured logs (already done if standards are in place)**: Ensure the BRE evaluation logs include `rule_id`, `payment_id`, `outcome`, `duration_ms`, and `trace_id`. If the service already uses structured logging middleware, this is just adding fields to existing log calls.
3. **Custom span (1 hour)**: Wrap the BRE evaluation in a manual OTel span with `rule_id`, `rule_version`, and `outcome` as span attributes. Auto-instrumentation already covers the DB queries inside the evaluation — the manual span adds business context.
4. **Dashboard panel (30 min)**: Add a panel to the existing payments dashboard — BRE evaluation rate, error rate, and p99 duration. No new dashboard, no new infrastructure.
5. **Alert (15 min)**: Add one alert rule — BRE evaluation error rate > 1% for 2 minutes → Slack warning. Escalate to PagerDuty if > 5%.

Total: ~2–3 hours of instrumentation work, done by the engineer writing the feature. The result: on day one in production, you know how many rules are being evaluated per second, what percentage are failing, and you can find any problematic evaluation in Grafana within 30 seconds by filtering traces on `rule_id`.

| Metrics | How much? How often? Trends    | Low         | Low          | Fast        | Prometheus, Datadog, CloudWatch  |
| Traces  | Where did the time go?         | Medium (sampled) | Medium  | Medium      | Tempo, Jaeger, Datadog APM       |

| Method | Applies To      | Measures                              |
|--------|-----------------|---------------------------------------|
| RED    | Services        | Rate, Errors, Duration (user-facing)  |
| USE    | Infrastructure  | Utilisation, Saturation, Errors       |

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level, interviewers want to see that you can connect observability to business outcomes — not just name the three pillars. The strongest answers describe a real investigation workflow: metric spike → trace exemplar → specific span → correlated log line → root cause. Be ready to talk about cardinality as the reason you can't put `user_id` in a Prometheus label, why tail-based sampling matters for catching rare errors, and how OpenTelemetry lets you instrument once and change backends without touching service code. In a fintech context, name the specific metrics you'd track (`payment_success_rate`, `nach_debit_failure_reason`, `bre_evaluation_duration`) — that specificity signals you've actually built this, not just read about it.
