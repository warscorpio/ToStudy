# Error Budgets — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is an error budget?**
A: An error budget is the maximum allowable amount of unreliability a service is permitted to have over a defined time window, derived directly from its SLO. If your SLO is 99.9% availability over 30 days, your error budget is 0.1% of that window — approximately 43.8 minutes of allowed downtime. It's not a punishment ceiling; it's a deliberate allocation of acceptable risk that lets engineering teams move fast without violating their reliability commitments.

**Q2. What is the relationship between SLIs, SLOs, and error budgets?**
A:
- **SLI (Service Level Indicator)** — the actual measured metric: request success rate, latency at p99, availability. The raw signal.
- **SLO (Service Level Objective)** — the target threshold for that metric over a time window: "99.9% of requests succeed over a rolling 30-day window." The commitment.
- **Error budget** — `1 - SLO target`, expressed as the permitted failure budget: the amount of time/requests that can fail before the SLO is breached. The budget is what error budget policies operate against.

SLA (Service Level Agreement) is a contractual, customer-facing version of an SLO — breach of an SLA typically triggers financial penalties. Error budgets are an internal engineering tool derived from SLOs, not SLAs.

**Q3. How do you calculate an error budget for a given time window?**
A: `Error budget = (1 - SLO) × window duration`

Examples for a 30-day window:
| SLO     | Error budget (minutes) | Error budget (seconds) |
|---      |---                     |---                     |
| 99%     | 432 min                | 25,920 s               |
| 99.9%   | 43.8 min               | 2,592 s                |
| 99.95%  | 21.9 min               | 1,296 s                |
| 99.99%  | 4.38 min               | 259 s                  |

For request-based SLOs (e.g., success rate), budget is in requests: at 1,000 RPS with a 99.9% SLO over 30 days, total requests ≈ 2.59B, error budget ≈ 2.59M allowed failures.

**Q4. What is the purpose of an error budget beyond just tracking downtime?**
A: It reframes the reliability vs. velocity tension into a shared resource that both product and engineering jointly consume. Key purposes:
- **Governance**: gives engineering a principled basis to slow down or pause feature releases when reliability is degrading ("we've consumed 80% of our budget with 15 days left").
- **Risk alignment**: leadership can't demand both "ship faster" and "zero incidents" simultaneously — the budget makes the trade-off explicit and quantified.
- **Incentive correction**: without error budgets, SRE/ops teams are incentivized to be maximally conservative (no deploys = no incidents); error budgets let them *use* remaining budget for controlled risk-taking.
- **Post-incident prioritization**: budget burn rate is concrete evidence for funding reliability work over feature work.

**Q5. What happens when an error budget is exhausted?**
A: The error budget policy kicks in — a pre-agreed set of actions teams take when the budget hits zero or a defined threshold. Typical consequences:
- Feature releases are frozen; only bug fixes and reliability improvements ship.
- The team shifts engineering focus to reliability work until the budget recovers.
- A postmortem is mandatory for the incidents that burned the budget.
- In extreme cases, rollback of recent changes.

The key word is *pre-agreed* — the policy should be documented and signed off by product and engineering leadership before an incident happens, not negotiated in the heat of an outage.

## Section 2: SLOs and Measurement

**Q6. How do you choose the right SLO target?**
A: Start with what users actually notice, not what sounds impressive. A common approach:
1. **Measure current baseline** — what is your actual reliability today? Setting an SLO far below current performance is meaningless; setting it above creates immediate budget exhaustion.
2. **User research / support data** — where does user-visible degradation start? For most web apps, users notice latency above ~1s; errors above ~1% generate support tickets.
3. **Business context** — a payments API warrants a tighter SLO (99.99%) than an internal analytics dashboard (99.5%).
4. **Competitor benchmarks** — don't set aspirational targets you can't sustain; don't under-commit either.

Avoid the temptation to set 100% SLOs — they're unachievable (planned maintenance alone prevents it), leave no error budget, and eliminate any room for risk-taking.

**Q7. What are the most common SLI types?**
A:
- **Availability** — ratio of successful requests to total requests. Most common. "Successful" must be defined (is a 500 a failure? a 429? a timeout?).
- **Latency** — ratio of requests that complete within a threshold: "99% of requests complete in < 200ms." Often measured as a percentile (p99, p95) rather than mean, since mean hides tail latency.
- **Error rate** — percentage of requests returning errors. Often the inverse of availability SLI.
- **Throughput** — whether the system processes at least N events/sec. Relevant for pipelines and queues.
- **Freshness** — how recently data was updated. Relevant for caches, ETL pipelines, data products.
- **Durability** — probability of data loss. Relevant for storage systems.

**Q8. What is a rolling window vs. a calendar window for SLOs, and which is better?**
A:
- **Calendar window** (monthly, quarterly): SLO resets at the start of each period. Simple to explain, maps to billing/SLA cycles. Downside: an incident on day 1 of a month vs. day 28 has wildly different consequences (day 1 leaves time to recover; day 28 is immediately critical).
- **Rolling window** (trailing 28 or 30 days): SLO measured over the last N days at any point in time. More operationally stable — the budget doesn't suddenly reset; old incidents naturally "age out" as they fall outside the window. Better for day-to-day operational decisions.

Rolling windows are generally preferred for operational SLOs; calendar windows are often used for contractual SLAs.

**Q9. Should every endpoint/operation share one SLO, or should you have separate SLOs?**
A: Separate SLOs per critical user journey, not per endpoint. Grouping all endpoints under one SLO means a slow internal admin endpoint drags down the SLO for your critical checkout path — or vice versa. Define SLOs around user-facing operations that matter: "checkout success rate", "search latency", "authentication availability". Internal endpoints that don't affect user experience can have looser SLOs or none at all. Start with 3–5 meaningful SLOs rather than dozens; too many SLOs are as useless as none.

## Section 3: Burn Rate and Alerting

**Q10. What is error budget burn rate?**
A: Burn rate measures how fast you're consuming your error budget relative to the rate that would exactly exhaust it by the end of the window. A burn rate of 1x means you're on track to use exactly 100% of the budget by the window end. A burn rate of 2x means you'll exhaust it in half the window. A burn rate of 10x means your budget is gone in 10% of the window.

Formula: `burn rate = (current error rate) / (1 - SLO)`

Example: SLO is 99.9% (error budget = 0.1%). Current error rate = 1%. Burn rate = 1% / 0.1% = 10x. At this rate, you exhaust a 30-day budget in 3 days.

**Q11. Why alert on burn rate instead of raw error rate?**
A: Raw error rate alerts cause two problems:
- **Too sensitive**: a brief spike to 5% errors for 30 seconds is operationally irrelevant if it burns almost no budget. A raw error rate alert would page oncall unnecessarily.
- **Too slow**: a steady 0.2% error rate (above the 0.1% budget rate) will exhaust your budget in 15 days — but a threshold alert on "error rate > 1%" would never fire.

Burn rate alerts catch both cases: they fire when the rate of budget consumption is significant *and* sustained, filtering noise while catching slow burns.

**Q12. What is a multiwindow, multi-burn-rate alerting strategy?**
A: The Google SRE Workbook recommends alerting on two burn rates across two time windows simultaneously. The pattern:
- **Fast burn, short window** (e.g., 14.4x burn over 1 hour) — catches severe, acute incidents. High urgency, page immediately. 1-hour window ensures the alert fires quickly; 14.4x rate = budget exhausted in 2 hours.
- **Slow burn, longer window** (e.g., 3x burn over 6 hours) — catches sustained moderate degradation that a short window would miss. Less urgent, but still requires response before the budget is gone.

Both conditions must be evaluated against their respective windows to avoid false positives from transient spikes. Combining fast and slow burn rate alerts gives both low false-positive rates and fast detection.

**Q13. What is "budget burn" alerting vs. "threshold" alerting, and what's the operational difference?**
A: Threshold alerting fires when a metric crosses a static value (`error_rate > 1%`). Budget burn alerting fires when the *rate of reliability degradation* threatens your SLO commitment. The operational difference: threshold alerts are metric-centric and require per-service tuning; burn rate alerts are automatically calibrated to your SLO — tighter SLOs naturally yield more sensitive alerts without reconfiguring thresholds. They also give oncall context: "you're burning budget at 6x" is more actionable than "error rate is 0.6%."

## Section 4: Error Budget Policies

**Q14. What is an error budget policy, and what should it include?**
A: A written, pre-agreed document that defines what engineering and product teams will do at different levels of budget consumption. A complete policy covers:
- **Thresholds** — at what % consumed does each action trigger (e.g., 50%, 75%, 100%).
- **Actions at each threshold** — what changes (release freeze, reliability sprint, escalation).
- **Who owns enforcement** — typically SRE/platform team, but product lead must co-sign.
- **Recovery conditions** — what does "budget restored" look like and when does normal velocity resume.
- **Exceptions process** — can leadership override the freeze, and under what conditions (revenue-critical release with a mitigation plan).

**Q15. Should the error budget policy be enforced automatically or through human process?**
A: Ideally both, applied to different decisions. Automated enforcement (CI/CD gate that blocks deploys when budget is below a threshold) is effective for well-understood, repeatable decisions. Human process is necessary for exceptions, nuanced risk assessments, and cases where the budget was consumed by an external factor outside engineering's control (cloud provider outage, dependency failure). Pure human process is too slow and gets bypassed under pressure; pure automation lacks context. The policy document should clearly separate which decisions are automated vs. human-gated.

**Q16. What do you do when the error budget is exhausted by an external factor (cloud provider outage)?**
A: This is a legitimate question — budget burned by a dependency failure you couldn't control is different from budget burned by your own poor release practices. Common approaches:
- **Exclude from budget**: if the event is clearly attributable to a declared cloud provider incident (they publish incident reports), some teams exclude that window from SLO calculation. This requires transparency and shouldn't be used to game numbers.
- **Treat it the same**: more conservative teams count it regardless — user impact is real even if the cause is external. This motivates investing in multi-region redundancy and graceful degradation.
- **Separate SLO for infrastructure-caused failures**: track infra-caused vs. own-code-caused budget consumption separately; policy triggers on own-code consumption only.

The important thing is deciding the policy *before* an incident, not retroactively.

## Section 5: Organizational Dynamics

**Q17. How do error budgets change the relationship between product and SRE/engineering teams?**
A: Error budgets transform reliability from a technical concern into a shared business resource. Product teams want to ship features fast (burns budget via deployment risk); SRE wants stability (conserves budget). Without error budgets, this tension is resolved through politics or escalation. With error budgets:
- Product can spend the remaining budget on riskier releases if reliability has been high.
- SRE can point to objective budget burn data rather than subjective "this feels risky."
- Both teams are incentivized to invest in reliability work (better testing, canary deploys) because it directly grows their capacity for future feature velocity.

The budget is the negotiating currency. Engineers stop being the "department of no" and start being collaborative risk managers.

**Q18. What is the "toil tax" concept and how does it relate to error budgets?**
A: Toil is repetitive, manual, automatable operational work (manual deploys, on-call responses to noisy alerts, manual scaling operations). It has no permanent value — doing it once doesn't make it easier next time. Error budget policies often include a "toil cap": if SRE spends more than X% of their time on toil, that's treated as a budget-equivalent signal that reliability investment is needed, regardless of the actual SLO numbers. The idea is that high toil is a leading indicator of future budget burn — unsustainable operational load leads to incidents.

**Q19. What's the danger of setting SLOs too aggressively tight?**
A: Perverse incentives. If the SLO is 99.99% and teams regularly hit 99.95%, the budget is perpetually exhausted — release freezes become permanent, morale degrades, and teams game the numbers (excluding incidents from the SLO window, changing the definition of "failure"). Tight SLOs also discourage experimentation: if any risk might exhaust the budget, teams stop trying new things. SLOs should be set at the threshold where users are *actually* impacted, not at the maximum achievable reliability. Aspirational SLOs punish good performance.

**Q20. What is "error budget as a management tool" vs. "error budget theater"?**
A: Error budget theater is when teams track budgets in a dashboard but the policy is never enforced — releases never actually stop, reliability work never gets prioritized over features, and the numbers are retroactively adjusted after bad incidents. Symptoms: SLO targets that are always barely met, postmortems with no follow-up action items closed, error budget policies that exist in a doc no one has read. Real error budget management requires leadership commitment to actually pause releases, real consequence to burning the budget, and engineering time genuinely allocated to reliability work when triggered. The measure of whether a program is real: has it ever caused a feature release to be delayed?

## Section 6: Scenario / Design Questions

**Q21. "Your team's 30-day rolling SLO is 99.9% availability. You've consumed 70% of your error budget in the first 10 days. What do you do?"**
A: First, diagnose before acting — understand *what* burned the budget (single large incident vs. steady drip of small errors). Then:
1. **Assess rate**: 70% in 10 days = ~7% per day; remaining budget = 30% spread over 20 days = 1.5% per day allowed. You're still burning at ~5x the sustainable rate.
2. **Trigger policy**: at 70% consumed, most policies mandate increased scrutiny on releases — no high-risk deploys without SRE sign-off.
3. **Incident review**: identify the top contributors to budget burn; are they addressable quickly (a bad config, a missing circuit breaker)?
4. **Forecast**: if the current burn rate continues, budget exhausts in ~4 more days — notify product leadership now, not when it hits 100%.
5. **Communicate**: update stakeholders with the forecast and the planned reliability work; frame it as data-driven, not an engineering veto.

**Q22. "How would you implement error budget tracking for a Node.js service in practice?"**
A: End-to-end implementation:
1. **Define the SLI**: success rate = `(total_requests - 5xx_errors) / total_requests`. Instrument with a counter metric in Prometheus: `http_requests_total{status_code}`.
2. **Record the SLO**: document 99.9% success rate over a 28-day rolling window for the service's critical endpoints.
3. **Calculate remaining budget**: Prometheus recording rule computes error ratio over the window; error budget remaining = `SLO_target - actual_error_ratio` (in time or request units).
4. **Burn rate alert**: Alertmanager or Datadog monitor fires at 14.4x burn over 1 hour AND 3x burn over 6 hours (multiwindow strategy).
5. **Dashboard**: Grafana panel showing budget remaining (%), burn rate, and a projected exhaustion date.
6. **Policy**: documented in the team's runbook; CI/CD pipeline checks a budget API and blocks non-critical deploys when remaining budget < 20%.

**Q23. "A product manager argues that your 99.9% SLO is too conservative and you should relax it to 99.5% to allow faster iteration. How do you respond?"**
A: Engage with the data, not as a blocker. Ask:
- What does our current actual reliability look like? If we're at 99.97%, a 99.5% SLO gives us 3.6 hours/month of allowed downtime — that's a meaningful relaxation, worth considering.
- What is the user impact at 99.5%? Pull support ticket data, session drop rates, conversion impact during past incidents. Quantify what 0.5% failure rate costs in revenue/NPS.
- What features specifically are blocked by the current SLO policy? Is the bottleneck actually the SLO, or is it deployment safety (slow CI, no canary)?
- Would a looser SLO actually change velocity, or would the budget still get burned by the same reliability issues?

Sometimes the right answer is to improve the deployment pipeline (canary releases, feature flags) so high-risk changes consume less budget per release — not to lower the target. Relaxing the SLO should be a last resort after ruling out that the real constraint is deployment process, not the SLO itself.

**Q24. "How do you handle error budgets in a multi-service architecture where Service A depends on Service B?"**
A: Key challenges:
- **Budget attribution**: if Service B is down, Service A's SLO burns — but the root cause is B. Track dependency-caused failures separately; attribute budget burn to the originating service.
- **Cascading policy**: if Service B exhausts its budget, it should trigger not just B's policy but potentially A's deployment freeze too — A shipping new features while B is unreliable makes things worse.
- **SLO composition**: Service A's achievable SLO is bounded by Service B's reliability. If B is 99.9%, A can't sustainably commit to 99.99% without redundancy/fallback patterns (circuit breakers, cached responses, graceful degradation).
- **Dependency SLOs**: Service B should publish its own SLO that Service A can plan against. Internal service SLOs are as important as external-facing ones.

## Quick Reference

| Concept             | Definition                                                           | Key detail                                                         |
|---                  |---                                                                   |---                                                                 |
| SLI                 | Measured metric (success rate, latency)                              | The raw signal                                                     |
| SLO                 | Target threshold for SLI over a window                              | The commitment; drives the budget                                  |
| Error budget        | `(1 - SLO) × window`                                                | Allowable failure; shared by product and engineering               |
| Burn rate           | `current_error_rate / (1 - SLO)`                                    | 1x = on track; >1x = depleting faster than sustainable            |
| Rolling window      | Trailing N-day SLO window                                           | Preferred for operations; old incidents age out                    |
| Calendar window     | Resets at period start                                               | Used for SLAs; creates uneven pressure across the month           |
| Error budget policy | Pre-agreed actions at budget thresholds                             | Must be signed off before incidents, not negotiated during them   |
| Toil                | Repetitive manual ops work                                           | Leading indicator of budget burn; cap via policy                  |

---

**Tip for the interview:** At 5 YOE level, interviewers care most about whether you understand error budgets as an organizational tool, not just a calculation. Be ready to explain the burn rate formula and multiwindow alerting, but also be ready to discuss how you'd convince a product manager to honor a release freeze. The best signal you can give: describe a time the budget policy actually changed a business decision — a delayed feature, a reliability sprint that got funded, or an SLO that was consciously relaxed with supporting data. That's what separates someone who has read the SRE book from someone who has operated under it.
