# TTL (DNS) — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is TTL in the context of DNS, precisely?**
A: TTL (Time To Live) is a numeric value, expressed in seconds, attached to every individual DNS resource record by whoever manages that record at the authoritative nameserver. It tells any resolver or client that caches the record the *maximum* duration it may continue serving that cached answer before it must discard it and re-query for a fresh copy. TTL is fundamentally a cache-expiry instruction, not a delivery guarantee or a propagation mechanism in itself — it governs how long a *stale* answer is allowed to keep being served, not how fast a *new* answer spreads.

**Q2. Where is TTL set, and can different records for the same domain have different TTLs?**
A: TTL is set per-record, by whoever manages the domain's authoritative DNS (via a zone file directly, or more commonly through a managed DNS provider's console/API like Route 53 or Cloudflare DNS). Yes — different records for the same domain are entirely independent in this regard: `example.com`'s A record might have a 300-second TTL while its MX record has an 86400-second TTL, and there's no requirement they match. This independence is intentional, since different record types have very different real-world volatility (an A record behind a load balancer might change far more often than mail server configuration).

**Q3. What's the practical effect of a record's TTL from the perspective of a resolver that has just received and cached it?**
A: The resolver starts a countdown from the moment it received the record. For any subsequent query for that same record arriving before the countdown expires, the resolver answers directly from its cache, without contacting the authoritative server at all. Once the countdown hits zero, the next query for that record forces the resolver to discard the stale cached copy and perform a fresh query against the authoritative chain, after which it caches the new answer with whatever TTL that fresh response carries (which may differ from the original, if the authoritative side has since changed it).

**Q4. Is TTL the same thing as "DNS propagation time"? Why is this a common point of confusion?**
A: No, and conflating the two is a very common imprecision. TTL is about how long *already-cached* answers remain valid — it says nothing about how quickly a record change becomes visible to clients who haven't cached anything yet (a brand-new client querying after your change will get the new value immediately, regardless of the old TTL). "Propagation" as commonly used loosely refers to the worst-case time for *all* existing caches holding the *old* value to expire and refresh — which is bounded by the *old* record's TTL at the time of the change, not the new TTL you might set going forward. This distinction matters operationally and is covered in depth in Section 4.

## Section 2: TTL Behavior Across Caching Layers

**Q5. What are the distinct layers at which a DNS answer can be cached, beyond the recursive resolver?**
A: Several independent layers, each potentially holding its own copy with its own effective lifetime: the **recursive resolver's cache** (ISP-level, public resolver like 8.8.8.8/1.1.1.1, or a corporate resolver) — the canonical "TTL-respecting" layer; the **operating system's stub resolver cache** (e.g., `nscd`/`systemd-resolved` on Linux, the DNS client service on Windows); the **browser's internal DNS cache** (browsers often maintain their own short-lived cache independent of the OS); and **application-level connection pinning** (a long-lived connection that resolved a name once doesn't re-resolve mid-connection, regardless of TTL — covered in Q24). Each layer can behave slightly differently with respect to honoring the authoritative TTL.

**Q6. Do all of these layers strictly honor the TTL value the authoritative server set?**
A: Not universally, which is a key thing to be precise about in an interview. Most resolvers and OS caches respect TTL reasonably faithfully, but some — particularly certain ISP-level or corporate resolvers — have historically imposed their own minimum caching floor, effectively overriding a very short TTL (e.g., refusing to cache for less than some internal minimum, for their own query-volume-reduction reasons) and continuing to serve a cached answer slightly longer than the authoritative TTL technically permits. Browser and application-level caches can introduce their own independent timing that's only loosely tied to the DNS TTL at all. The practical takeaway: TTL is the *maximum* a well-behaved cache should hold an answer, not a guarantee that every layer behaves exactly that way.

**Q7. How does TTL interact with the fact that a single resolver often serves many downstream clients?**
A: A large resolver (an ISP's recursive resolver, a corporate DNS forwarder) caches one answer and serves it to potentially thousands of individual clients behind it, all sharing that one cached copy until its TTL expires. This has two effects: it dramatically reduces the actual query volume reaching your authoritative nameservers (good — fewer redundant lookups for the same answer), but it also means a TTL-based change doesn't propagate to those downstream clients gradually and individually — they all flip over essentially simultaneously, whenever that one shared cache entry expires and gets refreshed, rather than each client refreshing on its own independent schedule.

## Section 3: Choosing TTL Values — Trade-offs

**Q8. What's the fundamental trade-off in choosing a short TTL?**
A: A short TTL (e.g., 30–60 seconds) means changes to the record propagate to caches quickly once made (useful for failover, fast IP changes, anything where rapid cutover matters), at the direct cost of significantly higher query volume against your authoritative nameservers — every cache expires and re-queries far more often — and a marginally higher per-request latency hit for clients on cache misses (a fresh full resolution chain is somewhat more expensive than a cache hit, though this is usually a minor factor relative to the volume cost in most architectures).

**Q9. What's the fundamental trade-off in choosing a long TTL?**
A: A long TTL (e.g., 24 hours / 86400 seconds) minimizes load on your authoritative infrastructure (far more cache hits, far fewer full resolution chains hitting your servers) and gives slightly better average resolution latency for clients (more cache hits). The cost: slow propagation if you ever need to change the record — clients holding a cached copy under the old long TTL will keep using the stale value for up to that full duration after you update the authoritative record, regardless of how quickly you make the change on your end.

**Q10. What TTL would you typically choose for a record you genuinely never expect to change (e.g., a long-standing MX record)?**
A: Lean toward a long TTL (hours to a day or more) — there's little upside to short-TTL agility for a record with no realistic change scenario on the horizon, and the downside (unnecessary query volume) is a pure cost with no corresponding benefit. The general principle: TTL should roughly track how *volatile* you expect a record to actually be, not be set defensively short "just in case" for everything, since that defensive instinct mostly just generates avoidable load.

**Q11. What TTL would you typically choose for a record fronting infrastructure that might need rapid failover (e.g., a primary/backup database endpoint, or a load balancer IP during an active migration)?**
A: Short — low enough (often well under a minute, sometimes as low as is practically supported) that if failover genuinely needs to happen, the change reaches clients quickly rather than being stuck behind a long stale-cache window during an incident. The query-volume cost of a short TTL on such a record is usually an acceptable trade against the alternative: a slow, TTL-bound recovery during exactly the moment you most need things to recover fast.

## Section 4: TTL and Planned Infrastructure Changes

**Q12. What is the standard operational procedure for TTL around a planned change (e.g., a migration or major cutover)?**
A: Lower the TTL on the relevant record well in advance of the actual change — and critically, **wait at least the duration of the *old* TTL** before making the real change, since any caches that already picked up the record under the old, longer TTL will keep honoring that longer duration regardless of when you update the TTL value itself (a fresh TTL only applies going forward, to *new* cache entries created after the change). Once that waiting window has passed, every active cache should now be operating under the new, short TTL — at which point you make the actual value change (the new IP, the new target), and it propagates quickly because everyone's now re-checking far more often. After the cutover is confirmed stable, raise the TTL back to its normal longer value to reduce ongoing query load.

**Q13. Why is "wait out the old TTL" specifically the step people forget, and what happens if you skip it?**
A: It's easy to assume that lowering the TTL value takes effect immediately for everyone — but a TTL change is itself just another field in a DNS record, and it only affects *new* cache entries created from that point forward. A resolver that cached the record five minutes before you lowered the TTL is still operating on the old TTL's countdown, which it received and locked in before your change. If you skip the waiting step and make the real value change immediately after lowering TTL, you end up with a slow, inconsistent rollout: some clients (those that hadn't cached recently) pick up the new value quickly, while others (those with a recently-cached long-TTL copy) remain stuck on the old value for however much of the old TTL's duration was left when you made the change — exactly the slow, unpredictable propagation you were trying to avoid by lowering TTL in the first place.

**Q14. How far in advance should you lower the TTL before a planned cutover, concretely?**
A: At minimum, far enough in advance that the *current* (about-to-be-replaced) long TTL has had a full opportunity to expire everywhere — practically, "at least the old TTL's duration," though many teams build in extra margin beyond the bare minimum to account for the imperfect TTL-honoring behavior discussed in Q6 (some caches running slightly longer than instructed). For a record currently at a 24-hour TTL, that means lowering it to a short value at least 24 hours (often more, for safety margin) before the actual cutover is performed.

**Q15. After a successful migration, why bother raising the TTL back up rather than just leaving it short permanently?**
A: A short TTL has an ongoing cost — elevated query volume against your authoritative nameservers indefinitely, for no further benefit once the change has stabilized and you're no longer expecting near-term further changes. Leaving it short "just in case" is the same defensive-but-costly instinct flagged in Q10; once the record has settled into its new stable state, raising the TTL back to a normal duration removes that ongoing load with no real downside, since you've already captured the agility benefit when it mattered (during the actual cutover window).

## Section 5: Negative Caching

**Q16. What is negative caching in DNS?**
A: When a query returns "this name doesn't exist" (NXDOMAIN) or "this record type doesn't exist for this name" (NODATA), resolvers cache that *negative* result too, not just successful answers — this avoids repeatedly re-querying the authoritative chain for something that's already confirmed not to exist, which would otherwise be pure wasted load on a predictable, repeatable miss.

**Q17. What governs the duration of negative caching, and how is it different from regular TTL?**
A: Regular per-record TTL doesn't apply, because there's no actual record to attach a TTL value to when the answer is "this doesn't exist." Instead, negative caching duration is governed by the **minimum TTL field in the zone's SOA record** — a single, zone-wide value that applies to all negative responses for that zone, distinct from any individual record's own TTL.

**Q18. What's the practical operational gotcha with negative caching that catches people off guard?**
A: If a hostname was queried (and returned NXDOMAIN) *before* you created the corresponding record, resolvers that cached that negative result won't re-query and discover your newly-created record until the SOA's negative-cache TTL expires — meaning a record you just correctly published can appear to "not exist yet" for some users, sometimes for a meaningfully long window, purely because of a stale negative cache from before the record existed. This is a common source of confusing "I just added the DNS record, why is it still not resolving" support tickets, and the fix is patience (waiting out the SOA minimum TTL) rather than re-checking your own record configuration, which may already be entirely correct.

## Section 6: TTL in Traffic Management

**Q19. How does TTL choice interact with DNS-based traffic management mechanisms like weighted routing or failover?**
A: Any DNS-layer traffic management (weighted routing for gradual migrations, GeoDNS, health-check-driven failover) only converges as fast as its underlying TTL allows — a client that's already resolved and cached an answer stays "locked in" to that answer (and whichever backend it implies) until the cache expires, regardless of how quickly the underlying routing policy or health status changes server-side. This is why DNS-based traffic shifting is generally described as coarse and slow-reacting relative to a real-time L4/L7 load balancer: the TTL puts a hard floor on how quickly *any* DNS-driven traffic decision can actually take effect for already-cached clients.

**Q20. For DNS-based health-check failover specifically, how should TTL be tuned?**
A: Short enough that, when an unhealthy endpoint is detected and removed from rotation, clients actually stop being routed there within an acceptable window for your failover SLA — if your target is "failover should be substantially complete within two minutes," a TTL of several minutes or more directly undermines that target regardless of how fast the health-check detection itself is, since the bottleneck shifts entirely to cache expiry. At the same time, this needs to be balanced against the corresponding query-volume cost (Q8) — a very aggressive sub-10-second TTL on a high-traffic record can meaningfully increase load on the DNS provider's health-check-integrated infrastructure, so the choice is typically tuned to the specific failover SLA needed, not set to the theoretical minimum by default.

**Q21. Does a short TTL guarantee fast failover in practice? What's the caveat?**
A: No — it sets the *theoretical* ceiling on propagation speed assuming perfect TTL-honoring behavior everywhere, but per Q6, not every caching layer respects very short TTLs precisely, and connection-level pinning (Q24) is entirely orthogonal to TTL and can keep already-established connections routed to a now-unhealthy endpoint regardless of how short the DNS TTL is. A short TTL is necessary but not sufficient for genuinely fast, reliable failover — it's typically combined with proper L4/L7 health-check-aware load balancing within each region/endpoint as the actual fine-grained failover mechanism, with DNS-level TTL tuning serving as the coarse, first layer.

## Section 7: Performance and Operational Considerations

**Q22. What should you monitor with respect to TTL and caching behavior for records you operate?**
A: Query volume trends at the authoritative level relative to configured TTLs (a sudden, unexplained spike in queries for a record can indicate the TTL was set lower than intended, a misbehaving client ignoring cache entirely and querying far more often than it should, or — in more concerning cases — abuse/reconnaissance traffic); propagation verification after any TTL or value change (actively querying from multiple independent resolvers/geographic vantage points to confirm a change has genuinely rolled out as expected, rather than purely trusting TTL math); and, for zones with negative caching relevance, NXDOMAIN rates that might indicate stale negative caching affecting newly-created records (Q18).

**Q23. How would you verify the current effective TTL of a record from the command line, and why might the answer differ between resolvers?**
A: `dig example.com` (or specifying the record type, `dig example.com A`) shows the TTL value in the response — but that TTL reflects however much time is *remaining* on that particular resolver's cached copy, not necessarily the record's originally configured TTL. Querying a resolver that just refreshed its cache shows close to the full configured TTL; querying one that cached the record a while ago shows a smaller remaining value, counting down toward zero. Querying the authoritative nameserver directly (`dig example.com @ns1.your-dns-provider.com`) bypasses any intermediate caching and shows the record exactly as currently configured, which is the most reliable way to confirm the authoritative-side TTL setting itself rather than a snapshot of someone's in-progress countdown.

**Q24. How does application-level connection pinning interact with TTL, and why is it a distinct problem from DNS caching itself?**
A: Once an application resolves a hostname and establishes a long-lived connection (a database connection, a gRPC channel, a kept-alive HTTP connection pool) to whatever IP that resolution returned, the connection itself doesn't periodically re-check DNS or respect the record's TTL mid-connection — TTL governs *resolution caching*, not the lifetime of connections already established using a prior resolution. So even with an aggressively short TTL configured correctly at the DNS layer, an application holding a long-lived connection from before a record change won't pick up the new value until that connection is torn down and a fresh resolution happens (on reconnect, restart, or explicit re-resolution logic). This is a frequent, genuinely distinct failure mode from DNS-layer caching issues, and is often the actual root cause behind "TTL is short but clients still seem stuck on the old value" investigations.

## Section 8: Scenario-Based Questions

**Q25. "A user reports they're still seeing old, broken behavior 20 minutes after your team updated a DNS record, well past the configured 60-second TTL. How do you investigate?"**
A: Work through the layers methodically rather than assuming "DNS isn't updating" as an undifferentiated cause: (1) confirm the change genuinely propagated to the authoritative nameservers in the first place by querying them directly, bypassing any resolver — sometimes the issue is actually a deployment/configuration problem, not a caching problem at all; (2) check whether the specific resolver the user is behind (their ISP's, a corporate one) might be overriding the short TTL with its own minimum caching floor (Q6); (3) check whether the staleness is happening at a layer other than DNS resolution entirely — a browser-level cache, an OS-level cache, or very commonly, application-level connection pinning (Q24), where the *application itself*, not DNS, is the one still using a stale resolved IP from before the change; (4) if DNSSEC is enabled, rule out a validation failure causing fallback to a stale cached state. The diagnostic discipline is isolating *which* layer is actually serving the stale answer rather than treating "DNS" as one monolithic suspect.

**Q26. "You're about to migrate a high-traffic production API to new infrastructure with a different IP. The current record's TTL is 24 hours. Walk through your TTL strategy."**
A: At least 24 hours (the current TTL's duration) before the planned cutover, lower the TTL on the record to something short — e.g., 60 seconds — and then *wait out that full 24-hour-old-TTL window* before touching the actual record value, since clients who cached the record under the old 24-hour TTL just before your change won't respect the new shorter TTL until their existing cache entry naturally expires (Q13). Only once that waiting period has elapsed do you perform the actual cutover: update the record to the new IP (or use a weighted routing policy to gradually shift traffic rather than an instant flip, if available). Monitor both old and new infrastructure's traffic and error rates during the transition to catch any clients still resolving the old value due to imperfect TTL-honoring behavior elsewhere (Q6) before fully decommissioning the old infrastructure. Once the cutover is confirmed stable, raise the TTL back to its normal longer duration.

**Q27. "Your authoritative nameservers are seeing unexpectedly high query volume for a specific record. What TTL-related causes would you investigate?"**
A: Check whether that record's TTL was recently lowered (intentionally for a migration, or accidentally via a misconfiguration) and never raised back afterward — a forgotten short TTL left in place long after the change it was meant to support has stabilized is a very common, simple explanation for sustained elevated query volume (Q15). Also check whether the record sits at the end of a chain involving a third-party target with its own short TTL (relevant for CNAME-based records specifically), since the *effective* TTL governing how often clients re-query can be shorter than what's configured on your own record if a downstream hop expires faster. If TTL configuration genuinely looks correct and conservative, then the elevated volume may instead point toward a misbehaving client population ignoring cache TTL altogether, or — in a more concerning case — abuse traffic, which would warrant a different investigation track entirely (e.g., checking for amplification-attack patterns rather than legitimate caching behavior).

**Q28. "Leadership wants to know why, despite your team setting an aggressively short 10-second TTL on a critical failover record, some users still experienced downtime for several minutes during an incident. How do you explain this?"**
A: A short TTL sets the *ceiling* on how fast DNS-driven failover can propagate to caches that are honoring it correctly — it doesn't account for caching layers that don't perfectly respect very short TTLs (some ISP/corporate resolvers impose their own minimum floor regardless of the configured value, Q6), nor does it have any effect on clients that had already established long-lived connections before the incident, since those connections are pinned to their originally-resolved IP and won't re-resolve mid-connection regardless of how short the DNS TTL is (Q24, Q21). The honest framing: TTL tuning is necessary for fast DNS-driven failover but not sufficient on its own — a fully robust failover strategy needs the short TTL *combined with* connection-level resilience (e.g., client libraries or infrastructure that periodically re-resolve and rebalance even mid-session) and, where possible, fine-grained L4/L7 health-check-aware load balancing within each endpoint rather than relying on DNS TTL expiry as the sole failover mechanism.

**Q29. "Your team wants to reduce authoritative DNS query costs/load across the board. Several records currently have a 60-second TTL left over from various past migrations. What would you recommend?"**
A: Audit each record's actual volatility going forward rather than leaving the short TTL by default inertia — for records tied to infrastructure that's now stable and not expecting near-term changes, raise the TTL back to something more typical (e.g., hours, per Q10–Q15), since the short TTL is no longer buying any real agility benefit and is purely generating avoidable query volume. For records still genuinely tied to volatile or failover-sensitive infrastructure, keep the short TTL deliberately, since the cost there is justified by the agility need (Q11, Q20). The general recommendation: TTL should be revisited as a deliberate, per-record decision tied to actual expected volatility, not left at whatever value a past one-off migration happened to set and then forgotten.

**Q30. "A newly created subdomain isn't resolving for some users even though you've confirmed the A record is correctly published on your authoritative nameservers. What TTL-related explanation should you check first?"**
A: Stale negative caching (Q16–18): if that subdomain was queried by anyone — including automated scanners, monitoring tools, or even an earlier attempt before the record was actually live — before you published the record, the NXDOMAIN response from that earlier query may still be cached by some resolvers, governed by the zone's SOA minimum TTL rather than the new record's own TTL. Those resolvers won't notice your new record exists until that negative-cache duration expires on their end, regardless of how correctly and immediately your authoritative side is now answering. The practical response is usually to confirm this is indeed the cause (e.g., by directly querying the authoritative server to show the record is correct, contrasted with a resolver still returning NXDOMAIN) and then simply wait out the SOA's negative-cache TTL, since there's typically no faster forcing mechanism for resolvers that already cached the negative result.

## Quick Reference Table

| Concept | Key Idea |
|---|---|
| TTL | Max duration a cache may serve a record before re-querying; set per-record by the authoritative side |
| Short TTL | Fast propagation, higher authoritative query load |
| Long TTL | Lower query load, slow propagation if the record changes |
| TTL ≠ propagation guarantee | Some resolvers/clients don't perfectly honor very short TTLs |
| Pre-change procedure | Lower TTL, wait out the *old* TTL's duration, then change the value, then raise TTL back |
| Negative caching | NXDOMAIN/NODATA cached per the zone's SOA minimum TTL, not per-record TTL |
| Chain TTL (CNAME) | Effective TTL = shortest TTL among all hops in the resolution chain |
| Connection pinning | Long-lived connections don't re-resolve mid-connection regardless of TTL |
| TTL in traffic mgmt | Sets the floor on how fast DNS-driven routing/failover changes can take effect |
| Verification | `dig` against the authoritative server directly shows the true configured value, bypassing caches |

---

**Tip for interviews:** What separates strong candidates on TTL specifically: (1) being precise that **TTL is a maximum, not a guarantee** — multiple caching layers exist beyond the authoritative TTL, some of which don't perfectly honor very short values (Q6); (2) knowing the **full pre-change TTL procedure**, especially the frequently-missed detail of waiting out the *old* TTL's duration before changing the value, not just lowering the TTL and immediately proceeding (Q12–13); (3) understanding that **negative caching is governed by SOA minimum TTL, not per-record TTL**, and being able to explain the "newly created record appears not to exist yet" gotcha that follows from it (Q16–18); (4) clearly separating **DNS-layer caching from application-level connection pinning** as two genuinely distinct causes of "stale value" symptoms, since conflating them is a common diagnostic mistake (Q24–25); and (5) framing TTL's role in traffic management/failover honestly — as a **necessary but not sufficient** lever, properly layered with connection-level resilience and real load-balancer health checking rather than treated as a complete failover solution on its own (Q20–21, Q28).
