# Two-Phase Commit (2PC): Coordinator Failure Problem — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is Two-Phase Commit (2PC)?**
A: 2PC is a distributed protocol that coordinates a single atomic transaction across multiple participants (database nodes, services, resource managers). It guarantees that either all participants commit the transaction or all abort — no partial commits. It works by separating the commit decision into two distinct phases: a voting phase (can everyone commit?) and an execution phase (everyone now does commit or abort).

**Q2. Walk through the two phases in detail.**
A:
- **Phase 1 — Prepare (Voting):** The coordinator sends a `PREPARE` message to all participants. Each participant checks whether it can commit (acquires locks, writes to its redo/undo log, durably persists the prepared state), then replies `YES` (vote to commit) or `NO` (vote to abort). A `YES` vote is a durable promise: "I can commit if asked to."
- **Phase 2 — Commit/Abort (Execution):** If all participants voted `YES`, the coordinator logs its commit decision durably, then sends `COMMIT` to all participants. If any participant voted `NO` (or timed out), the coordinator sends `ABORT` to all. Each participant then executes the commit or rollback and releases its locks.

**Q3. What roles do coordinator and participant play, and what state does each maintain?**
A: The **coordinator** drives the protocol: sends `PREPARE`, collects votes, makes the global decision, sends `COMMIT`/`ABORT`, and waits for acknowledgements. It persists its decision to a durable log before broadcasting it. A **participant** responds to coordinator commands: votes on `PREPARE`, executes `COMMIT` or `ABORT`, and persists its own state (voted, committed, aborted) durably at each step so it can survive restarts.

**Q4. What property does a `YES` vote guarantee?**
A: A `YES` vote is an irrevocable, durable promise. Once a participant votes `YES`, it cannot unilaterally abort the transaction on its own — it must wait for the coordinator's decision. It has written all necessary data to disk, acquired all needed locks, and is prepared to commit or roll back on command. This promise survives participant crashes: if a participant restarts after voting `YES`, it must still honor the commitment to commit or abort as instructed by the coordinator.

**Q5. What guarantees does 2PC provide?**
A:
- **Atomicity** — all nodes commit or all abort; no partial outcome.
- **Durability** — once the coordinator decides and logs the decision, committed data survives failures.

What 2PC does *not* provide: partition tolerance and availability during failures. It is a CA protocol in CAP terms — it blocks when the coordinator or a participant is unavailable.

**Q6. What is the fundamental blocking problem in 2PC?**
A: If the coordinator fails *after* participants have voted `YES` but *before* broadcasting the commit/abort decision, participants are stuck in the **prepared state** — holding locks, unable to commit or abort unilaterally, and with no way to determine the global outcome. They must wait for the coordinator to recover. This is the core flaw: 2PC is a **blocking protocol**.

## Section 2: The Coordinator Failure Problem

**Q7. What are the coordinator's failure windows, and which one is dangerous?**
A: The coordinator can fail at several points:
- **Before sending PREPARE** — harmless; no participant has done anything. Transaction times out and is treated as aborted.
- **After sending PREPARE, before receiving all votes** — coordinator aborts on recovery (safe; no participant has committed).
- **After receiving all YES votes, before writing commit to its log** — coordinator aborts on recovery (safe; no participant has committed, since coordinator never sent COMMIT).
- **After writing commit to log, before sending COMMIT to participants** ← **dangerous window**. The coordinator has decided to commit, but some or all participants haven't received `COMMIT`. If the coordinator never recovers, participants holding locks can't know the decision and block indefinitely.
- **After sending COMMIT to some participants, before all acknowledge** — similarly dangerous; the transaction is partially applied from participants' view until the coordinator recovers and re-sends.

**Q8. Why can't participants resolve the uncertainty themselves when the coordinator fails?**
A: A participant that voted `YES` knows its own vote but doesn't know the votes of other participants. If it contacts another participant, that participant also only knows its own vote — neither can determine the global outcome without the coordinator's decision log. Even if a participant can reach every other participant and they all voted `YES`, they still can't safely commit unilaterally: the coordinator may have decided to abort for a reason they're unaware of (e.g., a timeout before all votes arrived). The only safe action is to wait.

**Q9. What is the "in-doubt" or "uncertain" state, and what does it mean operationally?**
A: A participant enters the **in-doubt** state after voting `YES` but before receiving `COMMIT` or `ABORT`. Operationally it means the participant is holding:
- **Row/table locks** on the data involved in the transaction.
- **Undo/redo log space** for the prepared transaction.
- **Open transaction slots** in the database.

These resources are held for as long as the participant remains in-doubt. In a real database, an in-doubt transaction can block other transactions that need the same rows. A long coordinator outage means those locks are held for the entire duration — potentially hours or days until manual intervention.

**Q10. What happens when the coordinator recovers from a crash?**
A: On restart, the coordinator reads its durable log:
- If it finds a **commit record** for the transaction → re-send `COMMIT` to all participants (some may have already committed; they acknowledge again, idempotently).
- If it finds a **prepare record but no commit record** → the decision was never made or logged; send `ABORT` to all participants.
- If it finds **neither** → the transaction never started properly; treat as aborted.

This is why the coordinator *must* write its decision to durable storage before sending any `COMMIT` messages — if it crashed after sending `COMMIT` to one participant and before writing the log, on recovery it would falsely abort an already-partially-committed transaction.

**Q11. What is "heuristic resolution" and when is it used?**
A: When the coordinator fails and cannot recover within an acceptable time, DBAs or administrators may manually resolve in-doubt participants using **heuristic decisions** — forcing a commit or rollback based on external knowledge of the business context. This deliberately violates atomicity (some participants may be committed, others aborted), so it is a last resort that requires application-level compensating transactions to repair inconsistency afterward. Most databases expose commands like `COMMIT TRANSACTION WITH MARK` or XA's `xa_forget` for this purpose.

**Q12. What is the coordinator's single point of failure problem at the architecture level?**
A: In basic 2PC, there is one coordinator. If that node fails permanently (disk corruption, data center loss), every in-doubt transaction on every participant is stuck indefinitely. The coordinator holds the only authoritative record of the commit decision. This makes the coordinator a SPOF not just for availability but for **data consistency** — making it one of the most dangerous single points of failure in distributed systems design.

## Section 3: Participant Failure Modes

**Q13. What happens if a participant fails before voting?**
A: Safe. The coordinator either receives no vote (timeout) or an explicit failure signal. Since no participant has committed, the coordinator aborts the transaction. No locks were durably held, so cleanup is straightforward.

**Q14. What happens if a participant fails after voting YES but before receiving the decision?**
A: The participant restarts in the **in-doubt** state (it logged its `YES` vote before crashing). On recovery it contacts the coordinator to ask for the decision. If the coordinator is alive and has the decision, it re-sends `COMMIT` or `ABORT`. If the coordinator is also down, the participant remains in-doubt and blocks — same problem as coordinator failure, just from the other direction.

**Q15. What happens if a participant fails after receiving and acknowledging COMMIT?**
A: Safe. The participant committed durably before sending the acknowledgement. On recovery it finds the commit record in its log and the transaction is already done. No re-coordination needed.

**Q16. What is "participant blocking" and how does it differ from coordinator blocking?**
A: Coordinator blocking means participants are stuck waiting for a crashed coordinator. Participant blocking means the coordinator is stuck waiting for acknowledgements from crashed participants. Both cause the coordinator to hold the transaction open and keep re-trying `COMMIT`/`ABORT` until the participant recovers and acknowledges. The difference: participant blocking doesn't create in-doubt uncertainty for other participants — the coordinator already has the global decision — it just delays cleanup and resource release.

**Q17. Can a participant unilaterally abort after voting YES?**
A: No — this is the key constraint. Unilateral abort after a `YES` vote would violate atomicity if the coordinator had already decided to commit and sent `COMMIT` to other participants. Those participants would have committed while this one aborted — a split-brain consistency violation. The `YES` vote is an irrevocable binding promise that must be honored according to the coordinator's decision.

## Section 4: Recovery Mechanisms & Mitigations

**Q18. How do transaction logs enable coordinator recovery?**
A: The coordinator writes a force-flushed (fsync'd) log record at every critical state transition: transaction start, prepare sent, all votes received, commit/abort decided, and completion. On restart it replays this log to determine the state of each transaction and re-sends any outstanding messages. This write-ahead log (WAL) is what makes recovery deterministic — without it, crashed coordinators would have no way to reconstruct their decisions.

**Q19. What is "presumed abort" and how does it optimize recovery?**
A: Presumed abort is an optimization where, if the coordinator has no record of a transaction, it assumes it aborted. This means the coordinator doesn't need to log `ABORT` decisions — it only logs `COMMIT` decisions. Participants similarly assume abort if they have no record of a prepared transaction. This reduces log writes on the abort path, which is common. The trade-off: on recovery the coordinator can't distinguish "committed and log lost" from "aborted" without the commit log record, so it's critical that commit is always logged before sending `COMMIT` messages.

**Q20. How does making the coordinator highly available mitigate the coordinator failure problem?**
A: Run the coordinator as a replicated state machine (e.g., using Raft or Paxos) across multiple nodes. The coordinator's log is replicated to a quorum before any decision is broadcast. If the primary coordinator fails, a replica takes over with the full log state and can re-send outstanding `COMMIT`/`ABORT` messages. This eliminates the single point of failure for the coordinator — a coordinator failure becomes a short leader-election delay rather than indefinite blocking. This is the approach used by systems like Google Spanner (using Paxos groups as coordinators).

**Q21. What is Three-Phase Commit (3PC) and does it solve the coordinator failure problem?**
A: 3PC adds a `PRE-COMMIT` phase between `PREPARE` and `COMMIT`. After all `YES` votes, the coordinator sends `PRE-COMMIT` to all participants, waits for acknowledgements, then sends `COMMIT`. The key property: if a participant has received `PRE-COMMIT`, it knows all participants voted `YES` and the coordinator decided to commit — so it can safely commit unilaterally if the coordinator fails. If it has *not* received `PRE-COMMIT`, it knows it can safely abort.

3PC solves coordinator failure in **synchronous networks** (no unbounded message delay). But it fails in **asynchronous networks** (the real world): a participant can't distinguish "coordinator crashed" from "network is slow," so it may time out and commit unilaterally while the coordinator is actually alive and processing an abort decision — causing split brain. 3PC is non-blocking only under synchrony assumptions that don't hold in practice, which is why it's rarely used in production systems.

**Q22. What does the FLP impossibility result mean for 2PC?**
A: The Fischer-Lynch-Paterson (FLP) result proves that no deterministic consensus protocol can guarantee both safety and liveness in an asynchronous system with even one possible process failure. 2PC prioritizes safety (no inconsistent commits) over liveness (it blocks rather than making a wrong decision). 3PC attempts to recover liveness but sacrifices safety under network partitions. There is no free lunch: any practical solution either blocks under failure (2PC), risks split-brain under partition (3PC), or requires additional assumptions (synchronous networks, stable coordinators).

**Q23. What are coordinator-free alternatives to 2PC?**
A: Several patterns avoid the coordinator failure problem by not using a single coordinator:
- **Paxos/Raft-based atomic commit** — each participant is part of a replicated group; the "coordinator" role is distributed and fault-tolerant by design (e.g., Spanner, CockroachDB).
- **Saga pattern** — breaks a transaction into a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating transaction for rollback. No coordinator holds global locks; failures trigger compensation. Trades strong atomicity for availability and eventual consistency.
- **Optimistic protocols (e.g., Calvin, CRDT-based)** — pre-order transactions before execution to avoid coordination during commit.

## Section 5: 2PC in Real Systems

**Q24. How does XA (eXtended Architecture) relate to 2PC?**
A: XA is the standard API (X/Open specification) that defines how a transaction manager (coordinator) interacts with resource managers (participants — databases, message brokers, etc.) to implement 2PC. It defines calls like `xa_prepare`, `xa_commit`, `xa_rollback`, and `xa_recover`. Most enterprise databases (Oracle, PostgreSQL, MySQL) and message brokers implement XA. Java's JTA (Java Transaction API) sits on top of XA. XA inherits all 2PC's coordinator failure problems — it is 2PC with a standardized interface.

**Q25. How does Google Spanner implement distributed transactions without the classical 2PC coordinator failure problem?**
A: Spanner uses **Paxos groups** — each shard's data is replicated across multiple servers using Paxos, so the "coordinator" for any transaction is itself a replicated group, not a single node. The coordinator's state (transaction log) is replicated to a quorum before the decision is made. Coordinator "failure" is handled by Paxos leader election in milliseconds, not indefinite blocking. Spanner also uses **TrueTime** (bounded-uncertainty atomic clocks) to provide external consistency without blocking on clock synchronization. The result is effectively non-blocking 2PC under single-node failures, because the coordinator is never truly a single node.

**Q26. How does Kafka handle distributed commit across producers and consumers without 2PC?**
A: Kafka's transactional API uses a **transaction coordinator** (a specific broker) plus an internal transaction log topic. When a producer commits, the coordinator writes the commit marker to the log. If the coordinator fails, a new coordinator is elected from the replicated log and picks up where the previous one left off. Consumers using `read_committed` isolation skip messages in open transactions, only seeing committed data. This is essentially 2PC with a replicated coordinator — mitigating the SPOF problem via Kafka's own replication.

**Q27. When is 2PC actually the right tool?**
A: 2PC is appropriate when:
- You need strict atomicity across multiple heterogeneous data stores (e.g., a relational DB and a message broker in a single transaction).
- The transaction volume is low enough that coordinator failures are rare events, not design constraints.
- You have a replicated coordinator (removing the SPOF concern).
- The workload can tolerate blocking during coordinator recovery (e.g., internal back-office systems, financial ledgers where correctness matters more than millisecond availability).

Avoid 2PC for high-throughput, low-latency, user-facing services where locking and blocking are unacceptable — use Sagas or event-driven patterns instead.

## Section 6: Trade-offs & Decision-Making

**Q28. What are the fundamental costs of using 2PC?**
A:
- **Latency** — at minimum 2 round trips across all participants (prepare + commit), often more with acknowledgements. Across WAN or slow links this is significant.
- **Lock contention** — participants hold locks from `PREPARE` until `COMMIT` is received. Under coordinator failure, those locks are held indefinitely.
- **Coordinator SPOF** — without replication, a coordinator crash blocks all in-flight transactions.
- **Throughput ceiling** — the coordinator is a serialization point; its throughput limits the system's transaction rate.
- **Operational complexity** — in-doubt transactions require monitoring, alerting, and sometimes manual intervention.

**Q29. How does the Saga pattern trade off against 2PC?**
A:
- **2PC** gives strong atomicity (all-or-nothing) but blocks under coordinator failure, holds locks across the transaction duration, and requires all participants to support the prepare/commit protocol.
- **Sagas** give eventual consistency with compensating transactions (rollback by doing the inverse operation). No cross-service locking, no coordinator SPOF, works with services that don't support XA. Trade-off: compensating transactions are application-level logic that must be written and maintained; there is a window where partial state is visible (e.g., an order is created but payment hasn't been confirmed yet); and compensation may fail, requiring dead-letter queues and manual intervention.

**Q30. When should you choose Sagas over 2PC?**
A:
- When participants don't support the XA/2PC protocol (most REST microservices, NoSQL stores).
- When the transaction spans a long time (e.g., multi-step business process over seconds or minutes) — you can't hold locks that long.
- When availability is more important than strict atomicity — Sagas can make progress independently per step.
- When you own all the services involved and can design compensating logic upfront.

Choose 2PC over Sagas when you cannot tolerate any window of inconsistency (financial ledger, inventory reservation), you have XA-capable participants, and the transaction is short and bounded.

**Q31. What is "lock amplification" in 2PC and why does it matter at scale?**
A: In 2PC, a participant holds locks from the moment it votes `YES` until it receives `COMMIT` and applies it. If the coordinator is slow or fails, this lock window expands dramatically. At scale, many concurrent transactions each holding locks on overlapping rows creates a contention graph — transactions waiting for each other's locks. This is the concurrency cost of 2PC: the protocol serializes not just the commit decision but also the resource access window around it.

## Section 7: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q32. "You're designing a payment system where a charge must atomically debit a user account in DB-A and create a payment record in DB-B. Walk through your options."**
A:
- **Option 1 (2PC/XA)**: Use a transaction manager that coordinates both DBs. Works if both support XA. Atomic, but introduces coordinator SPOF, lock contention between DBs, and blocking risk. Appropriate if both DBs are in the same data center and coordinator is replicated.
- **Option 2 (Saga — choreography)**: Debit DB-A and publish a `PaymentDebited` event. A consumer creates the record in DB-B and publishes `PaymentRecorded`. If DB-B fails, publish `PaymentFailed`, trigger a compensating transaction to refund DB-A. No coordinator, but a window exists where DB-A is debited and DB-B isn't yet.
- **Option 3 (Outbox pattern)**: Write the DB-A debit and the DB-B instruction in the *same* local DB-A transaction into an outbox table. A relay process reads the outbox and writes to DB-B, with at-least-once delivery. Simpler than a full Saga; relies on idempotency at DB-B. Often the pragmatic choice for this pattern.

**Q33. "The coordinator in your 2PC system crashed and three participants are in-doubt. It's been 2 hours. What do you do?"**
A: First, attempt coordinator recovery — check if the coordinator's WAL is intact on disk and restart it. If the WAL is readable, the coordinator will re-send decisions and resolve participants automatically. If the coordinator is unrecoverable (disk failure, data loss): (1) query each participant's transaction log to determine their state — all should show `PREPARED`. (2) Determine the intended business outcome from application logs, audit trails, or the transaction initiator. (3) Use the DB's heuristic resolution command (e.g., `ROLLBACK PREPARED 'txn_id'` in PostgreSQL) on each participant to manually abort — accepting the atomicity violation. (4) Apply compensating transactions if any participant is found to have already committed (e.g., by another path). Document the incident; in-doubt transactions lasting hours indicate the coordinator has no redundancy and that should be fixed.

**Q34. "Explain why 2PC is called a 'blocking protocol' and what would have to be true about the network for it to be non-blocking."**
A: 2PC blocks because a participant in the `PREPARED` state has no safe action when it loses contact with the coordinator — it can't commit (the coordinator may have decided abort) or abort (the coordinator may have decided commit). Resolving this requires knowing the coordinator's decision, which requires the coordinator to be reachable.

For 2PC to be non-blocking, you'd need one of: (a) a **synchronous network** with known message delay bounds — participants can time out and take action knowing the coordinator isn't coming back (this is 3PC's assumption), or (b) a **replicated coordinator** with guaranteed availability so "coordinator failure" is never permanent. Real production systems choose (b) — replicate the coordinator's state machine so failures are temporary. The network being synchronous is not a realistic assumption.

**Q35. "A senior engineer says: 'Just use Sagas everywhere, 2PC is an anti-pattern.' Do you agree?"**
A: Not entirely. Sagas are the right choice for most modern microservice architectures — they avoid coordinator SPOFs, don't require XA support across services, and handle long-running workflows better. But they shift complexity from the infrastructure layer to the application layer: every Saga needs compensating logic, idempotency on every step, dead-letter handling, and careful design of the visible intermediate states. For short, bounded transactions across XA-capable stores where strict atomicity is non-negotiable (e.g., double-entry ledger updates), a 2PC with a replicated coordinator is simpler and more correct than a Saga with compensating transactions. The real anti-pattern is using 2PC with a single-node coordinator at scale — *that* is indefensible. 2PC with a Raft-replicated coordinator is used by production systems like Spanner and CockroachDB at global scale.

**Tip for the interview:** The key insight interviewers want to hear is *why* the coordinator failure is uniquely dangerous — not just "it crashes," but that it crashes *after participants have made an irrevocable promise*, leaving them holding locks with no safe unilateral action. From there, walk through the design space: replicated coordinator (the production answer), 3PC (clever but impractical), and Sagas (shifts complexity to the application). The strongest candidates also know that Spanner and CockroachDB "solved" 2PC by making the coordinator itself a Raft group — not by replacing 2PC, but by removing its SPOF.
