# PACELC Theorem — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: CAP Recap & Its Gaps

**Q1. What does the CAP theorem state, and what are its three properties?**
A: CAP (Brewer, 2000; formally proved by Gilbert & Lynch, 2002) states that a distributed data store can guarantee at most two of the following three properties simultaneously:
- **Consistency (C)**: Every read receives the most recent write or an error — more precisely, linearizability: the system appears as if there is a single up-to-date copy of the data.
- **Availability (A)**: Every request receives a non-error response — though it may not be the most recent write.
- **Partition tolerance (P)**: The system continues operating despite an arbitrary number of network messages being dropped or delayed between nodes.

Since network partitions can always occur in a real distributed system, P is not optional — you must tolerate partitions or stop being distributed. CAP therefore reduces to: **during a partition, choose C or A**.

**Q2. What are the three concrete criticisms of CAP that PACELC addresses?**
A: Three fundamental gaps:
- **Partitions are rare**: In practice, network partitions are infrequent. CAP says nothing about the trade-offs systems face during the 99.9%+ of time when no partition is occurring. Most of a system's operational life is partition-free — and trade-offs still exist.
- **Latency is invisible in CAP**: CAP treats availability as binary (respond or don't) and ignores *how fast* a system responds. In reality, a consistent system that responds in 2 seconds vs. an eventually consistent system that responds in 20ms are very different operationally — CAP does not capture this.
- **Consistency is treated as binary**: CAP's "consistent" specifically means linearizability. Real systems offer a spectrum — from linearizability down through sequential consistency, causal consistency, read-your-writes, monotonic reads, and eventual consistency. CAP collapses this spectrum into one bit.

**Q3. Who proposed PACELC and when?**
A: Daniel Abadi proposed PACELC in a 2012 paper *"Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design"*, specifically to address these gaps in CAP. The theorem reframes the trade-off space to cover both partition and non-partition scenarios.

## Section 2: PACELC Fundamentals

**Q4. What does PACELC stand for and state?**
A: PACELC is read as two conditional clauses:

```
If there is a Partition (P):
    trade off between Availability (A)  vs  Consistency (C)

Else (E) — during normal operation, no partition:
    trade off between Latency (L)  vs  Consistency (C)
```

Written compactly: **PA|PC / EL|EC** — a system makes one choice on the left of the slash (during partitions) and one on the right (during normal operation). The full classification of a system is expressed as one of: PA/EL, PA/EC, PC/EL, or PC/EC.

**Q5. Why does normal operation still involve a trade-off between latency and consistency?**
A: Because achieving consistency in a replicated system always requires coordination between nodes — and coordination takes time:
- **Synchronous replication**: A write is not acknowledged until a quorum of replicas confirms it. The client waits for the slowest confirming replica → higher write latency.
- **Quorum reads**: To guarantee you read the latest write, you query multiple replicas and take the most recent value → more round trips → higher read latency.
- **Leader routing**: To ensure linearizability, reads may be forced to go to the leader (primary), which may be geographically distant → speed-of-light latency.
- **Consensus protocols**: Raft/Paxos require at minimum two round trips per operation to achieve agreement.

In contrast, an eventually consistent system can respond from the nearest replica, no coordination required — dramatically lower latency. This latency-consistency tension exists in every replicated system, all the time, even when no partition has occurred.

**Q6. What is the minimum round-trip cost of strong consistency across geographic regions?**
A: The speed of light. A fiber-optic round trip between AWS us-east-1 (Virginia) and us-west-2 (Oregon) is ~70ms. Between us-east-1 and eu-west-1 (Ireland), ~170ms. Any synchronous write replication across these regions incurs at minimum this physical RTT, plus processing overhead. This is irreducible — no engineering can make light travel faster. A strongly consistent multi-region write will always be at least one RTT slower than a locally-acknowledged write. PACELC makes this cost explicit; CAP does not.

**Q7. What are the four possible PACELC classifications?**
A:
- **PA/EL**: Sacrifice consistency during partitions (stay available) and sacrifice consistency during normal operation (stay low latency). The "full AP" choice — maximize availability and performance at the cost of consistency guarantees. Example: Cassandra, DynamoDB (default), Riak.
- **PC/EC**: Sacrifice availability during partitions (stay consistent) and pay latency during normal operation (stay consistent). The "full CP" choice — never serve stale data, accept higher latency and possible unavailability. Example: HBase, etcd, Zookeeper, VoltDB, CockroachDB.
- **PA/EC**: Available during partitions but pay latency for consistency in normal operation. Unusual combination — optimizes for surviving partitions at the cost of latency when healthy. Example: Megastore (Google), early PNUTS (Yahoo).
- **PC/EL**: Consistent during partitions (block rather than serve stale data) but low latency in normal operation. Achieved by routing reads to nearest replica in normal operation (accepting possible staleness there) while enforcing consistency during partitions. Example: MongoDB in some configurations (primary-based writes = PC, secondary reads = EL).

**Q8. Is the "C" in PACELC the same as the "C" in CAP or ACID?**
A: No — and this is a frequent source of confusion:
- **CAP's C** = linearizability specifically: operations appear instantaneous and globally ordered.
- **PACELC's C** = a consistency *level* on a spectrum. Abadi intentionally left it general — the trade-off exists at every level from linearizability down to causal or read-your-writes consistency. PACELC is saying: any consistency guarantee costs latency; stronger guarantees cost more latency. You can be "EC" at causal consistency (cheaper) or "EC" at linearizability (expensive).
- **ACID's C** = application-level correctness constraints (referential integrity, check constraints) — a completely different concept; this is about your data obeying your schema rules, not about distributed ordering.

## Section 3: The Partition Side — PA vs. PC

**Q9. What does it mean for a system to choose PA (availability during partition)?**
A: The system continues accepting reads and writes on all reachable nodes even when partitioned. Different parts of the system may diverge — Node A accepts writes with value X while Node B (partitioned from A) accepts writes with value Y. The system returns a response (possibly stale) rather than an error. When the partition heals, diverged states must be reconciled — typically via last-write-wins, CRDTs, or application-level merge logic. The user gets high availability but may observe inconsistent data during the partition window.

**Q10. What does it mean for a system to choose PC (consistency during partition)?**
A: The system refuses to serve requests it cannot satisfy consistently. During a partition, a node that cannot communicate with enough peers (a quorum or the leader) will return an error or simply block. No stale data is ever served; no divergence occurs. The cost is that users on the minority side of a partition see failures until connectivity is restored. This is the right choice when serving stale data would be worse than an error — e.g., a bank balance, a unique username registry, or a distributed lock.

**Q11. Is "partition tolerance" actually a choice, or is it always required?**
A: Always required in any real distributed system. Networks drop packets, switches fail, and VMs restart — treating the network as reliable is the first fallacy of distributed computing. CAP's framing ("choose two of three") is slightly misleading: P is not optional, so the real choice is always between A and C *during a partition*. PACELC makes this explicit by stating the partition scenario as a condition ("if P") and adding the non-partition scenario separately.

## Section 4: The Else Side — EL vs. EC

**Q12. Give a concrete example of the EL vs. EC trade-off in a read operation.**
A: Consider a 3-node Cassandra cluster replicated across two data centers. Two ways to read:
- **EL (Consistency Level: LOCAL_ONE)**: Read from one local replica — sub-millisecond response, but that replica might be behind on recent writes; you may read stale data.
- **EC (Consistency Level: QUORUM)**: Read from 2 of 3 nodes, take the most recent value — guaranteed to see the latest committed write, but now you've added a network round-trip to the slower replica, and if one node is degraded, the read is slower still.

Both reads succeed (no partition). The only difference is the consistency guarantee — and the cost is entirely in latency and tail latency sensitivity. This trade-off happens on every single request; it has nothing to do with partitions.

**Q13. Give a concrete example of the EL vs. EC trade-off in a write operation.**
A: Same cluster, two write strategies:
- **EL (async replication)**: Write to the local node, acknowledge immediately to the client, replicate to other nodes in the background. Client gets a response in ~1ms; replicas may lag by 10–100ms. A reader on another node during that window sees the old value.
- **EC (synchronous quorum write)**: Write to the local node and wait for at least one remote replica to confirm before acknowledging. Client waits for the remote RTT (~70ms cross-region). Consistent — any subsequent quorum read is guaranteed to see the write.

The data is the same. The trade-off is: how much latency is the client willing to pay to be sure the write is durable and visible everywhere?

**Q14. What is "replication lag" in PACELC terms?**
A: Replication lag is the EL trade-off made visible: when a system chooses low latency (EL), it acknowledges writes before all replicas are updated. The gap between when the primary commits and when replicas apply the same write is the lag. During this window, a reader on a lagging replica sees stale data — even though the system is fully healthy with no partition. PACELC names this: the system is trading consistency (EC) for latency (EL). CAP would not even classify this scenario as a consistency violation, since no partition occurred.

**Q15. What consistency levels exist between "full linearizability" and "eventual consistency"?**
A: From strongest to weakest, the main levels on the spectrum:
- **Linearizability**: Every operation appears instantaneous; reads always reflect the latest completed write globally.
- **Sequential consistency**: All operations are globally ordered, but not necessarily in real-time; a read may not reflect a concurrent write that completed in wall-clock time.
- **Causal consistency**: Operations that are causally related (A happened before B, or A caused B) are seen in order by all nodes. Concurrent operations may be seen in different orders by different nodes.
- **Read-your-writes**: A client always sees its own most recent writes, but may see stale data from other clients.
- **Monotonic reads**: A client never observes a value older than one it has already read — the "clock doesn't go backward" for reads.
- **Eventual consistency**: Given no new writes, all replicas converge to the same value. No ordering guarantee is made during active writes.

Each level weaker than linearizability requires less coordination → less latency. PACELC's EL vs EC maps to this spectrum: EL systems pick something weaker than linearizability; EC systems target linearizability or sequential consistency.

**Q16. Is "low latency" the same as "high throughput"? How do they relate in the EL vs EC trade-off?**
A: Related but distinct. Latency is how long a single operation takes; throughput is how many operations the system handles per unit time. In the EL vs EC context:
- **EC (synchronous coordination)** hurts both: each operation waits for remote acknowledgment (higher latency per op), and the coordination overhead limits how many operations can be in flight concurrently (lower throughput).
- **EL (async replication)** helps both: writes acknowledge immediately (low latency per op), and many writes can be pipelined without waiting for replication (higher throughput).

This double benefit of EL is why high-throughput systems (Cassandra, DynamoDB at scale) almost universally choose EL — the latency win and throughput win compound. The EC systems pay this double cost in exchange for consistency guarantees.

## Section 5: Classifying Real Systems

**Q17. Where does Cassandra fall in PACELC, and how does its tunable consistency complicate the classification?**
A: Cassandra's default and design intent is **PA/EL** — available during partitions, low latency (local replica reads) in normal operation. But Cassandra's consistency level is tunable per-operation:
- `LOCAL_ONE` / `ONE`: PA/EL — fastest, weakest consistency.
- `QUORUM` / `LOCAL_QUORUM`: Closer to PA/EC — still available-by-preference, but pays quorum latency.
- `ALL`: Effectively PC/EC — consistent but unavailable if any node is down.

The classification isn't static per system; it's per-request in Cassandra. The reason Cassandra is still labeled PA/EL is that its architecture, default settings, and operational philosophy optimize for that quadrant. The stronger levels are opt-in overrides, not the baseline.

**Q18. Where does DynamoDB fall in PACELC?**
A: **PA/EL** by default. DynamoDB's eventually consistent reads (the default) are served from any of the replica nodes without quorum — lowest latency, possible stale reads. Its strongly consistent reads (`ConsistentRead: true`) add one synchronous round-trip to the leader, trading EL for EC on that specific read. On the partition side, DynamoDB prioritizes availability — it accepts writes on multiple nodes during partitions and reconciles with LWW (last-write-wins on timestamp) on healing, a PA choice. Overall classification: PA/EL with an opt-in EC path for reads.

**Q19. Where does ZooKeeper fall in PACELC?**
A: **PC/EC**. ZooKeeper uses ZAB (ZooKeeper Atomic Broadcast), a Paxos-variant consensus protocol. Every write goes through a leader and is replicated to a quorum of followers synchronously before being acknowledged — that's EC (consistency costs latency). During a partition, ZooKeeper will refuse to serve writes (and by default reads) if it cannot establish a quorum — that's PC (consistency over availability). It will return an error or block rather than serve potentially stale data. This is the right behavior for ZooKeeper's use cases: distributed locks, leader election, configuration management — all require that you never see stale state.

**Q20. Where does MongoDB fall in PACELC, and why is it a nuanced answer?**
A: **PC/EL** in typical replica-set configuration, with important nuance. On the partition side, MongoDB is PC: the primary-based architecture means that if a node can't reach the primary (or enough nodes to elect one), it stops accepting writes and returns an error — it won't serve potentially stale writes. On the else side, it's EL: reads can be routed to the nearest secondary replica (`readPreference: nearest`), which may lag behind the primary — low latency, but not guaranteed up-to-date (EL). The nuance: with `readConcern: linearizable` and `writeConcern: majority`, MongoDB becomes PC/EC for those operations — at significant latency cost.

**Q21. Where does CockroachDB / Google Spanner fall in PACELC?**
A: **PC/EC** for both. Both use distributed consensus (Raft in CockroachDB, Paxos in Spanner) for every write — all replicas must agree before the write is acknowledged. This is EC: consistency costs real latency. During partitions, they maintain consistency (PC) by refusing to commit writes that can't achieve quorum. Spanner goes further with TrueTime — it adds a deliberate `commit wait` delay (1–7ms) to ensure no transaction is assigned a timestamp in the ambiguous TrueTime interval, paying additional latency for external consistency (the strongest form of EC). Both systems explicitly trade latency for consistency as a first-class design choice.

**Q22. Where does etcd fall in PACELC?**
A: **PC/EC**. etcd uses Raft consensus — every write requires a quorum of nodes to agree. Reads with `linearizable` option (the default for sensitive operations) go through the leader, ensuring you see the latest committed value. During partitions, the minority partition stops serving writes entirely. etcd is designed for infrastructure coordination (Kubernetes cluster state, distributed locks, service discovery) — correctness is non-negotiable, and its PACELC classification reflects that. The latency cost (multiple round trips per operation, cross-AZ or cross-region if deployed that way) is accepted as the price of correctness.

## Section 6: Trade-offs & Decision-Making

**Q23. When should you explicitly choose PA/EL?**
A:
- **High-volume, read-heavy systems** where milliseconds matter and occasional stale reads are acceptable — analytics dashboards, social media feeds, product catalog lookups.
- **Global user bases** where routing writes to a single consistent primary would add unacceptable latency for users far from the primary region.
- **Counters and accumulations** where approximate values are acceptable (page views, like counts) — a brief period of stale counts doesn't harm the user experience.
- **Systems that can resolve conflicts at the application layer** — shopping carts (CRDT-style merge), user preference syncing (last-write-wins is fine), document collaboration (operational transforms).
- **When availability SLA outweighs consistency SLA** — "always respond" is more important than "always be right."

**Q24. When should you explicitly choose PC/EC?**
A:
- **Financial transactions**: A balance read must reflect the current true balance; serving stale data risks overdrafts or double-spends.
- **Unique constraint enforcement**: Username registration, seat booking, inventory reservation — two nodes must not both grant the same unique resource.
- **Distributed coordination**: Leader election, distributed locks, cluster membership — decisions made on stale state can cause split-brain.
- **Audit logs and compliance**: Regulatory requirements may demand that the system can prove the exact order of operations with no ambiguity.
- **Configuration management**: Services reading their configuration must not get a stale value that was already updated — acting on old config can cause cascading failures.

**Q25. PACELC shows four quadrants. Why is PA/EC rare?**
A: PA/EC means: stay available during partitions (don't block) but pay latency for consistency during normal operation. This is an unusual combination because the two choices work against each other philosophically. During a partition you're serving potentially inconsistent data (by choosing A over C), but in normal operation you're paying the coordination overhead to be consistent. You've accepted the worst of both worlds in a sense: the operational latency cost of coordination without the actual consistency guarantee when it matters most (under partition). Systems like Google's Megastore fit here — they use synchronous cross-region replication in normal operation for consistency but fail toward availability (serving the local, potentially stale copy) when cross-region connectivity is lost. It's a valid choice for specific geographic consistency patterns, but uncommon precisely because it's hard to justify the latency cost when you're going to sacrifice consistency during the hardest failures anyway.

**Q26. How does PACELC help you have a more precise conversation about consistency than CAP?**
A: CAP forces a binary label — "CP system" or "AP system" — which is usually misleading. Real systems:
- Are often tunable (the same system can behave differently per operation)
- Always operate mostly in the "else" (no-partition) case, which CAP ignores
- Trade off on a *spectrum* of consistency levels, not a binary

PACELC gives you a two-dimensional label (partition behavior × normal-operation behavior) and explicitly names latency as the resource being traded against consistency. Instead of "we chose AP because availability matters," you can say "we chose PA/EL because we need sub-10ms p99 reads globally and can tolerate eventual consistency with a max 100ms replication lag." That's an engineering decision, not a philosophy.

## Section 7: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q27. "You're designing a distributed inventory system for an e-commerce flash sale. 10,000 requests per second want to decrement stock. Which PACELC quadrant do you target and why?"**
A: **PC/EC** — and this is non-negotiable for inventory. The core invariant ("don't sell what you don't have") requires that every decrement sees the true current stock level. With PA/EL, two nodes could both serve "1 item left" and both decrement it, resulting in -1 inventory (oversell). No amount of eventual reconciliation undoes the fact that you've promised an item you can't ship.

The implementation: use a strongly consistent data store (etcd, Postgres with serializable isolation, DynamoDB with conditional writes and `ConsistentRead`) for the inventory counter. Accept that this costs latency and potential availability — protect the invariant first. For the flash sale throughput problem, the answer is queuing and rate limiting *before* the inventory write, not weakening consistency on the write itself.

**Q28. "Your team is debating whether to use Cassandra (PA/EL) or CockroachDB (PC/EC) for a multi-region user profile service. How do you frame the decision using PACELC?"**
A: The right question is: "What consistency level does a user profile actually require, and what's the cost of getting it wrong?"

For most profile fields (display name, avatar, bio, preferences):
- Stale reads are harmless — seeing a 200ms-old avatar is imperceptible.
- Writes are infrequent and rarely conflicting.
- Low-latency reads matter because profiles are fetched on every page load.
→ PA/EL (Cassandra) is appropriate. The EL benefit (local replica reads, sub-5ms p99) outweighs the EC cost.

For profile fields with uniqueness constraints (email address, username):
- Two nodes must not both approve the same username concurrently.
→ These specific operations need PC/EC guarantees.

The practical answer: use Cassandra (PA/EL) for the profile data itself, and a separate PC/EC store (Postgres, etcd) for uniqueness enforcement. Don't let the needs of one field force the entire service into a higher-cost consistency tier.

**Q29. "You've chosen a PA/EL database for a social feed. Users report that after posting, they refresh and don't see their own post. What's the name of this problem and how do you fix it without changing your PACELC quadrant?"**
A: This is a **read-your-own-writes** (or read-your-writes) violation — a well-known class of eventual consistency anomaly. The user writes to the primary node, then reads from a replica that hasn't yet received the replication. The fix does not require switching to EC:

Options within the PA/EL tier:
- **Sticky reads after writes**: For a short window (e.g., 1 second) after the user writes, route their reads to the same node they wrote to — they're guaranteed to see their own write. Only their reads are affected; global consistency is unchanged.
- **Client-side optimistic update**: Update the UI immediately on the client before the server confirms, using the locally known value. The "read" is served from client state, not the database.
- **Write tokens**: Return a version token (or timestamp) with the write; include it in subsequent read requests. If the read replica hasn't caught up to that token, route to the primary instead.

All three preserve the system's PA/EL overall classification — only specific reads by specific users are temporarily routed differently, not the entire system.

**Q30. "A senior engineer says: 'CAP is enough — I don't need PACELC.' How do you respond?"**
A: CAP is necessary but insufficient for most real-world system design conversations. Three concrete gaps you can raise:

First, CAP only helps you answer "what happens during a partition" — a rare event. PACELC forces you to also answer "what are you trading off right now, in normal operation, on every single request?" That's the trade-off that affects users daily.

Second, CAP says nothing about latency — the metric your SLA, your users, and your business most directly feel. A system with 500ms p99 and a system with 5ms p99 can both be "consistent" in CAP terms; PACELC is what makes the cost visible and discussable.

Third, CAP's binary C-or-A framing papers over the consistency spectrum. In a real design review, "we chose AP" conveys almost nothing — it doesn't say whether you're targeting causal consistency, read-your-writes, or pure eventual. PACELC's framing (EL vs. EC, with EC covering any point on the spectrum) at least surfaces the question of *what level* you're targeting and *what you're paying for it*.

CAP is a useful teaching tool. PACELC is what you actually use to make engineering decisions.

## The Core Mental Model

```
CAP asks:   "What do you do when the network breaks?"
PACELC asks: "What do you do when the network breaks — AND what are you trading every day when it doesn't?"

PA/EL → Optimize for "always respond fast"  (Cassandra, Dynamo)
PC/EC → Optimize for "always be right"      (etcd, Spanner, CockroachDB)
PA/EC → Available under partition, pay latency when healthy   (Megastore)
PC/EL → Block under partition, read local when healthy        (MongoDB)

The consistency axis is a spectrum:
Linearizability > Sequential > Causal > Read-your-writes > Monotonic reads > Eventual

EC doesn't mean "fully linearizable" — it means "some consistency guarantee that costs latency."
EL doesn't mean "no consistency" — it means "consistency level that doesn't require coordination."
```

---

**Tip for the interview:** CAP is what the interviewer expects you to know. PACELC is what signals you've thought about distributed systems in production. The strongest 5 YOE answer makes three moves: (1) acknowledge CAP and its value, (2) call out its blind spot — latency and the non-partition case — and introduce PACELC, (3) apply PACELC to the specific system in the question by naming the quadrant and explaining *why* the business requirement maps to that quadrant. If you can connect it to a tunable system like Cassandra — "the consistency level is a per-request choice between EL and EC, and we set it based on the operation's tolerance for staleness" — you've shown you understand the theory *and* how it operates in practice.
