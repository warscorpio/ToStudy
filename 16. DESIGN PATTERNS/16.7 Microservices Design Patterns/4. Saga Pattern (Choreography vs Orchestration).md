# Microservices Design Patterns: Saga Pattern (Choreography vs Orchestration) — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is the Saga pattern?**
A: The Saga pattern is a way to manage distributed transactions across multiple microservices without relying on a two-phase commit (2PC) protocol. A saga is a sequence of local transactions — each service performs its own local database transaction, then publishes an event or sends a command to trigger the next step. If any step fails, the saga executes a series of *compensating transactions* in reverse order to undo the effects of the steps that already succeeded, restoring the system to a consistent state. The term "saga" comes from a 1987 paper by Hector Garcia-Molina and Kenneth Salem, and was popularised in the microservices context by Chris Richardson. The core trade-off: sagas achieve *eventual consistency* rather than the strict ACID isolation of a 2PC transaction. Each local transaction is immediately visible to other operations, which means other requests can read intermediate (partially committed) saga states.

**Q2. What problem does the Saga pattern solve, and why is 2PC not the answer in microservices?**
A: The fundamental problem: a business operation that must atomically update data owned by multiple services. In a fintech loan disbursement flow, for example, the operation must: deduct from the funding ledger, create the loan record, update the customer's account, generate an EMI schedule, and notify the customer — each owned by a different service with its own database. Without coordination, any step can succeed while a later step fails, leaving the system in an inconsistent state.

**Why 2PC fails in microservices:**
- **Tight coupling** — 2PC requires a distributed transaction coordinator that all participants must register with and respond to. Every service must implement the 2PC protocol, coupling them to a shared coordinator.
- **Blocking protocol** — during the "prepare" phase, all participants hold locks on their resources until the coordinator issues a "commit" or "abort". A slow or crashed participant blocks all others, reducing availability.
- **Single point of failure** — the coordinator is a SPOF. If it crashes between "prepare" and "commit", participants may hold locks indefinitely.
- **Incompatibility** — many modern data stores (DynamoDB, Cassandra, Kafka, Redis) don't support the XA protocol that 2PC requires. Sagas work with any data store that can perform a local transaction.
- **Scale** — holding locks across network boundaries in a high-throughput system is a recipe for latency and deadlocks.

Sagas trade isolation for availability: each local transaction commits immediately (releasing locks), and rollback is achieved through compensating transactions rather than aborting an in-progress lock-holding transaction.

**Q3. What is a compensating transaction, and what makes it different from a simple rollback?**
A: A compensating transaction is a new forward transaction that semantically reverses the effect of a previously committed local transaction. It is fundamentally different from a database rollback:

- A **database rollback** undoes uncommitted changes within a single transaction. It is atomic, invisible to other readers, and leaves no trace in the database log.
- A **compensating transaction** is a new, separately committed transaction that reverses an *already committed* change. It is visible — any operation that read the intermediate state sees both the original change and the compensation. It leaves an audit trail.

Examples of compensating transactions in a fintech context:

| Step                                    | Original Transaction               | Compensating Transaction                        |
|-----------------------------------------|------------------------------------|-------------------------------------------------|
| Debit customer account for EMI          | `UPDATE balance -= emi_amount`     | `UPDATE balance += emi_amount` (credit back)    |
| Reserve loan disbursement amount        | `INSERT INTO reservations (...)`   | `DELETE FROM reservations WHERE ...`            |
| Create loan record                      | `INSERT INTO loans (...)`          | `UPDATE loans SET status = 'CANCELLED' WHERE …` |
| Send disbursement to NACH               | NACH debit submitted               | Submit NACH reversal / refund request           |
| Publish loan-approved Kafka event       | Event published to Kafka           | Publish loan-cancelled event (cannot un-publish)|

The last row illustrates an important constraint: some steps are *non-compensatable* — a published message, a sent email, or a submitted government filing cannot be unsent. These steps must be placed at the end of the saga where compensation is least likely to be needed, or must accept the consequence of idempotent re-processing.

**Q4. What are the two implementation strategies for sagas — Choreography and Orchestration — and what is the fundamental difference between them?**
A: The difference is in *who* controls the saga's flow and *how* services are coordinated:

**Choreography:**
- There is no central coordinator. Each service listens for events and reacts by performing its local transaction and publishing its own event.
- The saga's flow emerges from the chain of events — each service knows what to do when it sees a certain event, but no service has a global view of the saga.
- Coordination is distributed and implicit — encoded in each service's event subscriptions and event publications.
- Analogy: a jazz ensemble — each musician listens to the others and plays their part without a conductor directing every note.

**Orchestration:**
- A dedicated orchestrator service (or process) drives the saga. It sends commands to each participant service, waits for responses, and decides what to do next.
- The saga's flow is explicit and centralised — the orchestrator has the complete state machine and knows at all times which step the saga is on.
- Coordination is centralised and explicit — the orchestrator is the single source of truth for the saga's progress.
- Analogy: a conductor directing an orchestra — each musician plays when told, and the conductor decides the sequence, tempo, and error handling.

**Q5. What are the trade-offs between Choreography and Orchestration?**
A:

| Dimension                   | Choreography                                                               | Orchestration                                                                |
|-----------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Coupling                    | Loose — services only know about events, not about each other              | Tighter — orchestrator knows and depends on all participant services         |
| Central control             | None — flow is emergent from event chains                                  | Full — orchestrator owns the state machine                                   |
| Observability               | Hard — must correlate events across services to understand saga state      | Easy — orchestrator holds the complete saga state                            |
| Debugging                   | Difficult — "what step is the saga on?" requires event log correlation     | Easy — query the orchestrator's state table                                  |
| Testing                     | Hard — must simulate event chains end-to-end                               | Easier — orchestrator can be unit tested with mocked participants            |
| Cyclic dependencies         | Risk — Service A listens to Service B, B listens to A → hidden cycles      | Lower risk — orchestrator is the explicit hub                               |
| Single point of failure     | None — failure of one service doesn't stop others from consuming events    | Yes — orchestrator failure halts all in-flight sagas (mitigated with HA)    |
| Scalability of saga logic   | Good — each service scales independently                                   | Orchestrator is a bottleneck if it becomes complex                           |
| Adding a new step           | Touch both the preceding service (publish new event) and the new service   | Touch only the orchestrator (add a step) and the new service (add listener) |
| Where to use                | Simple, linear, stable flows with few participants                         | Complex, multi-step, conditional flows with many participants                |

At 5 YOE, the expected answer goes beyond listing these — it includes knowing that most production fintech systems with complex business flows (loan disbursement, KYC + credit check + account activation) use orchestration because debuggability and explicit state are critical in regulated environments, while choreography is appropriate for simpler, well-understood event chains (order placed → inventory reserved → shipping notified).

## Section 2: Choreography Implementation

**Q6. Walk through a complete choreography-based saga for a loan EMI payment flow.**
A:

```
EMI Payment Saga — Choreography

Step 1: Customer initiates payment → Payment Service
Step 2: Payment Service charges via UPI → publishes PaymentSucceeded
Step 3: Loan Service receives PaymentSucceeded → updates loan balance → publishes EmiRecorded
Step 4: Ledger Service receives EmiRecorded → creates journal entries → publishes LedgerUpdated
Step 5: Notification Service receives LedgerUpdated → sends payment confirmation SMS

Compensation chain (if Step 3 fails):
  LoanService publishes PaymentReversal
  Payment Service receives PaymentReversal → initiates UPI refund
  Notification Service receives PaymentReversal → sends failure SMS
```

```typescript
// src/payment-service/emi-payment.handler.ts
@Injectable()
export class EmiPaymentInitiationHandler {
  constructor(
    private readonly upiGateway:  UpiGatewayPort,
    private readonly paymentDao:  IPaymentDao,
    private readonly publisher:   IEventPublisher,
  ) {}

  async handlePaymentRequest(command: InitiateEmiPaymentCommand): Promise<void> {
    // Idempotency check — prevent duplicate processing
    const existing = await this.paymentDao.findByIdempotencyKey(command.idempotencyKey);
    if (existing) {
      // Already processed — republish the outcome event so downstream can reprocess
      await this.republishOutcome(existing);
      return;
    }

    const sagaId = command.sagaId ?? crypto.randomUUID();

    try {
      // Step 1: Charge via UPI — local transaction
      const result = await this.upiGateway.charge({
        vpa:             command.upiVpa,
        amountInPaise:   command.amountInPaise,
        idempotencyKey:  command.idempotencyKey,
      });

      // Record in payment DB — local transaction
      await this.paymentDao.insert({
        sagaId,
        loanId:          command.loanId,
        customerId:      command.customerId,
        amountInPaise:   command.amountInPaise,
        gatewayRef:      result.gatewayRef,
        status:          "SUCCESS",
        idempotencyKey:  command.idempotencyKey,
      });

      // Publish success event — triggers Loan Service
      await this.publisher.publish<PaymentSucceeded>({
        eventType:     "payment.succeeded",
        sagaId,
        loanId:        command.loanId,
        customerId:    command.customerId,
        amountInPaise: command.amountInPaise,
        gatewayRef:    result.gatewayRef,
        occurredAt:    new Date().toISOString(),
      });
    } catch (err: any) {
      await this.paymentDao.insert({
        sagaId, loanId: command.loanId, customerId: command.customerId,
        amountInPaise: command.amountInPaise, status: "FAILED",
        idempotencyKey: command.idempotencyKey, failureReason: err.message,
      });

      // Publish failure event — no compensation needed; nothing succeeded yet
      await this.publisher.publish<PaymentFailed>({
        eventType:  "payment.failed",
        sagaId,
        loanId:     command.loanId,
        customerId: command.customerId,
        reason:     err.message,
        occurredAt: new Date().toISOString(),
      });
    }
  }
}

// ─── Loan Service — reacts to PaymentSucceeded, performs second local transaction

// src/loan-service/emi-recorder.handler.ts
@Injectable()
export class EmiRecorderHandler {
  @EventPattern("payment.succeeded")
  async handlePaymentSucceeded(@Payload() event: PaymentSucceeded): Promise<void> {
    // Idempotency: if already recorded for this sagaId, skip
    const alreadyRecorded = await this.loanDao.existsEmiForSaga(event.sagaId);
    if (alreadyRecorded) return;

    try {
      const loan = await this.loanDao.findById(event.loanId);
      if (!loan) throw new Error(`Loan ${event.loanId} not found`);

      // Local domain operation
      loan.repayEmi(Money.ofPaise(event.amountInPaise));

      // Local transaction — commit updated loan state
      await this.loanDao.save(loan);
      await this.emiDao.insert({
        sagaId:         event.sagaId,
        loanId:         event.loanId,
        emiNumber:      loan.emisPaid,
        amountInPaise:  event.amountInPaise,
        gatewayRef:     event.gatewayRef,
        recordedAt:     new Date(),
      });

      // Publish success → triggers Ledger Service
      await this.publisher.publish<EmiRecorded>({
        eventType:             "emi.recorded",
        sagaId:                event.sagaId,
        loanId:                event.loanId,
        customerId:            event.customerId,
        emiNumber:             loan.emisPaid,
        amountInPaise:         event.amountInPaise,
        outstandingAfterPaise: loan.outstandingPrincipal.amountInPaise,
        gatewayRef:            event.gatewayRef,
        occurredAt:            new Date().toISOString(),
      });
    } catch (err: any) {
      // Compensation: tell Payment Service to refund
      await this.publisher.publish<EmiRecordingFailed>({
        eventType:  "emi.recording.failed",
        sagaId:     event.sagaId,
        loanId:     event.loanId,
        gatewayRef: event.gatewayRef,   // Payment Service needs this to refund
        reason:     err.message,
        occurredAt: new Date().toISOString(),
      });
    }
  }
}

// ─── Payment Service — compensating transaction listener ─────────────────────

// src/payment-service/refund.handler.ts
@Injectable()
export class PaymentRefundHandler {
  @EventPattern("emi.recording.failed")
  async handleEmiRecordingFailed(@Payload() event: EmiRecordingFailed): Promise<void> {
    // Compensating transaction: refund the UPI charge
    try {
      await this.upiGateway.refund({ gatewayRef: event.gatewayRef });

      await this.paymentDao.updateStatus(
        event.sagaId, "REFUNDED",
      );

      await this.publisher.publish<PaymentRefundInitiated>({
        eventType:  "payment.refund.initiated",
        sagaId:     event.sagaId,
        loanId:     event.loanId,
        customerId: (await this.paymentDao.findBySagaId(event.sagaId))!.customerId,
        gatewayRef: event.gatewayRef,
        occurredAt: new Date().toISOString(),
      });
    } catch (refundErr: any) {
      // Refund itself failed — requires manual intervention
      await this.alertService.escalate({
        sagaId:   event.sagaId,
        type:     "REFUND_FAILED",
        message:  `Refund failed for gatewayRef ${event.gatewayRef}: ${refundErr.message}`,
      });
    }
  }
}
```

**Q7. How do you ensure idempotency in a choreography-based saga — what happens when an event is delivered more than once?**
A: Message brokers like Kafka guarantee *at-least-once* delivery — the same event can arrive multiple times due to retries, consumer group rebalances, or producer retries. Every saga step must be idempotent:

```typescript
// ─── Idempotency strategy: saga-step tracking table ──────────────────────────

// CREATE TABLE saga_step_outcomes (
//   saga_id        UUID NOT NULL,
//   step_name      VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
//   status         VARCHAR(20) NOT NULL,   -- 'COMPLETED' | 'COMPENSATED' | 'FAILED'
//   processed_at   TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
//   PRIMARY KEY (saga_id, step_name)
// );

@Injectable()
export class IdempotentSagaStepExecutor {
  constructor(
    private readonly db:        DataSource,
    private readonly publisher: IEventPublisher,
  ) {}

  async executeStep<T>(params: {
    sagaId:    string;
    stepName:  string;
    work:      () => Promise<T>;
    onComplete: (result: T) => Promise<void>;  // publish success event
  }): Promise<void> {
    // Atomic check-and-insert: try to claim this step
    const claimed = await this.db.transaction(async (manager) => {
      const existing = await manager.query(
        `SELECT status FROM saga_step_outcomes
         WHERE saga_id = $1 AND step_name = $2`,
        [params.sagaId, params.stepName],
      );

      if (existing.length > 0) {
        // Step already completed — skip duplicate processing
        return false;
      }

      await manager.query(
        `INSERT INTO saga_step_outcomes (saga_id, step_name, status, processed_at)
         VALUES ($1, $2, 'IN_PROGRESS', NOW())`,
        [params.sagaId, params.stepName],
      );
      return true;
    });

    if (!claimed) {
      // Already processed — idempotent: do nothing
      return;
    }

    try {
      const result = await params.work();

      await this.db.query(
        `UPDATE saga_step_outcomes SET status = 'COMPLETED'
         WHERE saga_id = $1 AND step_name = $2`,
        [params.sagaId, params.stepName],
      );

      await params.onComplete(result);
    } catch (err) {
      await this.db.query(
        `UPDATE saga_step_outcomes SET status = 'FAILED'
         WHERE saga_id = $1 AND step_name = $2`,
        [params.sagaId, params.stepName],
      );
      throw err;
    }
  }
}

// ─── Usage in EmiRecorderHandler ──────────────────────────────────────────────

@EventPattern("payment.succeeded")
async handlePaymentSucceeded(@Payload() event: PaymentSucceeded): Promise<void> {
  await this.idempotentExecutor.executeStep({
    sagaId:    event.sagaId,
    stepName:  "record-emi",
    work:      async () => {
      const loan = await this.loanDao.findById(event.loanId);
      loan.repayEmi(Money.ofPaise(event.amountInPaise));
      await this.loanDao.save(loan);
      return loan;
    },
    onComplete: async (loan) => {
      await this.publisher.publish<EmiRecorded>({
        eventType: "emi.recorded",
        sagaId:    event.sagaId,
        // ...
      });
    },
  });
}
```

## Section 3: Orchestration Implementation

**Q8. Walk through a complete orchestration-based saga for a loan disbursement flow.**
A:

```
Loan Disbursement Saga — Orchestration

Orchestrator steps:
1. → Credit Bureau Service: pull CIBIL report
2. → Loan Service: approve loan (if score sufficient)
3. → Account Service: create disbursement account
4. → Ledger Service: debit funding pool, credit customer account
5. → NACH Service: set up NACH mandate for EMI collection
6. → Notification Service: send disbursement SMS + email

Compensation (if step 4 fails):
  Compensate step 3: cancel disbursement account
  Compensate step 2: mark loan as CANCELLED (step 1 is idempotent — no compensation needed)
```

```typescript
// ─── Saga state ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

// src/saga/loan-disbursement/loan-disbursement.state.ts

export type LoanDisbursementSagaStatus =
  | "STARTED"
  | "CREDIT_PULLED"
  | "LOAN_APPROVED"
  | "ACCOUNT_CREATED"
  | "LEDGER_UPDATED"
  | "MANDATE_CREATED"
  | "COMPLETED"
  | "COMPENSATING"
  | "COMPENSATED"
  | "FAILED";

export interface LoanDisbursementSagaState {
  sagaId:             string;
  loanId:             string;
  customerId:         string;
  principalInPaise:   number;
  tenureMonths:       number;

  // Populated as saga progresses
  cibilScore?:        number;
  approvedRate?:      number;
  disbursementAccountId?: string;
  ledgerEntryId?:     string;
  nachMandateId?:     string;

  status:             LoanDisbursementSagaStatus;
  currentStep:        number;
  failedStep?:        number;
  failureReason?:     string;
  compensatingFrom?:  number;

  createdAt:          Date;
  updatedAt:          Date;
}

// ─── Saga orchestrator ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

// src/saga/loan-disbursement/loan-disbursement.orchestrator.ts

@Injectable()
export class LoanDisbursementOrchestrator {
  private readonly logger = new Logger(LoanDisbursementOrchestrator.name);

  constructor(
    private readonly sagaRepo:       ISagaRepository<LoanDisbursementSagaState>,
    private readonly creditBureau:   ICreditBureauPort,
    private readonly loanService:    ILoanServicePort,
    private readonly accountService: IAccountServicePort,
    private readonly ledgerService:  ILedgerServicePort,
    private readonly nachService:    INachServicePort,
    private readonly notifService:   INotificationServicePort,
    private readonly publisher:      IEventPublisher,
    private readonly alertService:   IAlertService,
  ) {}

  // ── Entry point ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  async start(command: StartLoanDisbursementCommand): Promise<string> {
    const sagaId = crypto.randomUUID();

    const state: LoanDisbursementSagaState = {
      sagaId,
      loanId:          command.loanId,
      customerId:      command.customerId,
      principalInPaise: command.principalInPaise,
      tenureMonths:    command.tenureMonths,
      status:          "STARTED",
      currentStep:     0,
      createdAt:       new Date(),
      updatedAt:       new Date(),
    };

    await this.sagaRepo.save(state);
    await this.executeStep(state);
    return sagaId;
  }

  // ── Main execution driver — called on each step and on resume after failure ──

  private async executeStep(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    try {
      switch (state.currentStep) {
        case 0: await this.step1PullCreditReport(state); break;
        case 1: await this.step2ApproveLoan(state);       break;
        case 2: await this.step3CreateAccount(state);     break;
        case 3: await this.step4UpdateLedger(state);      break;
        case 4: await this.step5CreateNachMandate(state); break;
        case 5: await this.step6SendNotification(state);  break;
        default:
          await this.completeSaga(state);
      }
    } catch (err: any) {
      this.logger.error(`Saga ${state.sagaId} failed at step ${state.currentStep}: ${err.message}`);
      await this.beginCompensation(state, err.message);
    }
  }

  // ── Forward steps ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  private async step1PullCreditReport(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Step 1: Pull credit report`);

    const report = await this.creditBureau.pullReport(state.customerId);

    if (report.score < 650) {
      throw new Error(`CIBIL score ${report.score} below minimum threshold of 650`);
    }

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      cibilScore:   report.score,
      approvedRate: this.computeRate(report.score),
      status:       "CREDIT_PULLED",
      currentStep:  1,
      updatedAt:    new Date(),
    });

    await this.executeStep({ ...state, cibilScore: report.score, currentStep: 1 });
  }

  private async step2ApproveLoan(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Step 2: Approve loan`);

    await this.loanService.approve({
      loanId:           state.loanId,
      approvedRate:     state.approvedRate!,
      approvedBySystem: `saga:${state.sagaId}`,
    });

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      status:      "LOAN_APPROVED",
      currentStep: 2,
      updatedAt:   new Date(),
    });

    await this.executeStep({ ...state, currentStep: 2 });
  }

  private async step3CreateAccount(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Step 3: Create disbursement account`);

    const account = await this.accountService.createDisbursementAccount({
      customerId:     state.customerId,
      loanId:         state.loanId,
      amountInPaise:  state.principalInPaise,
    });

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      disbursementAccountId: account.accountId,
      status:                "ACCOUNT_CREATED",
      currentStep:           3,
      updatedAt:             new Date(),
    });

    await this.executeStep({ ...state, disbursementAccountId: account.accountId, currentStep: 3 });
  }

  private async step4UpdateLedger(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Step 4: Update ledger`);

    const entry = await this.ledgerService.disburse({
      fromAccount:   "LOAN_FUNDING_POOL",
      toAccount:     state.disbursementAccountId!,
      amountInPaise: state.principalInPaise,
      reference:     state.loanId,
    });

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      ledgerEntryId: entry.entryId,
      status:        "LEDGER_UPDATED",
      currentStep:   4,
      updatedAt:     new Date(),
    });

    await this.executeStep({ ...state, ledgerEntryId: entry.entryId, currentStep: 4 });
  }

  private async step5CreateNachMandate(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Step 5: Create NACH mandate`);

    const mandate = await this.nachService.createMandate({
      customerId:    state.customerId,
      loanId:        state.loanId,
      emiInPaise:    this.computeEmi(state.principalInPaise, state.approvedRate!, state.tenureMonths),
      tenureMonths:  state.tenureMonths,
    });

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      nachMandateId: mandate.mandateId,
      status:        "MANDATE_CREATED",
      currentStep:   5,
      updatedAt:     new Date(),
    });

    await this.executeStep({ ...state, nachMandateId: mandate.mandateId, currentStep: 5 });
  }

  private async step6SendNotification(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Step 6: Send notification`);

    // Notification is best-effort — non-compensatable — do it last
    await this.notifService.sendDisbursementConfirmation({
      customerId:     state.customerId,
      loanId:         state.loanId,
      amountInPaise:  state.principalInPaise,
    });

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      status:      "COMPLETED",
      currentStep: 6,
      updatedAt:   new Date(),
    });

    // Publish saga-completed event for downstream consumers
    await this.publisher.publish({
      eventType:     "loan.disbursement.completed",
      sagaId:        state.sagaId,
      loanId:        state.loanId,
      customerId:    state.customerId,
      amountInPaise: state.principalInPaise,
      occurredAt:    new Date().toISOString(),
    });

    this.logger.log(`[${state.sagaId}] Saga COMPLETED`);
  }

  // ── Compensation ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  private async beginCompensation(
    state: LoanDisbursementSagaState,
    reason: string,
  ): Promise<void> {
    const failedStep = state.currentStep;

    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      status:           "COMPENSATING",
      failedStep,
      failureReason:    reason,
      compensatingFrom: failedStep - 1,  // start compensating from the last SUCCESSFUL step
      updatedAt:        new Date(),
    });

    await this.compensateStep({ ...state, status: "COMPENSATING", compensatingFrom: failedStep - 1 });
  }

  private async compensateStep(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    const step = state.compensatingFrom!;

    this.logger.warn(`[${state.sagaId}] Compensating step ${step}`);

    try {
      switch (step) {
        case 4: await this.compensateStep5NachMandate(state); break;
        case 3: await this.compensateStep4Ledger(state);      break;
        case 2: await this.compensateStep3Account(state);     break;
        case 1: await this.compensateStep2LoanApproval(state); break;
        case 0: // Step 1 (credit pull) is idempotent — no compensation needed
        default:
          await this.finishCompensation(state);
          return;
      }

      // Move to the previous step's compensation
      await this.compensateStep({ ...state, compensatingFrom: step - 1 });
    } catch (compensationErr: any) {
      // Compensation failed — requires manual intervention
      this.logger.error(`[${state.sagaId}] Compensation of step ${step} failed: ${compensationErr.message}`);
      await this.alertService.escalate({
        sagaId:  state.sagaId,
        type:    "COMPENSATION_FAILED",
        step,
        message: compensationErr.message,
      });
    }
  }

  private async compensateStep5NachMandate(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    if (!state.nachMandateId) return;
    await this.nachService.cancelMandate(state.nachMandateId);
  }

  private async compensateStep4Ledger(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    if (!state.ledgerEntryId) return;
    await this.ledgerService.reverseEntry(state.ledgerEntryId, `Saga ${state.sagaId} compensation`);
  }

  private async compensateStep3Account(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    if (!state.disbursementAccountId) return;
    await this.accountService.cancelDisbursementAccount(state.disbursementAccountId);
  }

  private async compensateStep2LoanApproval(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    await this.loanService.cancel({
      loanId: state.loanId,
      reason: `Saga ${state.sagaId} compensation: ${state.failureReason}`,
    });
  }

  private async finishCompensation(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
      status:    "COMPENSATED",
      updatedAt: new Date(),
    });

    await this.publisher.publish({
      eventType:     "loan.disbursement.failed",
      sagaId:        state.sagaId,
      loanId:        state.loanId,
      customerId:    state.customerId,
      failureReason: state.failureReason,
      occurredAt:    new Date().toISOString(),
    });

    this.logger.warn(`[${state.sagaId}] Saga COMPENSATED — disbursement rolled back`);
  }

  // ── Helpers ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

  private computeRate(score: number): number {
    if (score >= 800) return 0.12;
    if (score >= 750) return 0.15;
    if (score >= 700) return 0.18;
    return 0.22;
  }

  private computeEmi(principal: number, rate: number, months: number): number {
    const r = rate / 12;
    return Math.round((principal * r * Math.pow(1 + r, months)) / (Math.pow(1 + r, months) - 1));
  }
}
```

**Q9. How do you persist saga state to survive orchestrator restarts and process crashes?**

```typescript
// src/saga/saga-repository.ts

// Saga state is stored in the database — not in memory.
// If the orchestrator crashes mid-saga, a recovery job resumes it from where it left off.

// CREATE TABLE saga_instances (
//   saga_id          UUID PRIMARY KEY,
//   saga_type        VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
//   status           VARCHAR(50)  NOT NULL,
//   current_step     INT          NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
//   state_json       JSONB        NOT NULL,
//   failure_reason   TEXT,
//   created_at       TIMESTAMP    NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
//   updated_at       TIMESTAMP    NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
//   next_retry_at    TIMESTAMP,
//   retry_count      INT          NOT NULL DEFAULT 0
// );

// CREATE INDEX idx_saga_status ON saga_instances(status) WHERE status IN ('STARTED', 'COMPENSATING');
// CREATE INDEX idx_saga_next_retry ON saga_instances(next_retry_at) WHERE next_retry_at IS NOT NULL;

@Injectable()
export class PostgresSagaRepository<T> implements ISagaRepository<T> {
  constructor(private readonly db: DataSource) {}

  async save(state: T & { sagaId: string; status: string }): Promise<void> {
    await this.db.query(
      `INSERT INTO saga_instances (saga_id, saga_type, status, current_step, state_json)
       VALUES ($1, $2, $3, $4, $5)`,
      [
        (state as any).sagaId,
        (state as any).constructor?.name ?? "SagaInstance",
        state.status,
        (state as any).currentStep ?? 0,
        JSON.stringify(state),
      ],
    );
  }

  async update(sagaId: string, changes: Partial<T>): Promise<void> {
    // Atomic update — load current state, merge changes, save back
    await this.db.transaction(async (manager) => {
      const rows = await manager.query(
        `SELECT state_json FROM saga_instances WHERE saga_id = $1 FOR UPDATE`,
        [sagaId],
      );
      if (rows.length === 0) throw new Error(`Saga ${sagaId} not found`);

      const currentState = JSON.parse(rows[0].state_json);
      const newState     = { ...currentState, ...changes };

      await manager.query(
        `UPDATE saga_instances
         SET status = $2, current_step = $3, state_json = $4, updated_at = NOW()
         WHERE saga_id = $1`,
        [sagaId, newState.status, newState.currentStep ?? 0, JSON.stringify(newState)],
      );
    });
  }

  async findById(sagaId: string): Promise<T | null> {
    const rows = await this.db.query(
      `SELECT state_json FROM saga_instances WHERE saga_id = $1`,
      [sagaId],
    );
    return rows.length > 0 ? JSON.parse(rows[0].state_json) : null;
  }

  async findStuckSagas(olderThanMinutes: number): Promise<T[]> {
    const cutoff = new Date(Date.now() - olderThanMinutes * 60 * 1000);
    const rows   = await this.db.query(
      `SELECT state_json FROM saga_instances
       WHERE status IN ('STARTED', 'COMPENSATING')
         AND updated_at < $1
         AND retry_count < 5`,
      [cutoff],
    );
    return rows.map((r: any) => JSON.parse(r.state_json));
  }
}

// ─── Recovery job — resumes stuck sagas ──────────────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class SagaRecoveryJob {
  constructor(
    private readonly sagaRepo:     PostgresSagaRepository<LoanDisbursementSagaState>,
    private readonly orchestrator: LoanDisbursementOrchestrator,
  ) {}

  @Cron("*/5 * * * *")  // every 5 minutes
  async recoverStuckSagas(): Promise<void> {
    const stuckSagas = await this.sagaRepo.findStuckSagas(10);  // stuck for > 10 minutes

    for (const state of stuckSagas) {
      this.logger.warn(`Recovering stuck saga ${state.sagaId} at step ${state.currentStep}`);
      try {
        if (state.status === "COMPENSATING") {
          await this.orchestrator.resumeCompensation(state);
        } else {
          await this.orchestrator.resumeExecution(state);
        }
      } catch (err: any) {
        this.logger.error(`Recovery of ${state.sagaId} failed: ${err.message}`);
      }
    }
  }
}
```

**Q11. How does the Saga pattern handle the "read your own writes" problem — where a step reads intermediate state that isn't yet final?**
A: This is the *lack of isolation* problem in sagas. Because each local transaction commits immediately, a concurrent request can read a partially-committed saga state:

```typescript
// Scenario: Loan Disbursement Saga has approved the loan (step 2)
// but not yet created the NACH mandate (step 5).
// A concurrent request asks: "Can this customer get another loan?"
// The loan is in APPROVED status — should this count against the active loan limit?

// ─── Strategy 1: Semantic locking — mark in-progress records explicitly ────────

// The orchestrator marks the loan as "DISBURSING" (saga in progress)
// before starting, and "ACTIVE" only after the saga completes.
// Any concurrent eligibility check that sees "DISBURSING" knows the record is dirty
// and treats it as if the loan already exists.

// In the Loan Service:
async approveLoan(params: ApproveLoanParams): Promise<void> {
  await this.loanDao.update(params.loanId, {
    status: "DISBURSING",   // sentinel status — saga in progress
  });
}

// In the eligibility check:
async canCustomerApplyForNewLoan(customerId: string): Promise<boolean> {
  const loans = await this.loanDao.findByCustomer(customerId);
  // Count ACTIVE and DISBURSING as "consuming a slot"
  const activeCount = loans.filter(l => ["ACTIVE", "DISBURSING"].includes(l.status)).length;
  return activeCount < 2;
}

// ─── Strategy 2: Countermeasures at read time ─────────────────────────────────

// Some reads should be deferred until the saga completes.
// The orchestrator publishes a "saga.completed" or "saga.failed" event,
// and the read endpoint waits for one of those before responding.

// Pattern: polling with a timeout
async waitForLoanStatus(loanId: string, sagaId: string, timeoutMs = 30_000): Promise<LoanStatus> {
  const deadline = Date.now() + timeoutMs;
  while (Date.now() < deadline) {
    const loan = await this.loanDao.findById(loanId);
    if (loan && !["DISBURSING", "PENDING"].includes(loan.status)) {
      return loan.status;
    }
    await this.sleep(500);
  }
  throw new GatewayTimeoutException(`Loan ${loanId} status not resolved within ${timeoutMs}ms`);
}

// ─── Strategy 3: Process manager / correlation ID ─────────────────────────────

// Expose the sagaId to the client alongside the optimistic response.
// Client polls a saga-status endpoint rather than the resource endpoint.

// POST /loans/apply → 202 Accepted + { sagaId: "saga-abc" }
// GET /sagas/saga-abc → { status: "COMPLETED", loanId: "loan-001" }
//                    or { status: "FAILED", reason: "CIBIL too low" }
```

```typescript
      status:           "AADHAR_VERIFICATION_PENDING",
      aadharRequestId:  requestId,
      updatedAt:        new Date(),
    });
  }

  }

  @Cron("*/10 * * * *")
  async timeoutPendingVerifications(): Promise<void> {
    const timeoutThreshold = new Date(Date.now() - 30 * 60 * 1000);  // 30 min timeout

    const timedOut = await this.sagaRepo.findByStatusAndUpdatedBefore(

    }
  }
```

**Q13. How does the Saga pattern interact with the Outbox pattern to guarantee event delivery?**
A: The Outbox pattern solves a subtle but critical problem: after a local transaction commits, the service must publish an event. If the service crashes between the commit and the publish, the event is lost — the next step of the saga never triggers. The Outbox pattern makes the event publish atomic with the local transaction:

```typescript
// ─── The dual-write problem ───────────────────────────────────────────────────

// ❌ Not atomic — crash between commit and publish loses the event
async step2ApproveLoan(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
  await this.loanDao.update(state.loanId, { status: "APPROVED" });  // ← commits
  // ↑ Service crashes here — event never published — saga stuck forever
  await this.publisher.publish({ eventType: "loan.approved", ... });
}

// ✅ Outbox pattern — event write is part of the same DB transaction
async step2ApproveLoan(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
  await this.db.transaction(async (manager) => {
    // Update domain state
    await manager.update(LoanEntity, state.loanId, { status: "APPROVED" });

    // Write event to outbox table — same transaction — atomic
    await manager.insert(OutboxEventEntity, {
      id:        crypto.randomUUID(),
      eventType: "loan.approved",
      payload:   JSON.stringify({
        sagaId:    state.sagaId,
        loanId:    state.loanId,
        // ...
      }),
      createdAt:  new Date(),
      published:  false,
    });
    // Both writes committed atomically — if service crashes, outbox row survives
  });
  // Separate process (outbox relay) polls outbox and publishes to Kafka
}

// ─── Outbox relay — polls and publishes ──────────────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class OutboxRelayService {
  @Cron("*/1 * * * * *")   // every second
  async relayUnpublishedEvents(): Promise<void> {
    const events = await this.outboxDao.findUnpublished(100);

    for (const event of events) {
      try {
        await this.kafkaProducer.send({
          topic:    event.eventType.replace(".", "-"),
          messages: [{ key: event.id, value: event.payload }],
        });

        await this.outboxDao.markPublished(event.id);
      } catch (err: any) {
        this.logger.error(`Failed to publish outbox event ${event.id}: ${err.message}`);
        // Will retry on next cron tick — at-least-once delivery
      }
    }
  }
}
```

**Q14. How do you monitor and observe sagas in production — what metrics and dashboards matter most?**

```typescript
// ─── Saga-level metrics ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class SagaMetricsService {
  private readonly sagaDuration       = new Histogram({ name: "saga_duration_ms",       labelNames: ["saga_type", "status"] });
  private readonly sagaStepDuration   = new Histogram({ name: "saga_step_duration_ms",  labelNames: ["saga_type", "step_name"] });
  private readonly sagaCompletedTotal = new Counter(  { name: "saga_completed_total",    labelNames: ["saga_type"] });
  private readonly sagaFailedTotal    = new Counter(  { name: "saga_failed_total",       labelNames: ["saga_type", "failed_step"] });
  private readonly sagaCompensatedTotal = new Counter({ name: "saga_compensated_total",  labelNames: ["saga_type"] });
  private readonly sagaStuckGauge     = new Gauge(    { name: "saga_stuck_count",         labelNames: ["saga_type"] });

  recordSagaCompleted(sagaType: string, durationMs: number): void {
    this.sagaDuration.observe({ saga_type: sagaType, status: "completed" }, durationMs);
    this.sagaCompletedTotal.inc({ saga_type: sagaType });
  }

  recordSagaFailed(sagaType: string, failedStep: string, durationMs: number): void {
    this.sagaDuration.observe({ saga_type: sagaType, status: "failed" }, durationMs);
    this.sagaFailedTotal.inc({ saga_type: sagaType, failed_step: failedStep });
  }

  recordSagaCompensated(sagaType: string): void {
    this.sagaCompensatedTotal.inc({ saga_type: sagaType });
  }

  recordStepDuration(sagaType: string, stepName: string, durationMs: number): void {

/*

  → loan disbursement saga_failed_total / saga_completed_total > 0.05

// In Jaeger/Zipkin, search by sagaId to see the complete end-to-end flow
// across all microservices — credit bureau, loan service, ledger, NACH.

@Injectable()
export class InstrumentedLoanDisbursementOrchestrator extends LoanDisbursementOrchestrator {
```

**Q15. How does the Saga pattern handle duplicate command processing — what if the orchestrator sends the same command twice?**
A:

```typescript
// Orchestrator restarts after a crash may re-send commands to participant services.
// Each participant must be idempotent with respect to the command it receives.

// ─── Command idempotency in participant services ───────────────────────────────

// Pattern: command carries a unique commandId derived from the sagaId + step
// Participants store processed commandIds and skip duplicates.

interface SagaCommand {
  commandId:  string;    // deterministic: `${sagaId}:step:${stepName}`
  sagaId:     string;
  // ... command-specific fields
}

// In the Account Service:
@Injectable()
export class DisbursementAccountHandler {
  async createAccount(command: CreateDisbursementAccountCommand): Promise<AccountCreatedResult> {
    // Idempotency: return same result if commandId already processed
    const existing = await this.commandLog.find(command.commandId);
    if (existing) {
      return JSON.parse(existing.resultJson) as AccountCreatedResult;
    }

    // Process the command
    const account = await this.accountDao.insert({
      customerId:    command.customerId,
      loanId:        command.loanId,
      amountInPaise: command.amountInPaise,
      status:        "ACTIVE",
    });

    const result: AccountCreatedResult = { accountId: account.id };

    // Record processed command with result — atomic with the business operation
    await this.db.transaction(async (manager) => {
      await manager.insert(ProcessedCommandEntity, {
        commandId:  command.commandId,
        resultJson: JSON.stringify(result),
        processedAt: new Date(),
      });
      // account insert already happened — this is just the idempotency record
    });

    return result;
  }
}

// ─── Deterministic commandId generation in the orchestrator ──────────────────

private makeCommandId(sagaId: string, step: string): string {

  // Safe to retry — participant deduplicates automatically
  return `${sagaId}:${step}`;
}
```

## Section 5: Pitfalls & Anti-patterns

**Q16. What is the "saga long transaction" anti-pattern — and how does the lack of isolation cause real problems?**
A: Because saga steps commit immediately, a long-running saga exposes intermediate states that other transactions can read and act upon — producing results that would not be possible in a fully isolated ACID transaction:

```typescript
// Scenario: Loan Disbursement Saga — 7-step process, takes ~30 seconds end-to-end

// Problem: Dirty read during the saga
// Time 0:   Saga starts. Loan created with status DISBURSING.
// Time 5s:  Eligibility check approves the customer for a second loan.
//           The first loan shows as DISBURSING — eligibility service counts it as "in flight".
//           But if the saga fails and compensates, the first loan is cancelled.
//           The customer may have been shown "approved for 2 loans simultaneously".

// Problem: Non-repeatable read across saga steps
// Time 0:   Saga reads customer credit limit: ₹10,00,000
// Time 15s: Another transaction reduces customer's credit limit to ₹5,00,000
//           (customer took out a credit card)
// Time 20s: Saga's ledger step disburses ₹10,00,000 — now exceeds the credit limit

// ─── Mitigations ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

// Mitigation 1: Pessimistic lock at saga start (counteracts concurrency benefits)
// Lock the customer record for the duration of the saga — blocks all concurrent changes.
// Acceptable for low-concurrency, high-stakes operations (mortgage applications).
// Not acceptable for high-throughput payment processing.

// Mitigation 2: Semantic lock + re-check at critical steps
async step4UpdateLedger(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
  // Re-validate credit limit just before the money moves — the most critical step
  const customer = await this.customerService.getById(state.customerId);
  if (state.principalInPaise > customer.availableCreditLimitPaise) {
    throw new Error("Credit limit changed during saga — aborting disbursement");
  }
  // Proceed with ledger update
  await this.ledgerService.disburse({ ... });
}

// Mitigation 3: Countermeasure — reject concurrent sagas for the same customer
async start(command: StartLoanDisbursementCommand): Promise<string> {
  // Check: is there already an in-progress saga for this customer?
  const activeSaga = await this.sagaRepo.findInProgressForCustomer(command.customerId);
  if (activeSaga) {
    throw new ConflictException(
      `Another disbursement saga (${activeSaga.sagaId}) is already in progress for this customer`
    );
  }
  // ... proceed
}
```

**Q17. What is the "saga without compensation" anti-pattern — when steps have no defined rollback?**
A:

```typescript
// ❌ Step with no compensation defined — saga leaves a financial orphan on failure
class LoanDisbursementOrchestrator {
  private async step4UpdateLedger(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    // Money debited from funding pool — committed, visible to everyone
    await this.ledgerService.disburse({ amountInPaise: state.principalInPaise });
    // No compensateStep4Ledger defined — if step 5 fails, money is gone with no record
  }

  private async compensateStep4Ledger(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
    // ✅ This MUST exist — reverse the ledger entry
    await this.ledgerService.reverseEntry(state.ledgerEntryId!, `Saga ${state.sagaId} failed`);
  }
}

// ─── Pre-flight checklist: every step that commits money must have a compensation ─

interface SagaStep<T> {
  execute:     (state: T) => Promise<Partial<T>>;    // returns state updates
  compensate?: (state: T) => Promise<void>;          // undefined only for idempotent reads
  isCompensatable: boolean;
}

// Enforce at compile time: if a step modifies financial data, compensation is required
type FinancialStep<T> = SagaStep<T> & { compensate: (state: T) => Promise<void>; isCompensatable: true };
```

**Q18. What is the "choreography spaghetti" anti-pattern — and how does it develop?**
A: Choreography starts simple but grows into an unmaintainable tangle as the system evolves:

```
Initial design (clean):
  PaymentInitiated → LoanService (updates balance)
                   → LedgerService (creates entry)
                   → NotificationService (sends SMS)

Six months later (spaghetti):
  PaymentInitiated → LoanService → EmiRecorded → LedgerService → LedgerUpdated → AuditService
                                               ↘ ComplianceService → ComplianceChecked → RiskService
                   → LedgerService (direct, for corrections)
  LoanStatusChanged → NotificationService
                    → LedgerService (for late fees)
                    → RiskService (for portfolio review)
  RiskAlertRaised → LoanService (status update)
                  → NotificationService (alert)
                  → ComplianceService (escalation)
```

When you add a new service and can't determine what events it should listen to without reading 15 service files; when a bug requires tracing events through 8 services to understand the flow; when a failing step causes cascading unexpected effects in services that "weren't supposed to be involved" — these are the signs that choreography has become spaghetti. The fix: switch to orchestration for this flow, giving it an explicit state machine with a single owner.

**Q19. How do you handle a saga where compensation itself fails — what's the escalation path?**
A:

```typescript
// When a compensating transaction fails, the system is in an inconsistent state
// that cannot be automatically resolved. The only safe action is human escalation.

@Injectable()
export class SagaCompensationFailureHandler {
  async handle(failure: CompensationFailure): Promise<void> {
    this.logger.error(`CRITICAL: Compensation failed for saga ${failure.sagaId}`, failure);

    // 1. Record the failure — permanent audit trail
    await this.sagaRepo.update(failure.sagaId, {
      status:                  "COMPENSATION_FAILED",
      compensationFailureAt:   new Date(),
      compensationFailureReason: failure.error,
    });

    // 2. Immediate PagerDuty alert — P1 incident
    await this.pagerDuty.trigger({
      severity:    "critical",
      summary:     `Saga compensation failure: ${failure.sagaId}`,
      details: {
        sagaId:         failure.sagaId,
        loanId:         failure.loanId,
        failedAtStep:   failure.failedStep,
        compensationError: failure.error,
        manualStepsNeeded: this.determineManualSteps(failure),
      },
    });

    // 3. Mark the affected resources with a "LOCKED" flag
    // — prevents any further automated operations until ops team reviews
    await this.loanDao.update(failure.loanId, {
      adminLocked:       true,
      adminLockReason:   `Saga ${failure.sagaId} compensation failure`,
      adminLockedAt:     new Date(),
    });

    // 4. Enqueue for manual reconciliation queue
    await this.reconciliationQueue.add({
      type:   "saga_compensation_failure",
      sagaId: failure.sagaId,
      loanId: failure.loanId,
      createdAt: new Date(),
    });
  }

  private determineManualSteps(failure: CompensationFailure): string[] {
    // Business-specific: what does ops need to do manually?
    return [
      `Verify ledger state for loan ${failure.loanId}`,
      `If ledger was debited: manually raise reversal entry`,
      `Update loan ${failure.loanId} status to CANCELLED in admin panel`,
      `Notify customer ${failure.customerId} of disbursement failure`,
      `Close PagerDuty incident after reconciliation is confirmed`,
    ];
  }
}
```

**Q20. What is the "saga as a distributed monolith" anti-pattern — when orchestration coupling negates microservices benefits?**

## Section 6: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q21. "Design a loan disbursement saga for a fintech platform. The saga involves: credit check, loan approval, ledger debit from funding pool, NACH mandate registration, customer notification. The credit check service is the most unreliable — it times out 10% of the time. Walk through your design choices."**
A:

```typescript
// ─── Design decisions driven by the unreliability of the credit check ─────────

// Decision 1: Orchestration over choreography
// Six steps, conditional logic (reject if score < 650), compensation required —
// orchestration gives us an explicit state machine and a single place to handle retries.

// Decision 2: Retry with exponential backoff on the credit check step ONLY
private async step1PullCreditReport(state: LoanDisbursementSagaState): Promise<void> {
  let lastErr: Error | null = null;

  for (let attempt = 0; attempt < 3; attempt++) {
    try {
      const report = await Promise.race([
        this.creditBureau.pullReport(state.customerId),
        this.sleep(5000).then(() => { throw new Error("Credit bureau timeout"); }),
      ]);

      await this.sagaRepo.update(state.sagaId, {
        cibilScore: report.score, status: "CREDIT_PULLED", currentStep: 1,
      });
      return;
    } catch (err: any) {
      lastErr = err;
      if (attempt < 2) {
        await this.sleep(Math.pow(2, attempt) * 1000);  // 1s, 2s backoff
      }
    }
  }

  // All 3 attempts failed — saga fails, NO compensation needed (nothing succeeded yet)
  throw lastErr!;
}

// Decision 3: Step ordering — most unreliable step first
// If the credit check fails, no money has moved, no NACH mandate has been created,
// no ledger entry exists. Compensation is trivially simple (nothing to undo).
// Irreversible steps (ledger debit, NACH mandate) come AFTER the unreliable steps.

// Decision 4: NACH mandate registration — async webhook pattern
// NACH mandate registration at NPCI is async (minutes to hours).
// Saga pauses after submitting the mandate request; resumes on webhook.

// Decision 5: Notification is last and non-compensatable
// SMS/email cannot be unsent. Placed last so it's only sent on confirmed success.
// If it fails, saga still marks COMPLETED — notification failure is best-effort,
// logged for retry but not a saga-level failure.

// Decision 6: Saga timeout — escalate if stuck for > 2 hours
// NACH webhook may be delayed. Set a 2-hour timeout before escalating to ops.

// ─── Flow summary ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

/*
Step 1: Credit check (unreliable → 3 retries, 5s timeout each)
Step 2: Loan approval (idempotent → command dedup)
Step 3: Ledger debit (use Outbox pattern → exactly-once guarantee)
Step 4: NACH mandate (async → webhook resume, 2-hour timeout)
Step 5: Notification (best-effort → not compensatable)

Compensation order (reverse):
  Step 4 fail → cancel NACH mandate (if submitted)
  Step 3 fail → reverse ledger entry
  Step 2 fail → cancel loan approval
  Step 1 fail → no compensation needed
*/
```

**Q23. "A senior engineer argues that for a simple 3-step saga (debit account → credit account → notify), choreography is overkill and you should just use a database transaction with a distributed lock. How do you respond?"**
A: The argument has merit in some cases — the response depends on whether the three steps cross service and database boundaries:

> "If all three steps — debit, credit, and notify — write to the same database, a single ACID transaction is absolutely the right answer. There's no saga needed. An ACID transaction provides isolation, atomicity, and no compensation logic required.
>
> The saga pattern becomes necessary only when the three steps touch *different* databases or services that you cannot enroll in a single transaction. If the debit is in a Payments PostgreSQL database, the credit is in a Ledger service with its own MySQL database, and the notify goes through a third-party SMS provider — there is no single transaction boundary that spans all three. A distributed lock keeps only one caller out at a time; it doesn't help if the credit service crashes after the debit succeeds.
>
> For a 3-step saga that genuinely crosses service boundaries, choreography may still be appropriate if the flow is stable and simple. Three events, three listeners, no conditional branching — that's a reasonable fit. My concern with choreography at even 3 steps is observability: 'what state is order 12345 in right now?' requires correlating events across three services. For a production fintech system under regulatory scrutiny, I'd still lean toward a lightweight orchestrator that persists saga state explicitly — it makes the ops team's job tractable.
>
> The distributed lock approach has a specific failure mode: the service that holds the lock crashes after step 1 commits and before step 2 starts. The lock is held until its TTL expires (5–30 seconds), during which step 2 never runs. After TTL, the lock is released — but who re-triggers step 2? Nothing does. You need a recovery mechanism, which is exactly what the saga's state persistence and recovery cron job provide. The distributed lock doesn't eliminate the problem — it just delays it."

**Q24. "In a high-throughput system processing 5,000 payment sagas per second, the orchestrator's saga state table is becoming a bottleneck. How do you address this?"**
A:

```typescript
// ─── Problem: 5,000 saga state updates/second on one PostgreSQL table ─────────

// Each saga step does a SELECT + UPDATE on saga_instances.
// At 5,000 sagas/s with 6 steps each = 30,000 writes/s on one table.
// PostgreSQL can handle ~10,000 writes/s on a well-tuned table — we're 3× over.

// ─── Solution 1: Partition the saga state table ───────────────────────────────

// Partition by saga_id hash — distribute writes across 16 partitions
// CREATE TABLE saga_instances (
//   saga_id    UUID,
//   ...
// ) PARTITION BY HASH (saga_id);
//
// CREATE TABLE saga_instances_0 PARTITION OF saga_instances
//   FOR VALUES WITH (MODULUS 16, REMAINDER 0);
// ... (16 partitions total)

// ─── Solution 2: In-memory saga state with async persistence ──────────────────

// Hot path: keep active saga state in Redis (fast writes, sub-millisecond)
// Cold path: asynchronously flush to PostgreSQL every 5 seconds for durability

@Injectable()
export class HybridSagaRepository<T> {
  private readonly REDIS_TTL = 3600;  // 1 hour — longer than any saga should run

  async save(state: T & { sagaId: string }): Promise<void> {
    const key = `saga:${(state as any).sagaId}`;
    // Fast write to Redis — hot path
    await this.redis.setex(key, this.REDIS_TTL, JSON.stringify(state));
    // Async write to PostgreSQL — does not block the saga
    this.persistToPostgres(state).catch(err =>
      this.logger.error(`Async persist failed for ${(state as any).sagaId}: ${err.message}`)
    );
  }

  async findById(sagaId: string): Promise<T | null> {
    // Check Redis first (warm path)
    const cached = await this.redis.get(`saga:${sagaId}`);
    if (cached) return JSON.parse(cached);
    // Fall back to PostgreSQL (recovery path)
    return this.findInPostgres(sagaId);
  }
}

// ─── Solution 3: Separate orchestrator instances per saga type ────────────────

// Instead of one orchestrator handling all saga types:
// PaymentSagaOrchestrator → its own partition of saga state
// DisbursementSagaOrchestrator → its own partition
// KycSagaOrchestrator → its own partition
//
// Each scales independently; saga tables are logically (and optionally physically) separate.

// ─── Solution 4: Event-carried state — reduce database reads per step ──────────

// Instead of loading saga state from DB on every step,
// carry the full saga state in the event/command payload.
// Each step receives complete context — no DB read needed to continue.

interface SagaStepCommand<T> {
  sagaState: T;    // full current state carried in the command
  sagaId:    string;
}

// Trade-off: larger message payloads; state in transit is not durable
// (crash between step and next persistence = state in the command only)
// Mitigate: event broker (Kafka) provides at-least-once delivery + consumer offset
```

**Q25. "How do you explain the difference between a Saga and a simple workflow (like a BullMQ job pipeline) to a junior engineer who asks why you need a 'pattern' for what seems like a queue?"**
A: A grounded, concrete explanation:

> "A BullMQ pipeline is great for processing a single job that stays within one service — resize an image, send an email, generate a PDF report. If the job fails, BullMQ retries it. The data for the job all lives in one place — typically whatever the job payload carries or a single database the job worker touches.
>
> A Saga handles something fundamentally different: a business operation that must atomically affect multiple services, each with their own database, each of which may succeed or fail independently. Imagine a loan disbursement: you need to pull a credit report from a credit bureau service, approve the loan in the loan service, debit money from a ledger service, and create a NACH mandate in a banking service. Each of these is a network call to a separate service. Each writes to a different database.
>
> The key problem a saga solves that a queue doesn't: **partial failure and rollback**. If step 3 — the ledger debit — succeeds, but step 4 — the NACH mandate — fails, money has already moved. A BullMQ retry of the whole job re-debits the ledger (double charge). The saga pattern has an explicit answer: run a *compensating transaction* that reverses the ledger debit — a new, forward transaction that semantically undoes what step 3 did.
>
> The second thing a saga solves: **state persistence across service boundaries**. A BullMQ job is a single unit of work. A saga is a long-running process that may span seconds, minutes, or hours (if an external API is async and responds via webhook). The saga state — 'we're on step 3, the NACH mandate registration is pending a callback' — must be stored in a database, not in a queue payload that gets lost if the orchestrator restarts.
>
> So: use BullMQ for single-service async jobs. Use the Saga pattern when the job crosses service boundaries, writes to multiple databases, and must handle partial failure with rollback."

| Idempotency requirement                | All steps must be idempotent — at-least-once delivery means duplicates occur      |
| Outbox pattern role                    | Makes event publish atomic with local transaction — prevents lost events           |
| Saga state persistence                 | PostgreSQL or Redis — survives orchestrator restarts; enables recovery            |
| Stuck saga recovery                    | Cron job finds sagas in non-terminal state older than N minutes; re-triggers      |
| Non-compensatable steps                | Sent emails, published events, filed government reports — place at saga end        |
| Semantic locking                       | Mark in-progress records with sentinel status to prevent dirty reads              |
| Compensation failure escalation        | Lock affected resources; PagerDuty P1; enqueue for manual reconciliation          |
| 2PC vs. Saga                           | 2PC: blocking, coordinator SPOF, incompatible with many stores; Saga: eventually consistent, non-blocking, widely compatible |
| Read your own writes problem           | Intermediate committed state visible to concurrent requests — mitigate with semantic locks or re-validation |
| When to use choreography               | Simple, linear, stable flows with ≤ 3–4 participants                             |
| When to use orchestration              | Complex, conditional, multi-step flows; regulated systems needing explicit state  |
| Common misuses                         | Saga without compensation, choreography spaghetti, distributed monolith orchestrator, no idempotency |
| Fintech canonical examples             | Loan disbursement, KYC onboarding, EMI collection, fund transfer, NACH mandate setup |
| Regulatory note                        | Compensation failures must produce audit records and trigger manual reconciliation — not silent failures |
| SOLID alignment                        | SRP (each step is one local transaction), OCP (add step without modifying existing), DIP (orchestrator depends on ports, not concrete services) |

**Tip for the Interview:** At the 5 YOE level, Saga questions surface as distributed systems design problems — "design the loan disbursement flow across 5 microservices," "how do you handle partial failure in a multi-service transaction," or "why can't you use a database transaction when data is split across services?" The answers that land demonstrate five things: (1) you know precisely why 2PC fails in microservices — blocking, coordinator SPOF, XA incompatibility, scale — not just "it doesn't work"; (2) you can articulate the choreography vs. orchestration trade-off with concrete examples of when each is appropriate, and you know that most production fintech systems with complex flows use orchestration because debuggability and explicit state are critical under regulatory scrutiny; (3) you've thought through the practical concerns — idempotency via saga step tracking table, outbox pattern for guaranteed event delivery, saga state persistence for crash recovery, and the recovery cron job; (4) you can handle the hard questions — what happens when compensation fails (lock the resource, escalate to ops, manual reconciliation), what happens when a step is non-compensatable (place it last), and how to handle lack of isolation (semantic locking, re-validation at critical steps); and (5) you connect the pattern to the fintech domain — NACH mandate registration via async webhook, RBI audit trail requirements for compensation records, and the specific ordering of steps to minimise the blast radius of failure.
