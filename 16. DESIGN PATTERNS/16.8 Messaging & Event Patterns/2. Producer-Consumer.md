# Messaging & Event Patterns: Producer-Consumer — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is the Producer-Consumer pattern?**
A: The Producer-Consumer pattern is a concurrency and messaging design pattern where one set of components (producers) generates work items or messages and places them into a shared buffer or queue, while another set of components (consumers) retrieves and processes those items. The two sides operate independently — producers do not wait for consumers to finish processing before generating more work, and consumers do not depend on specific producers. The queue between them acts as a decoupling mechanism: it absorbs bursts of production, smooths out processing rates, and allows the two sides to scale independently. The pattern appears in virtually every distributed system as the foundation for task queues, job schedulers, event streams, message brokers, and asynchronous communication between microservices.

In its simplest form the pattern is a bounded buffer protected by a mutex and two semaphores (the classic operating systems textbook version). In distributed systems, the buffer is a message broker — Kafka, RabbitMQ, SQS, Redis Streams — and the producers and consumers are separate processes or services, potentially running on different machines.

**Q2. What problem does the Producer-Consumer pattern solve, and what breaks without it?**
A: It solves three interconnected problems simultaneously:

**Temporal decoupling — producers and consumers run at different times:** Without a queue, the producer must wait for the consumer to be ready, and the consumer must wait for the producer to produce. Any difference in availability or timing cascades: if the consumer is restarting, the producer blocks; if the producer is ahead of the consumer, back-pressure must propagate all the way to the upstream caller. With a queue, the producer writes and moves on; the consumer reads whenever it is ready. A payment service can record a transaction and publish a message without waiting for the notification service to send the SMS.

**Rate decoupling — producers and consumers run at different speeds:** Without a queue, the faster side must be throttled to match the slower side. A burst of 50,000 payment events at 09:00 on the first of the month (EMI due date) cannot be handled by 5 notification service instances at steady-state capacity. With a queue, the burst is absorbed: the queue grows during the burst; the consumers drain it at their natural rate; the producer is never blocked. The queue is the shock absorber.

**Spatial decoupling — producers and consumers don't know about each other:** Without a queue, the producer must know the consumer's address, protocol, and interface. If the consumer changes its API, the producer must update. With a queue, the producer knows only the queue's address and the message schema. New consumers can be added without touching the producer; producers can be replaced without touching consumers.

Without the pattern: tightly coupled synchronous chains where every service depends on every downstream service's availability, latency, and capacity. A slow downstream cascades into a slow upstream, cascading into a slow user-facing API. With the pattern: each service operates at its own pace, failures are isolated, and the system degrades gracefully rather than catastrophically.

**Q3. What are the core components of the Producer-Consumer pattern and their responsibilities?**
A: Four components with distinct responsibilities:

**Producer:**
- Generates messages or work items.
- Publishes to the queue/broker without knowledge of who will consume.
- Responsible for message correctness — the payload must conform to the schema consumers expect.
- May apply back-pressure awareness — if the queue is full, the producer must decide: block, drop, or route to a dead letter queue.
- In fintech: a payment service publishes `PaymentSucceeded` events; a loan service publishes `EmiDue` events; an API handler publishes `LoanApplicationSubmitted` commands.

**Queue / Message Broker:**
- Durably stores messages between production and consumption.
- Provides delivery guarantees: at-most-once, at-least-once, or exactly-once.
- May provide ordering guarantees: global order, per-partition order, or no order.
- Handles consumer group management, offset tracking, dead letter queuing, and retry policies.
- Examples: Apache Kafka (partitioned log), RabbitMQ (AMQP exchange + queue), AWS SQS (managed queue), Redis Streams.

**Consumer:**
- Retrieves messages from the queue and processes them.
- Responsible for idempotency — the same message may be delivered more than once under at-least-once semantics.
- Responsible for committing offsets or acknowledging messages only after successful processing — prevents data loss on consumer crash.
- In fintech: a notification service consumes `PaymentSucceeded` and sends SMS; a ledger service consumes `LoanDisbursed` and creates journal entries.

**Consumer Group:**
- A set of consumers that collectively consume a topic or queue.
- Each message is delivered to exactly one consumer within the group (competing consumers pattern).
- Enables horizontal scaling of consumption: add more consumers to process faster.
- Different consumer groups receive independent copies of every message (fan-out pattern).

**Q4. What delivery guarantee models exist, and what are their trade-offs in a financial system?**
A: Three models with increasing strength of guarantee and increasing implementation complexity:

**At-most-once (fire and forget):**
- Message is delivered zero or one times — never more than once.
- If delivery fails, the message is lost; no retry.
- Implemented by acknowledging (committing the offset) before processing.
- Trade-off: simplest to implement, lowest latency, zero duplicate risk. Acceptable for non-critical observability events (metrics, debug logs) where occasional loss is tolerable.
- Never appropriate for financial events: a lost `PaymentSucceeded` message means the customer's loan balance is never updated, the ledger entry is never created, and the customer never receives confirmation.

**At-least-once (the practical default):**
- Message is delivered one or more times — processing is guaranteed, but duplicates are possible.
- Implemented by committing the offset only after successful processing. If the consumer crashes after processing but before committing, the message is redelivered.
- Trade-off: every consumer must be idempotent. Complexity shifts from the broker to the consumer.
- The correct model for fintech: a `PaymentSucceeded` event processed twice must have the same effect as processing it once. Use idempotency keys, database unique constraints, and the processed-event table pattern (see Idempotency topic) to handle duplicates safely.

**Exactly-once (the holy grail):**
- Message is delivered and processed exactly one time — no loss, no duplicates.
- Implemented by combining transactional producers (Kafka transactions), idempotent producers, and transactional consumers (atomic offset commit + business database write in one transaction).
- Trade-off: significant infrastructure complexity, higher latency, limited to specific broker + storage combinations (Kafka + same Kafka cluster, Kafka + supported databases via Kafka Connect).
- Appropriate for double-entry ledger updates, NACH debit submissions to NPCI, and regulatory reporting where neither loss nor duplication is acceptable.

**Q5. How does the Producer-Consumer pattern differ from Publish-Subscribe (Pub-Sub)?**
A: The two patterns share the queue as a decoupling mechanism but differ in delivery semantics:

| Dimension              | Producer-Consumer (Competing Consumers)                       | Publish-Subscribe (Fan-out)                                          |
|------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Message delivery       | One consumer receives each message                            | All subscribers receive each message                                 |
| Consumer groups        | One group; consumers within the group compete for messages    | Multiple groups; each group gets its own copy                        |
| Use case               | Distributing work across parallel workers                     | Broadcasting events to multiple independent listeners                |
| Example                | 10 EMI collection workers pull from a queue; each loan is processed once | LoanDisbursed event consumed by Ledger, Notification, and NACH — all three receive it |
| Load balancing         | Automatic — broker assigns messages to available consumers    | Each subscriber group independently load-balances within itself      |
| Kafka implementation   | Multiple consumers in the same consumer group                 | Multiple consumer groups, each with one or more consumers            |
| RabbitMQ implementation | Multiple consumers bound to the same queue (round-robin)     | Fanout exchange routes to multiple queues (one per subscriber)       |

In practice, most production systems use both simultaneously: a `PaymentSucceeded` event is published to a Kafka topic (Pub-Sub), and multiple consumer groups each contain multiple consumers (Producer-Consumer within each group): the Ledger consumer group, the Notification consumer group, and the NACH consumer group all receive the event independently, and within each group, multiple consumers compete for partition assignments.

```typescript
// src/queues/producers/emi-collection.producer.ts
@Injectable()
export class EmiCollectionProducer {
  constructor(
    @InjectQueue(QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION) private readonly queue: Queue,
  ) {}

  async enqueueEmiCollection(params: {
    loans:      ActiveLoanRow[];
    dueDate:    Date;
    batchId:    string;
  }): Promise<void> {
    const jobs = params.loans.map(loan => ({
      name: "collect-emi",
      data: {
        loanId:        loan.id,
        customerId:    loan.customerId,
        mandateId:     loan.nachMandateId,
        amountInPaise: loan.emiInPaise,
        dueDate:       params.dueDate.toISOString(),
        attemptNumber: 1,
        idempotencyKey: `emi:${loan.id}:${params.dueDate.toISOString().slice(0, 7)}`,
      } satisfies EmiCollectionJobPayload,
      opts: {
        // Deduplication: same jobId → BullMQ ignores if already queued
        jobId:    `${params.batchId}:${loan.id}`,
        priority: loan.isOverdue ? 1 : 10,  // overdue loans processed first
        attempts: 3,
        backoff:  { type: "exponential", delay: 60_000 },  // 1min, 2min, 4min
        delay:    0,
      },
    }));

    // Bulk add — more efficient than addding one by one
    await this.queue.addBulk(jobs);
  }

  async scheduleReminder(params: {
    loanId:      string;
    customerId:  string;
    dueDate:     Date;
    reminderDays: number;
  }): Promise<void> {
    const reminderAt = new Date(params.dueDate);
    reminderAt.setDate(reminderAt.getDate() - params.reminderDays);
    const delayMs = Math.max(0, reminderAt.getTime() - Date.now());

    await this.queue.add(
      "emi-reminder",
      {
        loanId:        params.loanId,
        customerId:    params.customerId,
        amountInPaise: 0,   // filled by consumer from DB
        dueDate:       params.dueDate.toISOString(),
        attemptNumber: 1,
        mandateId:     "",
        idempotencyKey: `reminder:${params.loanId}:${params.dueDate.toISOString().slice(0, 10)}`,
      } satisfies EmiCollectionJobPayload,
      {
        delay:    delayMs,
        jobId:    `reminder:${params.loanId}:${params.dueDate.toISOString().slice(0, 10)}`,
        attempts: 2,
        backoff:  { type: "fixed", delay: 300_000 },  // 5 min retry for reminders
      },
    );
  }
}

// ─── Consumer (worker) — processes jobs ──────────────────────────────────────

// src/queues/consumers/emi-collection.consumer.ts
@Processor(QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION, {
  concurrency: 20,    // process up to 20 jobs in parallel within this worker
  limiter: {
    max:      100,    // max 100 jobs processed per...
    duration: 1000,   // ...1 second (rate limiting)
  },
})
export class EmiCollectionConsumer extends WorkerHost {
  private readonly logger = new Logger(EmiCollectionConsumer.name);

  constructor(
    private readonly nachService:    INachServicePort,
    private readonly loanDao:        ILoanDao,
    private readonly emiDao:         IEmiDao,
    private readonly notifProducer:  NotificationProducer,
    private readonly metrics:        MetricsService,
  ) {
    super();
  }

  async process(job: Job<EmiCollectionJobPayload>): Promise<EmiCollectionResult> {
    const { loanId, mandateId, amountInPaise, idempotencyKey } = job.data;

    this.logger.log({
      type:           "emi_collection_started",
      jobId:          job.id,
      loanId,
      attemptsMade:   job.attemptsMade,
      idempotencyKey,
    });

    // Idempotency: skip if already collected for this month
    const alreadyCollected = await this.emiDao.existsByIdempotencyKey(idempotencyKey);
    if (alreadyCollected) {
      this.logger.warn(`EMI already collected — skipping duplicate job: ${idempotencyKey}`);
      return { status: "ALREADY_COLLECTED", loanId };
    }

    try {
      // Update job progress — visible in BullMQ dashboard
      await job.updateProgress(10);

      // Debit via NACH
      const result = await this.nachService.debit({
        mandateId,
        amountInPaise,
        idempotencyKey,   // forward to NACH for gateway-level dedup
      });

      await job.updateProgress(60);

      // Record EMI payment — atomic with idempotency key
      await this.emiDao.insertIdempotent({
        idempotencyKey,
        loanId,
        amountInPaise,
        gatewayRef:  result.transactionId,
        collectedAt: new Date(),
        status:      "SUCCESS",
      });

      // Update loan outstanding balance
      await this.loanDao.recordEmiPayment(loanId, amountInPaise);

      await job.updateProgress(90);

      // Enqueue notification — fire and forget (non-critical)
      await this.notifProducer.enqueue({
        customerId:  job.data.customerId,
        channel:     "sms",
        templateId:  "emi_collected_success",
        variables:   {
          amount:  (amountInPaise / 100).toLocaleString("en-IN"),
          loanId:  loanId.slice(-6),
        },
        priority: "normal",
      });

      await job.updateProgress(100);

      this.metrics.increment("emi.collection.success");
      return { status: "SUCCESS", loanId, transactionId: result.transactionId };
    } catch (err: any) {
      this.logger.error({
        type:         "emi_collection_failed",
        jobId:        job.id,
        loanId,
        error:        err.message,
        attemptsMade: job.attemptsMade,
        willRetry:    job.attemptsMade < (job.opts.attempts ?? 1) - 1,
      });

      this.metrics.increment("emi.collection.failure", {
        errorType: err.constructor.name,
      });

      // Throw to signal BullMQ to retry (if attempts remain) or move to failed queue
      throw err;
    }
  }

  // BullMQ lifecycle hooks
  @OnWorkerEvent("completed")
  onCompleted(job: Job): void {
    this.metrics.histogram("emi.collection.duration_ms", job.finishedOn! - job.processedOn!);
  }

  @OnWorkerEvent("failed")
  onFailed(job: Job, err: Error): void {
    if (job.attemptsMade >= (job.opts.attempts ?? 1)) {
      // Final failure — alert ops
      this.alertService.escalate({
        type:    "EMI_COLLECTION_FINAL_FAILURE",
        jobId:   job.id,
        loanId:  job.data.loanId,
        error:   err.message,
      });
    }
  }
}
```

```typescript
@Controller()
export class LoanEventConsumer {
  private readonly logger = new Logger(LoanEventConsumer.name);

  constructor(
    private readonly notifService:       NotificationService,
    private readonly processedEventDao:  ProcessedEventDao,
    private readonly metrics:            MetricsService,
  ) {}

  @EventPattern("loan.disbursed")
  async handleLoanDisbursed(
    @Payload()  payload: LoanDisbursedEvent,
    @Ctx()      context: KafkaContext,
  ): Promise<void> {
    const message   = context.getMessage();
    const partition = context.getPartition();
    const offset    = message.offset;
    const messageId = `loan.disbursed:${partition}:${offset}`;

    const start = Date.now();
    this.logger.log({
      type:      "kafka_event_received",
      topic:     "loan.disbursed",
      messageId,
      eventId:   payload.eventId,
      loanId:    payload.loanId,
      partition,
      offset,
    });

    // Idempotency: skip if already processed
    const claimed = await this.processedEventDao.claimMessage(messageId, "loan.disbursed");
    if (!claimed) {
      this.logger.warn(`Duplicate event skipped: ${messageId}`);
      this.metrics.increment("kafka.event.duplicate", { topic: "loan.disbursed" });
      return;   // Acknowledge without processing — prevent infinite retry
    }

    try {
      await this.notifService.sendDisbursementConfirmation({
        customerId:     payload.customerId,
        loanId:         payload.loanId,
        amountInPaise:  payload.principalInPaise,
        disbursedAt:    new Date(payload.disbursedAt),
      });

      await this.processedEventDao.markSuccess(messageId, "loan.disbursed");

      this.metrics.histogram("kafka.event.processing_ms", Date.now() - start, {
        topic:  "loan.disbursed",
        status: "success",
      });
    } catch (err: any) {
      // Release claim: allow Kafka to redeliver (consumer group rebalance or offset reset)
      await this.processedEventDao.releaseClaim(messageId, "loan.disbursed");
      this.metrics.increment("kafka.event.processing_error", { topic: "loan.disbursed" });
      this.logger.error({
        type:    "kafka_event_processing_failed",
        topic:   "loan.disbursed",
        eventId: payload.eventId,
        error:   err.message,
      });
      throw err;   // rethrow → Kafka will not advance the offset → message redelivered
    }
  }

  // Register all topics this consumer subscribes to (required for NestJS Kafka microservice)
  async onModuleInit(): Promise<void> {
    // No-op for event pattern consumers — topics registered via @EventPattern decorators
  }
}
```

**Q8. How do you implement back-pressure — preventing producers from overwhelming consumers?**
A:

```typescript
// Back-pressure: when consumers can't keep up with producers,
// the queue grows unboundedly until memory/disk is exhausted.
// Multiple strategies handle this at different layers.

// ─── Strategy 1: Queue depth monitoring with producer throttling ──────────────

@Injectable()
export class BackPressureAwareProducer {
  private readonly MAX_QUEUE_DEPTH     = 100_000;
  private readonly BACKOFF_THRESHOLD   = 80_000;   // start slowing at 80% capacity

  constructor(
    @InjectQueue(QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION) private readonly queue: Queue,
    private readonly metrics: MetricsService,
  ) {}

  async enqueueWithBackPressure(job: EmiCollectionJobPayload): Promise<void> {
    const waiting = await this.queue.getWaitingCount();
    const delayed = await this.queue.getDelayedCount();
    const total   = waiting + delayed;

    this.metrics.gauge("queue.depth", total, { queue: QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION });

    if (total > this.MAX_QUEUE_DEPTH) {
      throw new ServiceUnavailableException(
        `Queue depth ${total} exceeds maximum ${this.MAX_QUEUE_DEPTH} — shedding load`
      );
    }

    if (total > this.BACKOFF_THRESHOLD) {
      // Soft back-pressure: add delay proportional to queue saturation
      const saturation = (total - this.BACKOFF_THRESHOLD) / (this.MAX_QUEUE_DEPTH - this.BACKOFF_THRESHOLD);
      const delayMs    = Math.floor(saturation * 5000);   // up to 5s delay per job
      await this.sleep(delayMs);
    }

    await this.queue.add("collect-emi", job, { attempts: 3 });
  }

  private sleep(ms: number): Promise<void> {
    return new Promise(resolve => setTimeout(resolve, ms));
  }
}

// ─── Strategy 2: Kafka producer with explicit blocking on full topic ───────────

// Kafka producers buffer messages locally before sending.

      await job.moveToDelayed(Date.now() + 120_000);  // retry in 2 minutes
      return { status: "DEFERRED", loanId: job.data.loanId };
```

**Q9. How do you implement a dead letter queue (DLQ) strategy for messages that fail repeatedly?**
A:

```typescript
// A Dead Letter Queue receives messages that have exhausted all retry attempts.
// DLQ messages require human review or automated remediation — they must not be lost.

// ─── BullMQ: automatic DLQ via failed queue ───────────────────────────────────

// BullMQ keeps failed jobs in the "failed" state automatically.
// A separate worker processes the DLQ manually or on a schedule.

@Injectable()
export class DlqProcessorService {
  constructor(
    @InjectQueue(QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION) private readonly queue: Queue,
    private readonly alertService:                              AlertService,
    private readonly emiCollectionProducer:                     EmiCollectionProducer,
  ) {}

  // Run every 15 minutes — review failed jobs
  @Cron("*/15 * * * *")
  async processDlq(): Promise<void> {
    const failedJobs = await this.queue.getFailed(0, 100);

    for (const job of failedJobs) {
      const payload  = job.data as EmiCollectionJobPayload;
      const failReason = job.failedReason;

      // Classify failure type
      const isTransient  = this.isTransientFailure(failReason);
      const isMandate    = failReason?.includes("MANDATE_CANCELLED");
      const isInsufficientFunds = failReason?.includes("INSUFFICIENT_FUNDS");

      if (isTransient && job.attemptsMade < 10) {
        // Transient: retry after longer delay
        await job.retry("failed");
      } else if (isMandate) {
        // Mandate cancelled: mark loan for manual review; don't retry
        await this.markForManualReview(payload.loanId, "MANDATE_CANCELLED", failReason);
        await job.remove();
      } else if (isInsufficientFunds) {
        // Insufficient funds: standard NPA process
        await this.initiateNpaProcess(payload.loanId);
        await job.remove();
      } else {
        // Unknown failure: escalate to ops team
        await this.alertService.escalate({
          type:      "EMI_COLLECTION_DLQ",
          jobId:     job.id,
          loanId:    payload.loanId,
          error:     failReason,
          attempts:  job.attemptsMade,
        });
        // Do NOT remove from DLQ — keep for manual inspection
      }
    }
  }

  private isTransientFailure(reason: string | undefined): boolean {
    const TRANSIENT_PATTERNS = [
      "ECONNREFUSED", "ECONNRESET", "ETIMEDOUT",
      "socket hang up", "network error",
    ];
    return TRANSIENT_PATTERNS.some(p => reason?.includes(p));
  }
}

// ─── Kafka: DLQ via a separate topic ─────────────────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class KafkaDlqProducer {
  constructor(@Inject("KAFKA_PRODUCER") private readonly client: ClientKafka) {}

  async sendToDlq(
    originalTopic:   string,
    payload:         unknown,
    error:           Error,
    originalHeaders: Record<string, string>,
    attempts:        number,
  ): Promise<void> {
    const dlqTopic = `${originalTopic}.dlq`;

    await this.client.emit(dlqTopic, {
      value: JSON.stringify({
        originalPayload: payload,
        originalTopic,
        error:           error.message,
        errorStack:      error.stack,
        attempts,
        failedAt:        new Date().toISOString(),
      }),
      headers: {
        ...originalHeaders,
        "x-dlq-original-topic": originalTopic,
        "x-dlq-error":          error.message,
        "x-dlq-attempts":       String(attempts),
        "x-dlq-failed-at":      new Date().toISOString(),
      },
    }).toPromise();
  }
}

// Consumer with DLQ routing:
@EventPattern("payment.collected")
async handlePaymentCollected(
  @Payload() payload: PaymentCollectedEvent,
  @Ctx()     context: KafkaContext,
): Promise<void> {
  let lastError: Error | null = null;

  for (let attempt = 0; attempt <= 3; attempt++) {
    try {
      await this.processPayment(payload);
      return;
    } catch (err: any) {
      lastError = err;
      if (attempt < 3) await this.sleep(Math.pow(2, attempt) * 1000);
    }
  }

  // All retries exhausted — send to DLQ
  await this.dlqProducer.sendToDlq(
    "payment.collected",
    payload,
    lastError!,
    this.extractHeaders(context.getMessage().headers),
    4,
  );
  // DO NOT throw — we don't want Kafka to redeliver (it's in the DLQ now)
}
```

**Q11. How does the Producer-Consumer pattern structure a complete EMI due-date notification and collection system?**
A:

```typescript
// Complete flow: 1st of each month
//
// Cron Job (Producer)
//   → EMI Collection Queue
//     → NACH Debit Worker (Consumer)
//       → On success: EMI Success Queue
//         → Loan Balance Update Worker
//         → Ledger Entry Worker
//         → SMS Notification Worker
//       → On failure: EMI Failure Queue
//         → NPA Assessment Worker
//         → Retry Worker (3 attempts over 3 days)
//         → DLQ: Manual Review Queue

// ─── Cron producer ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class EmiDueDateCronProducer {
  constructor(
    private readonly loanDao:         ILoanDao,
    private readonly emiProducer:     EmiCollectionProducer,
    private readonly metrics:         MetricsService,
  ) {}

  @Cron("0 6 1 * *")   // 6:00 AM on the 1st of every month (IST)
  async produceEmiCollectionJobs(): Promise<void> {
    const today     = new Date();
    const batchId   = `emi-batch:${today.toISOString().slice(0, 10)}`;

    this.logger.log(`Starting EMI collection batch: ${batchId}`);

    // Stream loans in chunks — don't load 100,000 loans into memory at once
    let offset = 0;
    const chunkSize = 500;
    let totalEnqueued = 0;

    while (true) {
      const loans = await this.loanDao.findDueForCollection(today, chunkSize, offset);
      if (loans.length === 0) break;

      await this.emiProducer.enqueueEmiCollection({
        loans,
        dueDate: today,
        batchId,
      });

      totalEnqueued += loans.length;
      offset        += chunkSize;

      this.logger.log(`Enqueued ${totalEnqueued} EMI collection jobs`);
      this.metrics.gauge("emi.batch.enqueued", totalEnqueued, { batchId });

      // Small delay between chunks — don't spike Redis
      await this.sleep(100);
    }

    this.logger.log(`EMI batch complete: ${totalEnqueued} jobs enqueued`);
  }

  private sleep(ms: number): Promise<void> {
    return new Promise(resolve => setTimeout(resolve, ms));
  }
}

// ─── Downstream pipeline: on collection success ───────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class EmiSuccessConsumer extends WorkerHost {

    // All three operations in parallel — they're independent
    await Promise.all([

        templateId: "emi_receipt",
        variables: {
          amount: (amountInPaise / 100).toLocaleString("en-IN"),
          ref:    gatewayRef,
        },
      }),
    ]);
  }
}

// ─── Downstream pipeline: on collection failure ───────────────────────────────

@Processor(QUEUES.EMI_FAILURE, { concurrency: 10 })
export class EmiFailureConsumer extends WorkerHost {
  async process(job: Job<EmiFailurePayload>): Promise<void> {
    const { loanId, customerId, reason, attemptNumber } = job.data;

    // Classify failure
    if (reason === "INSUFFICIENT_FUNDS" && attemptNumber < 3) {

      await this.emiCollectionQueue.add("collect-emi", {

        customerId,

        attemptNumber:  attemptNumber + 1,
        idempotencyKey: `${job.data.idempotencyKey}:retry-${attemptNumber}`,
      }, {
        delay:    24 * 60 * 60 * 1000,   // 24 hours
        attempts: 1,
      });

      await this.notificationQueue.add("send-payment-failure-warning", {
        customerId,
        templateId: "emi_failed_retry_tomorrow",
        variables:  { attempts_remaining: String(3 - attemptNumber) },

      await this.npaAssessmentQueue.add("assess-npa", {
        loanId, customerId, reason,

      await this.notificationQueue.add("send-npa-warning", {
        customerId,
        templateId: "emi_failed_npa_warning",
        variables:  {},
        priority:   "critical",
      });
    }
  }
}
```

**Q12. How does the Producer-Consumer pattern handle ordered processing — ensuring events for the same loan are processed in sequence?**
A:

```typescript
// Problem: two events for the same loan arrive out of order
// Event 1: EmiRepaid (loanId: loan-001, outstanding: 45,000)
// Event 2: LoanStatusChanged (loanId: loan-001, status: CLOSED)
// If Event 2 is processed before Event 1: loan shows CLOSED with wrong outstanding balance

// ─── Kafka: partition key guarantees order within a partition ──────────────────

// All events for a given loanId are routed to the same Kafka partition
// via the partition key. Within a partition, messages are ordered.

// Producer: use loanId as the partition key
await producer.send({
  topic: "loan.events",
  messages: [{
    key:   event.loanId,          // ← loanId as partition key
    value: JSON.stringify(event),
  }],
});

// Consumer: single consumer per partition ensures sequential processing
// (Kafka assigns one partition to one consumer within a consumer group)
// Within a partition, messages are processed in order automatically.

// ─── BullMQ: serial queue per loan using dynamic queue names ──────────────────

// Challenge: BullMQ processes jobs concurrently by default.
// For ordering, use a per-loan queue or a serial job chain.

@Injectable()
export class OrderedLoanEventProducer {
  private readonly queues = new Map<string, Queue>();

  constructor(
    private readonly connection: Redis,
  ) {}

  async enqueue(event: LoanEvent): Promise<void> {
    // Get or create a queue specifically for this loan
    const loanQueue = this.getOrCreateLoanQueue(event.loanId);

    await loanQueue.add(event.eventType, event, {
      // Chain jobs: each waits for the previous to complete
      // (requires BullMQ Pro for built-in job chaining)
    });
  }

  private getOrCreateLoanQueue(loanId: string): Queue {
    if (!this.queues.has(loanId)) {
      // One queue per loan — concurrency: 1 means strict serial processing
      const queue = new Queue(`loan-events:${loanId}`, { connection: this.connection });
      this.queues.set(loanId, queue);
    }
    return this.queues.get(loanId)!;
  }
}

// ─── Alternative: sequencing via a sequence number in the event ───────────────

// Each event carries a sequence number.
// The consumer checks: "have I processed sequence N-1 before processing N?"
// If not, it delays the current event.

@EventPattern("loan.events")
async handleLoanEvent(
  @Payload() event: LoanEvent,
  @Ctx()     context: KafkaContext,
): Promise<void> {
  const expectedSeq = (await this.seqTracker.getLastProcessed(event.loanId)) + 1;

  if (event.sequenceNumber < expectedSeq) {
    // Already processed — skip
    return;
  }

  if (event.sequenceNumber > expectedSeq) {
    // Future event arrived early — delay processing
    await this.eventBuffer.store(event);
    throw new Error(`Out-of-order event: expected ${expectedSeq}, got ${event.sequenceNumber}`);
    // Kafka will redeliver — buffer will have the missing events by then
  }

  // Process in order
  await this.processEvent(event);
  await this.seqTracker.updateLastProcessed(event.loanId, event.sequenceNumber);

  // Process any buffered future events that are now in order
  await this.processPendingBuffered(event.loanId, event.sequenceNumber + 1);
}
```

**Q13. How do you implement the Outbox pattern with the Producer-Consumer pattern to guarantee event delivery?**
A:

```typescript
// Problem: a service commits a DB transaction and then publishes to Kafka.
// If the service crashes between commit and publish, the event is lost.
// The Outbox pattern makes the event publish atomic with the DB transaction.

// ─── Outbox table ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

// CREATE TABLE outbox_events (
//   id              UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
//   aggregate_type  VARCHAR(100) NOT NULL,
//   aggregate_id    VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
//   event_type      VARCHAR(255) NOT NULL,
//   payload         JSONB        NOT NULL,
//   published       BOOLEAN      NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE,
//   published_at    TIMESTAMP,
//   created_at      TIMESTAMP    NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
//   schema_version  VARCHAR(20)  NOT NULL DEFAULT '1.0.0'
// );
// CREATE INDEX idx_outbox_unpublished ON outbox_events(created_at) WHERE published = FALSE;

// ─── Write event to outbox inside the business transaction ────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class LoanDisbursementService {
  async disburse(loanId: string, token: string): Promise<void> {
    await this.db.transaction(async (manager) => {
      // Business operation
      const loan = await manager.findOne(LoanEntity, { where: { id: loanId }, lock: { mode: "pessimistic_write" } });
      loan.status     = "DISBURSED";
      loan.disbursedAt = new Date();
      await manager.save(loan);

      // Write event to outbox — SAME transaction — atomic with loan update
      await manager.insert(OutboxEventEntity, {
        aggregateType: "Loan",
        aggregateId:   loanId,
        eventType:     "loan.disbursed",
        schemaVersion: "1.0.0",
        payload:       {
          eventId:          crypto.randomUUID(),
          loanId,
          customerId:       loan.customerId,
          principalInPaise: loan.principalInPaise,
          disbursedAt:      new Date().toISOString(),
        },
        published: false,
      });

      // Both committed or both rolled back — event cannot be lost
    });
  }
}

// ─── Outbox relay — polls and publishes ──────────────────────────────────────

@Injectable()
export class OutboxRelayService {
  private readonly logger = new Logger(OutboxRelayService.name);
  private isRunning = false;

  constructor(
    private readonly outboxDao:  OutboxEventDao,
    private readonly producer:   KafkaProducer,
    private readonly metrics:    MetricsService,
  ) {}

  // Run every second — near-real-time delivery
  @Cron("* * * * * *")
  async relay(): Promise<void> {
    if (this.isRunning) return;  // prevent concurrent relay runs
    this.isRunning = true;

    try {
      const events = await this.outboxDao.findUnpublished(100);
      if (events.length === 0) return;

      // Batch send to Kafka — more efficient than one message at a time
      const messages = events.map(event => ({
        key:   event.aggregateId,   // partition key for ordering
        value: JSON.stringify(event.payload),
        headers: {
          "event-type":    event.eventType,
          "aggregate-type": event.aggregateType,
          "schema-version": event.schemaVersion,
          "outbox-id":     event.id,
        },
      }));

      await this.producer.sendBatch({
        topicMessages: [{
          topic:    events[0].eventType,    // route by event type
          messages,
        }],
      });

      // Mark as published — only AFTER Kafka acknowledges
      await this.outboxDao.markPublished(events.map(e => e.id));

      this.metrics.increment("outbox.events.published", events.length);
      this.logger.debug(`Relayed ${events.length} outbox events`);
    } catch (err: any) {
      this.logger.error(`Outbox relay failed: ${err.message}`);
      this.metrics.increment("outbox.relay.error");
      // Events remain unpublished → next cron tick will retry
    } finally {
      this.isRunning = false;
    }
  }
}
```

```typescript
      const failureRate = total > 0 ? (failed / total) * 100 : 0;
      this.metrics.gauge("queue.failure_rate", failureRate, { queue: queueName });

  async monitorConsumerLag(): Promise<void> {

    await admin.connect();

    const consumerGroups = [
      "notification-service-group",
      "ledger-service-group",
      "risk-service-group",

    for (const groupId of consumerGroups) {
      const offsets = await admin.fetchOffsets({ groupId, topics: ["loan.disbursed", "payment.collected"] });

          }

          // Alert: consumer has stopped processing (lag growing)
          // Tracked by comparing lag to previous reading — if lag grew by > 1000 in 1 minute,
          // the consumer may be stuck
        }
      }
    }
```

**Q15. How do you handle schema evolution in a Producer-Consumer system without breaking existing consumers?**
A:

```typescript
// Schema evolution is one of the hardest problems in event-driven systems.
// A Kafka topic may be consumed by 5 services, each deployed independently.
// Adding a field breaks no consumer; removing or renaming a field breaks all consumers
// that read that field.

// ─── Strategy 1: Additive-only changes (backward compatibility) ───────────────

// Safe changes:
// - Add a new optional field with a default value
// - Add a new enum value to an existing enum field
// - Add a new message type to an existing topic

// Breaking changes (never do without a migration plan):
// - Remove a field consumers read
// - Rename a field
// - Change a field's type
// - Change a field from optional to required

// ─── Strategy 2: Schema versioning ───────────────────────────────────────────

// Every event carries a schemaVersion field.
// Consumers check the version and apply the appropriate parsing logic.

@EventPattern("loan.disbursed")
async handleLoanDisbursed(@Payload() rawPayload: unknown): Promise<void> {
  const envelope = rawPayload as { schemaVersion: string; [key: string]: unknown };

  let event: LoanDisbursedEventV2;

  switch (envelope.schemaVersion) {
    case "1.0.0":
      // Migrate v1 → v2: v1 used "amount" (rupees), v2 uses "principalInPaise"
      const v1 = rawPayload as LoanDisbursedEventV1;
      event = {
        ...v1,

      event = rawPayload as LoanDisbursedEventV2;
  }
```

## Section 4: Pitfalls & Anti-patterns

**Q16. What is the "poison pill" message problem, and how do you handle it?**
A:

```typescript
// A poison pill is a message that always causes the consumer to fail —
// due to a malformed payload, an impossible business condition, or a bug
// in the consumer's processing logic.
// Without handling, the consumer retries indefinitely, blocking all subsequent messages.

// Symptoms:
// - Kafka consumer lag growing without bound for one partition
// - BullMQ worker stuck on one job repeatedly
// - Error logs show the same message ID failing thousands of times

// ─── Detection ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

@EventPattern("loan.disbursed")
async handleLoanDisbursed(
  @Payload() payload: LoanDisbursedEvent,
  @Ctx()     context: KafkaContext,
): Promise<void> {
  const message = context.getMessage();
  const msgKey  = `${context.getTopic()}:${context.getPartition()}:${message.offset}`;

  // Track failure count per message
  const failCount = await this.redis.incr(`msg:fail:${msgKey}`);
  await this.redis.expire(`msg:fail:${msgKey}`, 86_400);  // 24h TTL

  if (failCount > 5) {
    // Poison pill detected — skip and send to DLQ
    this.logger.error(`Poison pill detected: ${msgKey} has failed ${failCount} times`);

    await this.dlqProducer.sendToDlq(
      context.getTopic(),
      payload,
      new Error(`Poison pill: failed ${failCount} times`),
      this.extractHeaders(message.headers),
      failCount,
    );

    // Return without throwing — advances Kafka offset past the poison pill
    await this.redis.del(`msg:fail:${msgKey}`);
    return;
  }

  try {
    await this.processLoanDisbursed(payload);
    await this.redis.del(`msg:fail:${msgKey}`);  // clear on success
  } catch (err) {
    throw err;  // Kafka will redeliver; failure counter increments
  }
}

// ─── BullMQ: poison pill via max attempts + DLQ ───────────────────────────────

@Processor(QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION, { concurrency: 10 })
export class EmiCollectionConsumer extends WorkerHost {
  @OnWorkerEvent("failed")
  async onFailed(job: Job, err: Error): Promise<void> {
    if (job.attemptsMade >= (job.opts.attempts ?? 3)) {
      // Job exhausted all attempts — it's a poison pill or a permanent failure
      await this.dlqService.moveToManualReview({
        jobId:     job.id!,
        queue:     QUEUES.EMI_COLLECTION,
        payload:   job.data,
        lastError: err.message,
        attempts:  job.attemptsMade,
      });
    }
  }
}
```

**Q17. What is the "unbounded retry" anti-pattern, and how does it cascade into system failure?**
A:

```typescript
// Unbounded retry: a consumer that retries failed messages indefinitely
// causes queue buildup, repeated hammering of failing downstream services,
// and alert fatigue that masks the real problem.

// ❌ Anti-pattern: retry without limit or backoff
@EventPattern("payment.collected")
async handlePaymentCollected(@Payload() payload: PaymentCollectedEvent): Promise<void> {
  while (true) {
    try {
      await this.loanService.updateBalance(payload.loanId, payload.amountInPaise);
      return;
    } catch (err) {
      // Infinite retry — if loanService is down, this blocks the Kafka partition forever
      await this.sleep(1000);
    }
  }
}

// ✅ Bounded retry with exponential backoff and DLQ
@EventPattern("payment.collected")
async handlePaymentCollected(@Payload() payload: PaymentCollectedEvent): Promise<void> {
  const MAX_ATTEMPTS = 5;
  let lastError: Error | null = null;

  for (let attempt = 0; attempt < MAX_ATTEMPTS; attempt++) {
    try {
      await this.loanService.updateBalance(payload.loanId, payload.amountInPaise);
      return;
    } catch (err: any) {
      lastError = err;
      // Don't retry on client errors — they won't fix themselves
      if (this.isClientError(err)) throw err;
      // Exponential backoff: 1s, 2s, 4s, 8s, 16s
      if (attempt < MAX_ATTEMPTS - 1) {
        await this.sleep(Math.pow(2, attempt) * 1000);
      }
    }
  }

  // All retries exhausted — send to DLQ, advance Kafka offset
  await this.dlqProducer.sendToDlq("payment.collected", payload, lastError!, {}, MAX_ATTEMPTS);
}

// ─── The cascade: why unbounded retry is dangerous ────────────────────────────

// If the loan service is down for 5 minutes (300 seconds):
// Each payment.collected consumer instance is stuck retrying indefinitely.
// Kafka partitions are blocked — new messages pile up behind the stuck ones.
// After 5 minutes: 50,000 * 5 min * 10 consumers = enormous retry volume.
// When loan service recovers: 50,000 simultaneous retry attempts → DDoS of the recovering service.
// Bounded retry with DLQ: 50,000 messages in DLQ, loan service recovers normally,
// DLQ processor drains the queue at a controlled rate.
```

**Q18. What is the "consumer group imbalance" problem, and when does it degrade throughput?**
A:

```typescript
// Kafka assigns partitions to consumers in a group.
// If partitions < consumers: some consumers are idle (wasteful).
// If one consumer is slow: it holds partitions others could process faster.

// ─── Example: 3 partitions, 5 consumers in group ─────────────────────────────

// Kafka assigns:
// Consumer 1 → Partition 0
// Consumer 2 → Partition 1
// Consumer 3 → Partition 2
// Consumer 4 → IDLE
// Consumer 5 → IDLE
// Adding consumer 4 and 5 added cost with no throughput benefit.

// Rule: for N partitions, use exactly N consumers per group (or fewer).
// Scale consumers by adding partitions to the topic (up to a practical limit).

// ─── Rebalancing storm ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

// When a consumer joins or leaves, Kafka triggers a rebalance:
// all consumers in the group pause processing while partitions are reassigned.
// Frequent pod restarts → frequent rebalances → near-zero throughput.

// Solution: use Kafka's Cooperative Sticky Assignor (available in recent versions)
// which only reassigns the partitions that need to move, not all of them.

// NestJS Kafka microservice config:
{
  consumer: {
    groupId:                "emi-collection-group",
    sessionTimeout:         45_000,   // increase from default 30s → fewer false-death rebalances
    heartbeatInterval:      3_000,
    maxPollIntervalMs:      300_000,  // 5 min max between polls (for long-running jobs)
    // Cooperative sticky rebalancing — less disruption on scale events
    partitionAssigners:     [PartitionAssigners.roundRobin],
  },
}

// ─── Monitoring consumer health ────────────────────────────────────────────────

// Alert: consumer group has 0 active members
// → All consumers crashed; Kafka lag will grow until they restart

// Alert: consumer group rebalancing more than once per minute
// → Consumers crashing and restarting frequently (memory leak? OOM kill?)

// Alert: one consumer has 10× more partitions than others
// → Partition assignment is skewed; one consumer is a bottleneck
```

**Q20. What is the "at-least-once without idempotency" anti-pattern — and why does it cause financial data corruption?**
A:

```typescript
// At-least-once delivery means every message is eventually processed,
// but it may be processed MORE than once.
// Without idempotency in the consumer, duplicate processing = duplicate side effects.

// ❌ Non-idempotent consumer — double EMI deduction on duplicate message

@EventPattern("payment.collected")
async handlePaymentCollected(@Payload() event: PaymentCollectedEvent): Promise<void> {
  // No idempotency check
  // If this message is delivered twice, the following runs twice:

  // 1. Deducts EMI from outstanding balance — TWICE
  await this.loanDao.deductEmiFromBalance(event.loanId, event.amountInPaise);

  // 2. Creates ledger entry — TWICE (double the bookkeeping entry)
  await this.ledgerDao.createEmiEntry({
    loanId:        event.loanId,
    amountInPaise: event.amountInPaise,
    entryType:     "EMI_REPAYMENT",
  });

  // 3. Sends SMS — TWICE ("Your EMI of ₹5,000 has been collected" × 2)
  await this.smsService.send(event.customerId, "emi_collected");
}

// Real consequence: customer's outstanding balance shows ₹90,000 when it should be ₹95,000.
// Ledger is off by ₹5,000. Customer receives 2 SMSs.
// If the loan auto-closes at ₹0 outstanding, the loan might close prematurely.

// ✅ Idempotent consumer — same message can arrive 10 times; effect is the same as once

@EventPattern("payment.collected")
async handlePaymentCollected(
  @Payload() event: PaymentCollectedEvent,
  @Ctx()     context: KafkaContext,
): Promise<void> {
  const messageId = `${context.getTopic()}:${context.getPartition()}:${context.getMessage().offset}`;

  // Claim the message — fails silently if already claimed (duplicate)
  const claimed = await this.processedEventDao.claimMessage(messageId, "payment.collected");
  if (!claimed) {
    this.logger.warn(`Duplicate message skipped: ${messageId}`);
    return;
  }

  try {
    // Atomic transaction: all three operations succeed together or all fail
    await this.db.transaction(async (manager) => {
      await manager.decrement(LoanEntity, { id: event.loanId }, "outstandingPaise", event.amountInPaise);
      await manager.insert(LedgerEntryEntity, {
        loanId:         event.loanId,
        amountInPaise:  event.amountInPaise,
        entryType:      "EMI_REPAYMENT",
        referenceId:    event.eventId,           // idempotency at DB level too
      });
    });

    await this.processedEventDao.markSuccess(messageId, "payment.collected");

    // SMS is sent outside the transaction — non-critical, acceptable if missed once
    await this.smsQueue.add("send-emi-confirmation", {
      customerId: event.customerId,
      templateId: "emi_collected",
      idempotencyKey: `sms:emi:${event.eventId}`,  // SMS provider deduplicates
    });
  } catch (err) {
    await this.processedEventDao.releaseClaim(messageId, "payment.collected");
    throw err;
  }
}
```

```typescript
// ─── Current state ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

// Topic: "payment.collected"
// Existing consumer groups:
// - "ledger-service-group"       (5 consumers)
```

**Q25. "How do you explain the Producer-Consumer pattern to a product manager who is asking why the EMI collection takes 4 hours instead of happening instantly when customers get their salary at midnight?"**
A: A clear, non-technical explanation:

> "Let me explain what's actually happening and why it's the right design.
>
> We have 800,000 customers with EMIs due on the 1st. At midnight, their salary credits land. At 12:01 AM, if we tried to collect all 800,000 EMIs simultaneously, we'd be sending 800,000 simultaneous requests to the bank's NACH gateway. The NACH gateway — which processes all of India's automated bank debits — has a rate limit. It processes a certain number of transactions per second. If we send 800,000 at once, the gateway rejects most of them as overload, we'd be hammering it, and we'd actually get worse results than if we're orderly about it.
>
> The Producer-Consumer pattern is how we solve this. Think of it like a post office with a sorting room. At midnight, our system creates 800,000 'EMI collection letters' and places them in a large mailroom — that's the queue. The workers in our system — the NACH gateway connectors — then work through these letters at a steady pace: say, 200 per second. At that rate, 800,000 letters are processed in about 67 minutes. But we spread it across 4 hours to be safe, because some will need retries.
>
> The advantages of this design are real: if the NACH gateway has a brief outage at 1 AM, the letters just wait in the mailroom — they don't vanish. When the gateway recovers, the workers pick up where they left off. If we need to process faster, we hire more workers — scale up the server instances. If we need to process certain customers first — VIPs or overdue accounts — we mark their letters as priority and the workers pick those up first.
>
> The alternative — trying to do everything at midnight simultaneously — would be like 800,000 people all trying to pay at the same bank counter at exactly the same second. Nothing gets done; everyone's request is rejected. The queue is what makes the system both reliable and polite to the infrastructure it depends on."

## Quick Reference Table

| Dimension                           | Producer-Consumer Pattern                                                          |
|-------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Category                            | Messaging & Event Patterns                                                         |
| Intent                              | Decouple work generation (producer) from work processing (consumer) via a queue    |
| Core components                     | Producer, Message Broker/Queue, Consumer, Consumer Group                           |
| Three decoupling types              | Temporal (timing), Rate (speed), Spatial (location/knowledge)                     |
| Delivery guarantees                 | At-most-once (fire-forget), At-least-once (default), Exactly-once (transactional) |
| At-least-once requirement           | All consumers must be idempotent — duplicates are possible and must be safe        |
| Competing consumers                 | Multiple consumers in the same group share a queue — each message processed by one |
| Fan-out (Pub-Sub)                   | Multiple consumer groups — each group gets a full independent copy of every message |
| Ordering guarantee                  | Kafka: per-partition order via partition key; no global order across partitions    |
| Back-pressure strategies            | Queue depth monitoring + producer throttle; circuit breaker on consumer; rate limiter |
| Dead letter queue                   | Receives messages that exhausted all retries; requires human review or remediation |
| Poison pill handling                | Track per-message failure count; after N failures, move to DLQ and advance offset |
| Outbox pattern                      | Write event to outbox table inside the business transaction; relay publishes to broker |
| Schema evolution                    | Additive-only changes preferred; version field + migration logic for breaking changes |
| BullMQ use case                     | Single-org task queues; retry + backoff; job priorities; delays; progress tracking |
| Kafka use case                      | Cross-service event streams; fan-out; replay; high throughput; ordering per key    |
| Consumer group imbalance            | Partitions ≤ consumers; excess consumers are idle; scale by adding partitions      |
| Rebalancing mitigation              | Cooperative Sticky Assignor; increase sessionTimeout; increase maxPollIntervalMs  |
| Kafka consumer lag monitoring       | Track lag per partition per consumer group; alert on lag > threshold               |
| Priority queues                     | Separate physical queues per tier (critical/high/normal/low) on separate resources |
| Noisy neighbour prevention          | Separate queues and separate Redis/broker resources per priority tier              |
| Idempotency implementation          | Processed-message table with unique INSERT; check before processing; release on failure |
| Common fintech examples             | EMI collection, loan disbursement notifications, KYC verification, NACH mandate    |
| Unbounded retry danger              | Blocks partitions; cascades into DDoS on recovering services; use bounded retry + DLQ |
| SOLID alignment                     | SRP (producer generates; consumer processes), OCP (add consumers without touching producer), DIP (both depend on queue abstraction) |

**Tip for the Interview:** At the 5 YOE level, Producer-Consumer questions surface as system design and reliability problems — "design the EMI collection system for 1 million customers," "your Kafka consumer lag is 500,000 — how do you diagnose it," or "OTP delivery is being blocked by marketing emails — how do you fix this?" The answers that land demonstrate five things: (1) you know the three decoupling types (temporal, rate, spatial) and can explain concretely why each matters for the specific problem at hand; (2) you understand at-least-once delivery deeply — not just "use an idempotency key" but the specific implementation: processed-message table with atomic INSERT, release-on-failure so retries are possible, advance-offset-without-throwing on DLQ; (3) you can size a system — if NACH gateway handles 250 req/s and we have 1M jobs in 4 hours, you can calculate how many consumer instances and at what concurrency; (4) you handle the failure modes — poison pill detection and DLQ, circuit breaker when downstream is slow, back-pressure when the queue is full; and (5) you can explain the fan-out pattern precisely — same Kafka topic, different consumer group IDs, each group gets its own offset, adding a new consumer requires zero producer changes. In fintech specifically, the at-least-once + idempotency story is where concrete experience shows — every financial event that causes a balance update, a ledger entry, or a charge to a customer must be processed exactly once despite the messaging infrastructure guaranteeing only at-least-once.
