# Consumers: Consumer Groups, Partition Assignment, Rebalancing — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: The Problem — Why Consumer Groups Exist

**Q1. What are the two classic message consumption models, and why do consumer groups unify them?**
A: The two classical models are:
- **Queue (competing consumers)**: A message is delivered to exactly one consumer from a pool of workers. Good for parallelizing work — multiple workers share the load of processing a stream.
- **Pub-Sub (broadcast)**: A message is delivered to every subscriber. Good for fan-out — multiple independent systems each receive a full copy of every message.

Consumer groups give you both in one abstraction. Within a consumer group, partitions are divided among members — competing-consumer semantics. Across consumer groups, every group receives all messages independently — pub-sub semantics. A topic with one consumer group is a queue. The same topic with N consumer groups is a broadcast to N independent queues. No message routing configuration is needed; the group boundary is the unit that determines delivery semantics.

**Q2. What is a consumer group, and what does `group.id` do?**
A: A consumer group is a named set of consumers that cooperate to consume a topic. The `group.id` is the string identifier for the group — all consumers with the same `group.id` are members of the same group and share the partition assignment. Kafka tracks each group's committed offsets in the internal `__consumer_offsets` topic, keyed by `(group.id, topic, partition)`. Two applications consuming the same topic with different `group.id` values each receive the full stream independently — they have separate offset tracking and separate partition assignments.

**Q3. What is the fundamental constraint on partition-to-consumer assignment within a group?**
A: **Each partition is assigned to exactly one consumer within a group at any time.** This is the invariant that makes consumption ordered and deduplicated within a group — two consumers in the same group never read from the same partition concurrently. The consequences:
- **Maximum parallelism = partition count**: If a topic has 12 partitions and your group has 20 consumers, 12 consumers are active and 8 are idle — waiting in case an active member fails.
- **Scaling ceiling**: To increase read parallelism, you must increase the partition count. Adding consumers beyond the partition count has zero throughput benefit and is pure standby capacity.
- **Ordering guarantee scope**: Kafka guarantees ordering within a partition. Since one partition maps to one consumer, a consumer sees its partition's messages in order. Cross-partition ordering is not guaranteed.

## Section 2: Consumer Group Fundamentals

**Q4. What is the Group Coordinator and where does it live?**
A: The Group Coordinator is a specific Kafka broker responsible for managing a consumer group's lifecycle — tracking membership, triggering rebalances, and storing committed offsets. It is not a special-purpose server; it is a regular broker elected for this role. A consumer finds its group's coordinator by:
1. Computing `hash(group.id) mod num_partitions(__consumer_offsets)` to determine which partition of `__consumer_offsets` is responsible for this group.
2. Sending a `FindCoordinator` request to any broker.
3. The broker responds with the leader of that `__consumer_offsets` partition — that broker is the Group Coordinator for this group.

The coordinator role shifts to a different broker if the current coordinator fails, triggered by leader election for the relevant `__consumer_offsets` partition.

**Q5. What is the Group Leader, and how is it different from the Group Coordinator?**
A: The Group Leader is a consumer — the first consumer to successfully join the group in the current generation. The Group Coordinator is a broker. They are distinct entities with distinct responsibilities:
- **Group Coordinator (broker)**: Manages the protocol — receives JoinGroup requests, elects the leader, distributes the assignment, tracks heartbeats, triggers rebalances.
- **Group Leader (consumer)**: Runs the partition assignment algorithm — receives the full member list and metadata from the coordinator, computes which consumer gets which partition, and sends the result back to the coordinator.

This design is deliberate: putting assignment logic on the client (leader) rather than the broker means Kafka can support pluggable, user-defined assignment strategies without modifying broker code. The broker is only an intermediary; it doesn't understand or evaluate the assignment.

**Q6. Walk through the full JoinGroup / SyncGroup protocol from consumer startup to consuming.**
A: Five phases:

1. **FindCoordinator**: Consumer sends `FindCoordinator(group.id)` to any broker. Response identifies the Group Coordinator broker.
2. **JoinGroup**: Consumer sends `JoinGroup` to the coordinator, including its `group.id`, supported assignment strategies, and topic subscriptions. All consumers in the group must complete JoinGroup before the coordinator proceeds. The coordinator elects the first joiner as Group Leader and responds to all members with:
   - To the leader: the full member list with each member's metadata
   - To followers: just their own member ID and the leader's ID
3. **Assignment (leader only)**: The Group Leader runs the assignment strategy locally — no network call. It decides which consumer gets which partition.
4. **SyncGroup**: All members send `SyncGroup` to the coordinator. The leader includes the full assignment map; followers send empty assignments. The coordinator distributes the correct assignment to each member.
5. **Consume**: Each consumer begins fetching from its assigned partitions, sending periodic heartbeats to signal liveness.

**Q7. What is a "generation ID" and why does it matter?**
A: Each rebalance increments a generation ID — a monotonically increasing integer associated with the consumer group's current assignment epoch. Every message the coordinator sends to consumers includes the current generation ID.

The generation ID prevents stale consumers from interfering with the current assignment. If a consumer was slow to process and missed a rebalance, any commits it tries to make with an old generation ID are rejected by the coordinator — the coordinator knows those offsets correspond to a stale assignment. Without the generation ID, a slow consumer rejoining after a rebalance could commit offsets for partitions it no longer owns, corrupting the offset state for the consumer that now legitimately owns that partition.

## Section 3: Partition Assignment Strategies

**Q8. What is the RangeAssignor and what is its known weakness?**
A: RangeAssignor (the older default) works per-topic: for each subscribed topic, it sorts consumers alphabetically, divides the partitions by consumer count, and assigns a contiguous range to each consumer. For topic T with 7 partitions and 3 consumers:
- Consumer 0: partitions 0, 1, 2 (3 partitions)
- Consumer 1: partitions 3, 4 (2 partitions)
- Consumer 2: partitions 5, 6 (2 partitions)

The weakness: if consumers subscribe to multiple topics and partition counts don't divide evenly, the "remainder" partitions are always assigned to the first consumers alphabetically. With 3 topics each having 7 partitions and 3 consumers, Consumer 0 consistently gets the extra partition for every topic — ending up with 9 partitions while Consumer 2 gets 6. This systematic imbalance compounds with more topics.

**Q9. What does RoundRobinAssignor do differently?**
A: RoundRobinAssignor pools all partitions across all subscribed topics, sorts them (by topic name + partition number), and distributes them round-robin across all consumers. For 3 topics × 7 partitions = 21 partitions across 3 consumers: each consumer gets exactly 7 partitions. No systematic front-loading of extra partitions onto early consumers.

Limitation: RoundRobinAssignor doesn't account for existing assignments — on a rebalance, it recalculates from scratch and may shuffle all partitions to different consumers even when minimal movement was possible. A consumer that was processing partitions [0, 3, 6] before the rebalance might end up with [1, 4, 7] after, even though [0, 3, 6] is still a valid balanced assignment.

**Q10. What is the StickyAssignor and what problem does it solve?**
A: StickyAssignor achieves two goals simultaneously:
1. **Balanced distribution**: Equivalent even distribution to RoundRobinAssignor.
2. **Maximum stickiness**: Preserves existing partition assignments as much as possible during a rebalance — only moves partitions that *must* move (to achieve balance after a member joins or leaves).

This matters because partitions aren't always stateless from the consumer's perspective. A stream-processing consumer might maintain an in-memory aggregation cache for its partitions. If StickyAssignor preserves those partition assignments across rebalances, the cache remains warm and useful. With RoundRobinAssignor, the consumer's partitions change on every rebalance, invalidating the cache each time.

The trade-off: StickyAssignor uses the eager (stop-the-world) rebalancing protocol — all consumers still pause and revoke all partitions before any new assignment is applied, even though the resulting assignment changes as few partitions as possible.

**Q11. What is the CooperativeStickyAssignor and how does it differ from StickyAssignor?**
A: CooperativeStickyAssignor (available since Kafka 2.4, default since 3.0) applies the same "sticky" assignment logic but uses the **cooperative incremental rebalancing protocol** instead of the eager protocol. The key difference:

With eager (StickyAssignor): all consumers revoke ALL partitions → wait → get new assignments → resume. Zero consumption during the entire rebalance.

With cooperative (CooperativeStickyAssignor): only the partitions that *need to move* are revoked. Consumers keeping their partitions never stop. The rebalance completes in two rounds — first to identify what moves, second to apply those moves — and only the affected partitions experience a brief gap. The rest of the group continues consuming throughout.

For a 10-consumer group where one consumer joins and takes 1 partition from each existing consumer: eager rebalancing pauses all 11 consumers; cooperative rebalancing only briefly pauses the 10 partitions being transferred, while all other partitions continue processing without interruption.

## Section 4: Rebalancing — Triggers, Cost, and Protocols

**Q12. What are the triggers that initiate a consumer group rebalance?**
A: Six conditions trigger a rebalance:
- **Consumer joins**: A new consumer starts and sends JoinGroup.
- **Consumer leaves (graceful)**: A consumer calls `close()`, sending an explicit LeaveGroup to the coordinator.
- **Consumer dies (session timeout)**: A consumer stops sending heartbeats and the coordinator's `session.timeout.ms` timer expires — the coordinator evicts it and triggers a rebalance.
- **Consumer livelock (max.poll.interval.ms exceeded)**: A consumer is alive (heartbeats are sent by a background thread) but has not called `poll()` within `max.poll.interval.ms` — treated as a processing failure and triggers rebalance.
- **Partition count changes**: The number of partitions in a subscribed topic increases.
- **Subscription changes**: A consumer changes its topic subscription via a pattern-based subscription that now matches a new topic.

**Q13. What is the "stop-the-world" cost of eager rebalancing?**
A: During an eager (non-cooperative) rebalance, every consumer in the group must:
1. Commit its current offsets
2. Revoke **all** its partitions — stop fetching, discard any prefetched data
3. Send JoinGroup and wait for the coordinator to collect all members
4. Wait for the leader to compute assignments
5. Send/receive SyncGroup to get the new assignment
6. Resume fetching from the newly assigned partitions

During steps 2–5, **zero messages are being consumed from any partition in the group**. For a large group or a slow rebalance, this gap can be seconds to tens of seconds — a full processing blackout. Every rebalance trigger (including the completely routine "one new consumer joins") causes this full group-wide pause in eager mode.

The rebalance gap directly causes downstream effects: message lag grows during the pause, consumers restart fetching slightly behind, and any consumer maintaining in-flight processing state must either commit or risk reprocessing on reassignment.

**Q14. How does the cooperative (incremental) rebalancing protocol work in two rounds?**
A: Cooperative rebalancing avoids the full stop by splitting the rebalance into two join-sync cycles:

**Round 1 — Identify what moves:**
- All consumers send JoinGroup (but do NOT revoke any partitions yet — they keep consuming).
- The leader computes the new "ideal" assignment.
- SyncGroup is sent — consumers that need to give up specific partitions receive that list; consumers keeping their partitions receive confirmation.
- Consumers revoke only the specific partitions they were told to give up.

**Round 2 — Assign the freed partitions:**
- The consumers that just revoked their partitions trigger another (smaller) JoinGroup.
- The freed partitions are assigned to the consumers that need them.
- SyncGroup finalizes — the new owners start consuming the transferred partitions.

Throughout both rounds, consumers whose partitions didn't change never stop consuming. The "pause" applies only to the specific partitions being transferred, and only briefly between Round 1 revocation and Round 2 assignment. The group's total throughput dip is proportional to the fraction of partitions being moved, not the full group size.

**Q15. What is a "rebalance storm" and when does it occur?**
A: A rebalance storm is a cascade where one rebalance triggers another before the first completes, keeping the group in a continuous rebalance loop with near-zero throughput. Common causes:
- **Slow consumers causing session timeouts during rebalance**: The rebalance itself takes time. If a consumer's `session.timeout.ms` is shorter than the time required to complete a full group rejoin + assignment, the coordinator evicts consumers mid-rebalance, triggering another rebalance.
- **Frequent deployments in large groups**: Rolling restarts of 100 consumers mean 100 sequential rebalances — each deploy triggers one as a consumer leaves, and another as it rejoins, serializing the entire deployment through the group's rebalance cycle.
- **max.poll.interval.ms set too low**: A slow batch (e.g., a database write taking longer than expected) trips the poll interval, evicting the consumer, triggering a rebalance — the consumer rejoins, takes a slow batch again, trips again.

Fix: set `session.timeout.ms` generously (30–45s), `max.poll.interval.ms` to cover your maximum realistic processing time (5–15 minutes for batch processors), and migrate to CooperativeStickyAssignor to reduce the impact of each individual rebalance.

## Section 5: Consumer Liveness — Timeouts and Failure Detection

**Q16. What are the three timeout parameters that govern consumer liveness, and what does each control?**
A: Three distinct timeouts interact in Kafka consumer liveness detection:

- **`heartbeat.interval.ms`** (default: 3,000 ms): How frequently the consumer background thread sends heartbeat messages to the coordinator. Rule of thumb: set to ≤ 1/3 of `session.timeout.ms`. Heartbeats are sent by a separate thread — they continue even when the main thread is blocked processing a batch.

- **`session.timeout.ms`** (default: 45,000 ms in recent versions): How long the coordinator waits for a heartbeat before declaring the consumer dead and triggering a rebalance. If the consumer's process dies, the OS closes the TCP connection and the coordinator detects the failure faster than `session.timeout.ms` — but if the network is partitioned, the coordinator can only detect failure by timeout.

- **`max.poll.interval.ms`** (default: 300,000 ms = 5 minutes): The maximum time allowed between two consecutive `poll()` calls. If the main processing thread is stuck (a slow downstream write, a GC pause, a deadlock), it won't call `poll()` — but the heartbeat background thread is still alive and sending heartbeats, so `session.timeout.ms` never expires. `max.poll.interval.ms` catches this "livelock" — a consumer that is alive but not making progress. When exceeded, the consumer proactively sends a LeaveGroup and triggers a rebalance.

**Q17. What is the consumer livelock problem, and why does `max.poll.interval.ms` exist as a separate timeout from `session.timeout.ms`?**
A: A livelock occurs when a consumer is technically alive (heartbeats are being sent) but is not processing messages — typically because the main thread is blocked on a slow operation (writing to a database, calling an external API, performing CPU-heavy computation). Without `max.poll.interval.ms`:
- The heartbeat thread signals "I'm alive" to the coordinator indefinitely
- The coordinator never triggers a rebalance
- The consumer's partitions are locked and nobody is consuming them
- Message lag grows unboundedly with no failure signal

`max.poll.interval.ms` is separate from `session.timeout.ms` because the two detect different failure modes: session timeout detects a dead process (no heartbeats at all); poll interval detects a live-but-stuck process (heartbeats present, but processing has stalled). Both are needed, and they operate independently.

**Q18. How should you tune these three timeouts for a consumer that does heavy per-batch processing?**
A: For a consumer that reads a batch of 500 records and writes them to a database (potentially taking 60 seconds per batch):

- **`max.poll.interval.ms`**: Set to the maximum realistic processing time with headroom — e.g., 120,000 ms (2 minutes). If this is exceeded, the consumer is genuinely stuck.
- **`session.timeout.ms`**: Set to detect actual crashes quickly — e.g., 30,000–45,000 ms. This controls crash detection speed and rebalance latency after a failure.
- **`heartbeat.interval.ms`**: Set to `session.timeout.ms / 3` — e.g., 10,000 ms. Three heartbeats per session window gives tolerance for transient network hiccups without false positives.
- **`max.poll.records`**: Also tune this — the number of records per `poll()` call. Reducing from 500 to 100 records per batch means each processing iteration is shorter, less likely to exceed `max.poll.interval.ms`, and allows the consumer to poll (and commit) more frequently.

The common mistake: setting `max.poll.interval.ms` equal to the default 5 minutes without checking whether the processing actually fits within 5 minutes under load. Under GC pressure or a slow downstream service, a batch that normally takes 10 seconds can take 6 minutes, tripping the timeout.

## Section 6: Offset Management and the Rebalancing Interaction

**Q19. What is the `__consumer_offsets` topic and what is a "committed offset"?**
A: `__consumer_offsets` is a Kafka-internal, compacted topic (default 50 partitions) that stores the offset position each consumer group has committed for each `(topic, partition)` pair. A committed offset represents the position the group will start consuming from after a restart, crash recovery, or partition reassignment — it's the "checkpoint."

A committed offset is the offset of the **next message to be consumed**, not the last consumed message. If the consumer has processed messages 0 through 99 and commits offset 100, it will resume from message 100 after any restart. Committing the wrong offset (e.g., committing the offset of the last consumed message rather than +1) causes the last message to be reprocessed on restart — a subtle off-by-one that is a common source of duplicate processing bugs.

**Q20. How does a rebalance interact with uncommitted offsets, and what processing guarantee does this create?**
A: During a rebalance (or on consumer crash), partitions are revoked and reassigned. The new owner of a partition starts consuming from the last **committed** offset for that partition — not from where the previous consumer was in-memory. If the previous consumer had consumed messages 100–199 but only committed through offset 100, the new owner re-reads messages 100–199 from the beginning.

This creates **at-least-once delivery** semantics: messages between the last commit and the rebalance point will be reprocessed. The options:
- **Auto-commit** (`enable.auto.commit=true`, default): offsets are committed every `auto.commit.interval.ms` (default 5s). Simple but creates a fixed window of potential reprocessing on any crash or rebalance.
- **Manual commit after processing** (`commitSync()` / `commitAsync()` after writing to downstream): tighter at-least-once — only committed after downstream write succeeds, minimizing but not eliminating reprocessing.
- **Exactly-once** (`isolation.level=read_committed` + transactional producers): requires Kafka Streams or explicit transactional API; ties the offset commit and the downstream write into one atomic transaction.

**Q21. What is the risk of committing offsets before processing completes?**
A: If you commit an offset before the corresponding messages have been fully processed and written downstream, you risk **at-most-once** semantics: if the consumer crashes after committing but before processing, those messages are gone from the consumer's perspective — the next consumer picks up after the committed offset and never sees them. This is often worse than at-least-once, because data loss is silent and hard to detect. For most use cases, commit after processing (or after downstream write confirmation) is the correct default.

## Section 7: Static Group Membership

**Q22. What is static group membership and what problem does it solve?**
A: By default, each consumer is assigned a dynamic member ID by the coordinator when it joins. When the consumer restarts (for any reason — deploy, crash, OOM kill), it is treated as a brand-new member: it sends JoinGroup with a new member ID, which triggers a rebalance to incorporate the "new" consumer. For a group of 20 consumers doing a rolling deploy, that's 40 rebalances (one leave + one join per consumer), each causing a full group-wide pause in eager mode.

Static group membership (`group.instance.id`) gives a consumer a stable, persistent identity. The consumer configures a `group.instance.id` (any unique string per consumer instance, e.g., `hostname` or `pod_name`). When it rejoins:
- If it rejoins before `session.timeout.ms` expires: the coordinator recognizes the returning instance, reassigns it to its previous partitions, and **does not trigger a rebalance**.
- If it rejoins after `session.timeout.ms`: treated as a new member and a rebalance is triggered.

This makes rolling deploys dramatically cheaper — as long as each consumer restarts within the session timeout window, no rebalances occur.

**Q23. When should you use static group membership, and what's the risk if the timeout is too long?**
A: Use static group membership for:
- **Stateful stream processors**: Consumers that maintain local state (aggregations, join tables) keyed to their assigned partitions. Preserving the partition assignment preserves the cache; a rebalance would invalidate in-memory state and require rebuilding from scratch.
- **Rolling deploys of large consumer groups**: Reduces or eliminates rebalance cost per-deploy, keeping throughput stable during deployment.
- **Consumers with slow startup**: If a consumer takes 30 seconds to initialize (e.g., loading a large local index), static membership means it rejoins as a recognized instance rather than triggering a group-wide pause.

The risk of setting `session.timeout.ms` too long: if a statically-identified consumer actually dies (process killed, node fails), the coordinator doesn't trigger a rebalance until `session.timeout.ms` expires. During that window, the dead consumer's partitions are idle — message lag accumulates on those partitions without recovery. Tune `session.timeout.ms` to balance "don't rebalance on normal restarts" vs. "recover quickly from actual failures."

## Section 8: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q24. "Your Kafka consumer group of 10 consumers is processing an orders topic with 10 partitions. A new business requirement means you need to double processing throughput. What are your options, and what limits each?"**
A: Two levers — partition count and consumer count — interact:

- **Add more consumers (current limit: 10 active)**: You can add 10 more consumers right now, but they'll all be idle. Consumers > partitions = idle excess. No throughput gain without increasing partitions first.
- **Increase partition count to 20**: Allows 20 consumers to run concurrently — doubling parallelism. Caveat: **partition count can only be increased, never decreased** without a full topic recreation. Increasing partitions triggers a rebalance in all consumer groups subscribed to the topic. Existing messages in the topic are not redistributed — existing partitions keep their data; only new messages hash to the expanded range. If consumers depend on key-based partition affinity (e.g., all orders for customer X always go to the same partition), increasing the partition count changes the hash-to-partition mapping for new messages, breaking the affinity for the existing stream.
- **Optimize per-consumer throughput**: Before changing partitions, check if consumers are CPU/IO-bound. If each consumer is only using 10% of CPU, the bottleneck is the processing logic, not parallelism — more partitions won't help.

Recommended path: increase to 20 partitions (accepting the re-keying implication), add 10 consumers to the group, and migrate to CooperativeStickyAssignor to minimize the rebalance disruption during the change.

**Q25. "A consumer in your group regularly triggers rebalances every 3–4 minutes. Heartbeat logs show the session is healthy. What is the likely cause, and how do you diagnose it?"**
A: The session is healthy (heartbeats are present) but rebalances happen on a ~4-minute schedule — this is almost certainly `max.poll.interval.ms` being exceeded. The consumer's main thread is taking too long between `poll()` calls; the consumer proactively sends LeaveGroup (or the coordinator detects it), triggering the rebalance.

Diagnostic steps:
1. Check `max.poll.interval.ms` — if it's at the default 300,000ms (5 min) but rebalances happen at 3–4 min, something upstream is responsible. If it's lower (e.g., 120,000ms), the processing likely exceeds that.
2. Instrument time-per-batch in the processing loop — log `System.currentTimeMillis()` before and after each `poll()` loop iteration. Find the slow batch.
3. Look for: slow downstream database writes, blocking external API calls, large GC pauses (check GC logs), or synchronous HTTP calls in the processing path.

Fixes: reduce `max.poll.records` so each batch is smaller and processes faster; increase `max.poll.interval.ms` to give the consumer more room; move slow I/O to async patterns so `poll()` is called more frequently; address the underlying bottleneck (add a connection pool, cache responses, batch writes).

**Q26. "You have a 50-consumer group running with the default (eager) RangeAssignor. Each rolling deploy takes 30 minutes of degraded throughput. How do you fix this with minimal code changes?"**
A: Two changes — both are consumer configuration, no application logic changes needed:

1. **Switch to `CooperativeStickyAssignor`**: Replace `partition.assignment.strategy=org.apache.kafka.clients.consumer.RangeAssignor` with `CooperativeStickyAssignor`. This eliminates the stop-the-world pause for each leave/join — consumers that aren't being affected by the topology change continue processing uninterrupted. The 50 rebalances during a rolling deploy now each affect only the partitions of the consumer being replaced, not the entire group.

2. **Enable `group.instance.id` (static group membership)**: Set a unique `group.instance.id` per consumer instance (e.g., the pod name). If each consumer restarts within `session.timeout.ms`, the coordinator recognizes the returning instance and skips the rebalance entirely — no join/leave cycle for that consumer. A rolling deploy in a 50-consumer group could complete with zero rebalances if restarts are fast enough.

Combining both: static membership suppresses rebalances on fast restarts; cooperative rebalancing minimizes impact when rebalances do happen (slow restarts, actual failures). The 30-minute degradation window shrinks to near zero.

**Q27. "Your event processing pipeline reads from Kafka and writes results to PostgreSQL. You need to guarantee no duplicate writes even if a consumer crashes mid-batch. Describe the commit strategy."**
A: The goal is exactly-once semantics between Kafka consumption and Postgres writes. Two approaches:

**Approach 1 — Transactional outbox pattern**: Within a single Postgres transaction, write both the business data AND the Kafka offset (`(group_id, topic, partition, offset)`) to a dedicated `kafka_offsets` table. On consumer startup, read the committed offset from Postgres rather than from `__consumer_offsets` — seek the Kafka consumer to that position. If the transaction commits, both the data and offset advance atomically. If it rolls back, neither advances. Disable Kafka auto-commit (`enable.auto.commit=false`) and never call `commitSync()`; Kafka offsets are managed in Postgres, not Kafka.

**Approach 2 — Idempotent writes with deduplication key**: Design each Postgres write to be idempotent — include a deduplication key derived from the Kafka message metadata (e.g., `topic:partition:offset` or a business-level message ID) and use `INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING`. Then commit Kafka offsets manually after the Postgres write succeeds. If a crash causes reprocessing, the re-inserted rows are no-ops. This works without a distributed transaction but requires Postgres schema changes per table.

Approach 1 is cleaner but requires the Postgres `kafka_offsets` table to be read on every consumer startup. Approach 2 is more resilient to large offset gaps but requires every downstream table to have the deduplication key.

**Q28. "Design a consumer group configuration for a payment processing service. Requirements: process each payment exactly once, recover from consumer failure within 10 seconds, and support zero-downtime deploys."**
A: Reconciling these three requirements:

**Exactly-once**: Use transactional writes (Approach 1 from Q27) or idempotent write patterns. Set `enable.auto.commit=false`; commit only after confirmed downstream write. `isolation.level=read_committed` to avoid reading uncommitted transactional messages from upstream producers.

**10-second failure recovery**: `session.timeout.ms=10000` (10s). Consumers must send heartbeats every `10000/3 ≈ 3333ms`, so `heartbeat.interval.ms=3000`. This means a consumer crash is detected and rebalance triggered within 10s. Trade-off: shorter session timeout means a transient network hiccup (brief GC pause, slow DNS) could cause a false eviction — tune GC carefully and monitor false positive rates.

**Zero-downtime deploys**: `group.instance.id` per pod (e.g., `payments-consumer-pod-{pod_name}`). `session.timeout.ms=10000` — if each pod restarts within 10 seconds, no rebalance is triggered. Assignment strategy: `CooperativeStickyAssignor` — if a rebalance is triggered (restart takes longer than 10s), only the affected partitions pause, not the whole group.

**Partition count**: At least 3× the consumer count for flexibility — allows scaling up or taking consumers offline for maintenance without the group hitting idle-consumer ceiling immediately.

## The Core Mental Model

```
Consumer Group = "A team of workers sharing a queue"
  Partition count = max team size (more workers than partitions = idle workers)
  group.id        = team name (different teams each get the full message stream)

Rebalance = "Team restructuring"
  Triggers:  member joins, leaves, crashes, livelock, partition count change
  Eager:     whole team stops, goes to HR, gets new assignments, resumes
  Cooperative: only people changing desks get up; everyone else keeps working

Three timeouts — three different failures they detect:
  heartbeat.interval.ms  → "how often do I check in?"
  session.timeout.ms     → "if I stop hearing from you, I assume you're dead"
  max.poll.interval.ms   → "if you stop consuming, I assume you're stuck"

Offset commit = checkpoint
  Commit AFTER processing  →  at-least-once (reprocessing on failure, no loss)
  Commit BEFORE processing →  at-most-once  (no reprocessing, possible loss)
  Transactional commit     →  exactly-once  (no reprocessing, no loss, higher complexity)

Static membership = "I know you'll be right back"
  Fast restart within session.timeout → no rebalance → zero deployment disruption
```

---

**Tip for the interview:** Consumer group questions at 5 YOE almost always escalate to one of two places: rebalancing costs (why they're expensive, how cooperative rebalancing helps, what triggers them unnecessarily) or offset commit semantics (at-least-once vs exactly-once, how rebalances interact with uncommitted offsets). Know the three timeouts cold — interviewers frequently ask "how does Kafka detect a dead consumer vs. a slow one?" and the session.timeout vs max.poll.interval distinction is the answer. The strongest signal: knowing that consumers > partitions = idle (not more parallelism), and that the partition count is the actual throughput ceiling — "to scale past X consumers, you must first scale the partition count."
