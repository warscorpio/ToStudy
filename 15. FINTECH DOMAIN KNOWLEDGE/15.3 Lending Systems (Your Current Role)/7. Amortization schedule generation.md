# Amortization Schedule Generation — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is an amortization schedule, and what does it represent in a lending system?**
A: An amortization schedule is the complete, installment-by-installment breakdown of a loan repayment — showing for each period how much of the payment goes toward interest, how much reduces the principal, and what the outstanding principal balance is after the payment. It is the contractual repayment plan attached to every term loan.

The schedule serves multiple roles simultaneously:

| Role | Who uses it | What they need |
|---|---|---|
| **Borrower communication** | Customer | "How much do I owe each month and when?" |
| **Collections trigger** | Loan servicing system | Due dates and amounts for NACH debit scheduling |
| **DPD computation** | NPA classification pipeline | Unpaid due dates for days-past-due calculation |
| **Interest accrual engine** | Accounting system | Outstanding principal per day for daily interest accrual |
| **Prepayment base** | Foreclosure / part-payment | Principal outstanding on a given date |
| **Regulatory disclosure** | RBI / borrower | Mandatory under RBI Fair Practices Code for lenders |
| **Income projection** | Finance / ALM | Future interest income by period |

Every row in the schedule is a legally binding commitment — the amounts and dates form part of the loan agreement. This means the schedule generation algorithm must be deterministic (same inputs always produce the same output), reproducible (regenerating the schedule for audit must produce identical results), and immutable once disbursed (changes require an amendment, not a silent overwrite).

**Q2. What are the core mathematical components of an EMI-based amortization schedule?**
A: A standard equated monthly installment (EMI) loan has three fundamental parameters:

- **P** — Principal: the loan amount disbursed.
- **r** — Monthly interest rate: annual rate ÷ 12. For a 12% per annum loan, `r = 0.01`.
- **n** — Number of installments (tenure in months).

**EMI formula (reducing balance method):**

```
EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n
          ──────────────
          (1 + r)^n − 1
```

**Per-installment breakdown (for installment k):**

```
Interest component of EMI_k = Outstanding_principal_(k-1) × r
Principal component of EMI_k = EMI − Interest_k
Outstanding_principal_k = Outstanding_principal_(k-1) − Principal_k
```

Key properties:
- The EMI amount is constant across all installments (equated monthly installment).
- The interest component is highest in the first installment and decreases each month.
- The principal component is lowest in the first installment and increases each month.
- The final outstanding balance must be exactly zero (or within rounding tolerance).

**Why reducing balance matters:**
Most Indian retail loans (home loans, personal loans, auto loans) use the reducing balance (diminishing balance) method — interest is charged only on the outstanding principal, not the original principal. A flat-rate loan (interest charged on original principal throughout) results in a much higher effective interest rate and is increasingly regulated by RBI for transparency reasons.

**Q3. What is the difference between monthly reducing balance, daily reducing balance, and annual reducing balance?**
A: The reducing balance method specifies how frequently the outstanding principal is recomputed for interest calculation:

| Method | Interest computation | Common use case | Effective rate vs stated rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Annual reducing** | Principal reduces once per year | Older home loans; rare today | Highest effective rate |
| **Monthly reducing** | Principal reduces after each EMI payment | Most retail loans (personal, auto, MSME) | Matches stated rate exactly if no gaps |
| **Daily reducing** | Principal reduces every calendar day | Overdraft, credit card, flexi loans | Lowest effective rate; borrower benefits from early payments |

For monthly reducing:
```
Interest for month k = Outstanding_principal_at_start_of_month_k × (annual_rate / 12)
```

For daily reducing:
```
Interest for day d = Outstanding_principal_on_day_d × (annual_rate / 365)
```

The daily reducing method requires a different schedule structure — instead of a fixed EMI, the interest component varies based on the exact number of days in each billing cycle and the actual outstanding balance each day. This is important for loans with irregular payment patterns or early partial payments.

**Q4. What is the first EMI date problem, and why does it cause schedule generation errors in practice?**
A: The first EMI date problem arises because there is often a gap between the loan disbursement date and the first EMI due date that is not exactly one month. This gap creates a "broken period" or "stub period" — a partial interest period that must be handled separately from the standard EMI schedule.

**Example:**
- Disbursement date: March 15
- First EMI date: May 1 (standard practice — first EMI falls on the 1st of the second calendar month after disbursement)
- Stub period: March 15 to April 1 = 17 days of interest not covered by any EMI

Three approaches to handle the stub period:

**Approach 1 — Collect stub interest upfront (most common in Indian retail lending)**
Charge interest for the stub period (March 15 to April 1) at disbursement or with the first EMI. The main schedule starts on April 1 with the full principal as if it were a clean month-start loan.

```typescript
function computeStubInterest(
  principal: bigint,
  annualRate: number,
  disbursementDate: Date,
  firstEmiDate: Date,
): bigint {
  const stubDays = differenceInDays(firstEmiDate, disbursementDate);
  // Stub interest = P × (annual_rate / 365) × stub_days
  const stubInterest = Number(principal) * (annualRate / 365) * stubDays;
  return BigInt(Math.round(stubInterest));
}
```

**Approach 2 — Adjust first EMI (less common)**
The first EMI is higher than subsequent EMIs — it includes the standard EMI plus the stub period interest. All subsequent EMIs are standard.

**Approach 3 — Adjust tenure (rare)**
Add an extra installment to cover the stub interest. Tenure becomes n+1 installments where installment 0 is the stub.

Failing to model the stub period results in: the last EMI having a non-zero residual balance (the loan doesn't fully close), or the final installment being significantly larger than expected (a surprise to the borrower and a regulatory disclosure violation).

**Q5. What is the "last EMI adjustment" problem, and why is it unavoidable?**
A: Floating-point arithmetic and rounding at each installment accumulate small errors across the loan tenure. After computing all n installments using the EMI formula, the outstanding principal at the end is almost never exactly zero — it is typically off by ₹1 to ₹5.

This residual must be absorbed somewhere:

**The standard fix — adjust the last EMI:**
```typescript
function generateSchedule(principal: bigint, annualRate: number, tenureMonths: number): ScheduleRow[] {
  const r = annualRate / 12;
  const emiExact = Number(principal) * r * Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths)
                   / (Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths) - 1);
  const emi = BigInt(Math.round(emiExact));  // rounded EMI for all installments

  const rows: ScheduleRow[] = [];
  let outstanding = principal;

  for (let k = 1; k <= tenureMonths; k++) {
    const interest = BigInt(Math.round(Number(outstanding) * r));
    let principalComponent = emi - interest;

    if (k === tenureMonths) {
      // Last installment: absorb the residual
      // Principal component = exact outstanding balance (not emi - interest)
      principalComponent = outstanding;
      // Last EMI may differ from standard EMI by a few rupees
    }

    const closingBalance = outstanding - principalComponent;
    rows.push({
      installment_number: k,
      emi: k === tenureMonths ? interest + principalComponent : emi,
      interest,
      principal: principalComponent,
      opening_balance: outstanding,
      closing_balance: closingBalance,
    });

    outstanding = closingBalance;
  }

  return rows;
}
```

The last installment adjustment is contractually disclosed in the loan agreement ("the last installment may differ from regular installments due to rounding"). In Indian practice this is standard; regulators accept it as long as the adjusted last EMI is disclosed.

## Section 2: Schedule Generation In Depth

**Q6. How do you implement a production-grade amortization schedule generator in TypeScript, handling rounding, stub periods, and the last-EMI adjustment?**
A: A complete implementation:

```typescript
import Decimal from 'decimal.js';

// Always use Decimal for monetary arithmetic — never native float
Decimal.set({ precision: 28, rounding: Decimal.ROUND_HALF_EVEN });

interface LoanParameters {
  principalPaise:    bigint;     // disbursed amount in paise
  annualRatePercent: number;     // e.g., 18.0 for 18% p.a.
  tenureMonths:      number;     // e.g., 36
  disbursementDate:  Date;
  firstEmiDate:      Date;       // must be >= disbursementDate
  emiDay:            number;     // day of month for all EMIs (e.g., 1, 5, 15)
}

interface ScheduleRow {
  installmentNumber:  number;
  dueDate:            Date;
  openingBalance:     bigint;    // paise
  emi:                bigint;    // paise
  interest:           bigint;    // paise
  principal:          bigint;    // paise
  closingBalance:     bigint;    // paise
  isStub:             boolean;   // true for stub-period row
  isLastInstallment:  boolean;
}

function generateAmortizationSchedule(params: LoanParameters): ScheduleRow[] {
  const {
    principalPaise, annualRatePercent, tenureMonths,
    disbursementDate, firstEmiDate,
  } = params;

  const P = new Decimal(principalPaise.toString());
  const annualRate = new Decimal(annualRatePercent).div(100);
  const r = annualRate.div(12);       // monthly rate

  // Step 1: Compute stub period interest
  const stubDays = differenceInCalendarDays(firstEmiDate, disbursementDate);
  const stubInterestDecimal = P.mul(annualRate).div(365).mul(stubDays);
  const stubInterestPaise = BigInt(stubInterestDecimal.toFixed(0, Decimal.ROUND_HALF_EVEN));

  // Step 2: Compute EMI using the standard formula with Decimal precision
  const onePlusR = r.plus(1);
  const onePlusRPowN = onePlusR.pow(tenureMonths);
  const emiDecimal = P.mul(r).mul(onePlusRPowN).div(onePlusRPowN.minus(1));
  const emiPaise = BigInt(emiDecimal.toFixed(0, Decimal.ROUND_HALF_EVEN));

  const rows: ScheduleRow[] = [];

  // Step 3: Stub row (if stub period exists)
  if (stubDays > 0 && stubInterestPaise > 0n) {
    rows.push({
      installmentNumber: 0,
      dueDate:           firstEmiDate,
      openingBalance:    principalPaise,
      emi:               stubInterestPaise,
      interest:          stubInterestPaise,
      principal:         0n,
      closingBalance:    principalPaise,
      isStub:            true,
      isLastInstallment: false,
    });
  }

  // Step 4: Regular installments
  let outstanding = new Decimal(principalPaise.toString());
  let currentDueDate = firstEmiDate;

  for (let k = 1; k <= tenureMonths; k++) {
    const openingBalance = BigInt(outstanding.toFixed(0));
    const interestDecimal = outstanding.mul(r);
    const interestPaise = BigInt(interestDecimal.toFixed(0, Decimal.ROUND_HALF_EVEN));

    let principalPaise_: bigint;
    let emiThisRow: bigint;
    const isLast = k === tenureMonths;

    if (isLast) {
      // Last installment: close out the exact remaining balance
      principalPaise_ = openingBalance;
      emiThisRow = interestPaise + principalPaise_;
    } else {
      principalPaise_ = emiPaise - interestPaise;
      emiThisRow = emiPaise;
    }

    const closingBalance = openingBalance - principalPaise_;
    outstanding = new Decimal(closingBalance.toString());

    rows.push({
      installmentNumber: k,
      dueDate:           currentDueDate,
      openingBalance,
      emi:               emiThisRow,
      interest:          interestPaise,
      principal:         principalPaise_,
      closingBalance,
      isStub:            false,
      isLastInstallment: isLast,
    });

    currentDueDate = addMonthsOnDay(currentDueDate, 1, params.emiDay);
  }

  // Step 5: Validate — closing balance of last row must be zero
  const lastRow = rows[rows.length - 1];
  if (lastRow.closingBalance !== 0n) {
    throw new Error(
      `Schedule generation error: closing balance is ${lastRow.closingBalance} paise, expected 0`
    );
  }

  return rows;
}

// Add months while preserving the target EMI day
// e.g., Jan 31 + 1 month → Feb 28 (not Feb 31)
function addMonthsOnDay(date: Date, months: number, targetDay: number): Date {
  const next = addMonths(date, months);
  const daysInNextMonth = getDaysInMonth(next);
  const day = Math.min(targetDay, daysInNextMonth);
  return setDate(next, day);
}
```

**Q7. How do you store the amortization schedule in the database, and what constraints ensure integrity?**
A: The schedule is stored as an append-only set of rows — one per installment — that are generated at disbursement and never silently mutated.

```sql
CREATE TABLE loan_repayment_schedule (
  id                  UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  loan_id             UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES loans(id),
  installment_number  INT NOT NULL,
  due_date            DATE NOT NULL,
  opening_balance     BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (opening_balance >= 0),
  emi                 BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (emi > 0),
  interest            BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (interest >= 0),
  principal           BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (principal >= 0),
  closing_balance     BIGINT NOT NULL CHECK (closing_balance >= 0),
  is_stub             BOOLEAN NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE,
  is_last_installment BOOLEAN NOT NULL DEFAULT FALSE,
  -- Payment tracking
  amount_paid         BIGINT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0 CHECK (amount_paid >= 0),
  paid_at             TIMESTAMPTZ,
  status              installment_status NOT NULL DEFAULT 'PENDING',
  -- Immutability: original scheduled values never change; adjustments go in separate columns
  adjusted_emi        BIGINT,          -- set on restructuring; NULL if no adjustment
  adjusted_due_date   DATE,            -- set on moratorium / restructuring
  waived_amount       BIGINT,          -- set on waiver
  generated_at        TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  schedule_version    INT NOT NULL DEFAULT 1,  -- increments on restructuring
  UNIQUE (loan_id, installment_number, schedule_version),
  -- Double-entry check: interest + principal = emi
  CONSTRAINT emi_components_match CHECK (interest + principal = emi)
);

CREATE TYPE installment_status AS ENUM (
  'PENDING', 'PARTIAL', 'PAID', 'OVERDUE', 'WAIVED', 'WRITTEN_OFF'
);

-- Ensure closing balance of row k = opening balance of row k+1
-- Enforced via trigger on INSERT
CREATE OR REPLACE FUNCTION validate_schedule_continuity()
RETURNS TRIGGER AS $$
DECLARE
  prev_closing BIGINT;
BEGIN
  IF NEW.installment_number > 1 THEN
    SELECT closing_balance INTO prev_closing
    FROM loan_repayment_schedule
    WHERE loan_id = NEW.loan_id
      AND installment_number = NEW.installment_number - 1
      AND schedule_version = NEW.schedule_version;

    IF prev_closing IS NOT NULL AND prev_closing <> NEW.opening_balance THEN
      RAISE EXCEPTION
        'Schedule continuity violation: row % opening_balance=% but prev closing=%',
        NEW.installment_number, NEW.opening_balance, prev_closing;
    END IF;
  END IF;
  RETURN NEW;
END;
$$ LANGUAGE plpgsql;

CREATE TRIGGER check_schedule_continuity
  BEFORE INSERT ON loan_repayment_schedule
  FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION validate_schedule_continuity();
```

**Q8. How do you generate due dates correctly across months with different lengths, and what is the "end of month" problem?**
A: Due date generation is deceptively complex. Common failure modes:

**The end-of-month problem:**
If a loan's EMI day is the 31st and the next EMI falls in February (28 days), what is the due date? Three conventions:

| Convention | Rule | Example (Jan 31 + 1 month) |
|---|---|---|
| **Clamp to month end** | Use the last day of the month if the target day doesn't exist | Feb 28 |
| **Carry forward** | Use the 1st of the following month | March 1 |
| **Fixed shift** | Move EMI day to 28th permanently for all months | Feb 28, March 28, ... |

Indian lending practice overwhelmingly uses "clamp to month end." The `addMonthsOnDay` function in Q6 implements this.

**The holiday problem:**
If a computed due date falls on a Sunday or bank holiday, the due date shifts. Two options:

```typescript
async function adjustForHoliday(dueDate: Date): Promise<Date> {
  // Convention 1: Move to previous business day (pre-debit for NACH)
  // Convention 2: Move to next business day (more common; borrower gets extra time)
  let adjusted = dueDate;

  while (isWeekend(adjusted) || await isBankHoliday(adjusted)) {
    adjusted = addDays(adjusted, 1);   // next business day
  }
  return adjusted;
}
```

The chosen convention must be applied consistently and disclosed in the loan agreement. Inconsistency (applying it to some months but not others) creates discrepancies between the schedule and NACH debit dates.

**The month boundary crossing problem:**
Adding exactly 1 month to October 31 gives November 30 (clamped). Adding 1 month to November 30 gives December 30 — but the borrower expects December 31 (end of month). This asymmetry is handled by always adding months from the original EMI day, not from the previous due date:

```typescript
function generateDueDates(firstEmiDate: Date, emiDay: number, count: number): Date[] {
  return Array.from({ length: count }, (_, i) => {
    // Add i months to the first EMI's year/month, then set to emiDay
    const target = addMonths(firstEmiDate, i);
    const daysInMonth = getDaysInMonth(target);
    return setDate(target, Math.min(emiDay, daysInMonth));
    // Always derived from original emiDay — no drift from month to month
  });
}
```

## Section 3: Special Cases

**Q9. How do you handle moratorium periods in an amortization schedule?**
A: A moratorium is a period at the start of the loan (or mid-tenure, as in COVID-19 RBI moratorium) during which the borrower does not make principal repayments. Two types:

**Principal moratorium (EMI holiday on principal only):**
The borrower pays only interest during the moratorium. Principal repayment begins after the moratorium period. Common in education loans (moratorium during course + 6 months) and project finance.

**Full moratorium (no payment at all):**
Neither principal nor interest is paid. Interest accrues and is capitalised (added to principal) at the end of the moratorium. Effective principal for EMI computation = original principal + capitalised moratorium interest. Used during COVID-19 RBI moratorium (March–August 2020).

```typescript
function generateScheduleWithMoratorium(params: LoanParameters & {
  moratoriumMonths: number;
  moratoriumType: 'INTEREST_ONLY' | 'FULL';
}): ScheduleRow[] {
  const { moratoriumMonths, moratoriumType, principalPaise, annualRatePercent } = params;
  const r = annualRatePercent / 100 / 12;
  const rows: ScheduleRow[] = [];

  let outstanding = principalPaise;
  let currentDate = params.firstEmiDate;

  // Phase 1: Moratorium rows
  for (let k = 1; k <= moratoriumMonths; k++) {
    const interest = BigInt(Math.round(Number(outstanding) * r));

    if (moratoriumType === 'INTEREST_ONLY') {
      // Pay interest; principal stays the same
      rows.push({
        installmentNumber: k,
        dueDate: currentDate,
        openingBalance: outstanding,
        emi: interest,
        interest,
        principal: 0n,
        closingBalance: outstanding,
        isStub: false,
        isLastInstallment: false,
      });
    } else {
      // FULL moratorium: no payment; interest capitalised
      outstanding = outstanding + interest;   // capitalise interest into principal
      rows.push({
        installmentNumber: k,
        dueDate: currentDate,
        openingBalance: outstanding - interest,
        emi: 0n,
        interest,
        principal: 0n,
        closingBalance: outstanding,   // balance increased by capitalised interest
        isStub: false,
        isLastInstallment: false,
      });
    }
    currentDate = addMonthsOnDay(currentDate, 1, params.emiDay);
  }

  // Phase 2: Regular amortization on the (possibly capitalised) outstanding balance
  const remainingTenure = params.tenureMonths - moratoriumMonths;
  const regularRows = generateAmortizationSchedule({
    ...params,
    principalPaise: outstanding,
    tenureMonths: remainingTenure,
    firstEmiDate: currentDate,
  });

  // Re-number installments to continue from where moratorium left off
  return [
    ...rows,
    ...regularRows.map(r => ({
      ...r,
      installmentNumber: r.installmentNumber + moratoriumMonths,
    })),
  ];
}
```

**Q10. How do you handle part-prepayment (partial foreclosure) and regenerate the schedule?**
A: A part-prepayment is when a borrower pays an extra lump sum on top of the regular EMI, reducing the outstanding principal. This triggers a schedule regeneration — the remaining installments must be recomputed from the new lower outstanding balance.

Two options after part-prepayment:
- **Reduce tenure** (same EMI, fewer installments): the borrower continues paying the same EMI but finishes earlier. Preferred by most borrowers.
- **Reduce EMI** (same tenure, lower installments): the borrower finishes at the same time but pays less each month. Preferred when cash flow is tight.

```typescript
async function applyPartPrepayment(
  loanId: string,
  prepaymentAmount: bigint,
  prepaymentDate: Date,
  option: 'REDUCE_TENURE' | 'REDUCE_EMI',
): Promise<ScheduleRow[]> {
  const loan = await db('loans').where({ id: loanId }).first();

  // Step 1: Find the outstanding principal as of prepaymentDate
  const outstandingPrincipal = await computeOutstandingPrincipal(loanId, prepaymentDate);
  const newPrincipal = outstandingPrincipal - prepaymentAmount;

  if (newPrincipal <= 0n) {
    // Prepayment amount >= outstanding → full foreclosure
    return await applyFullForeclosure(loanId, prepaymentDate);
  }

  // Step 2: Find the next EMI due after prepaymentDate
  const nextInstallment = await db('loan_repayment_schedule')
    .where({ loan_id: loanId, status: 'PENDING' })
    .where('due_date', '>', prepaymentDate)
    .orderBy('due_date', 'asc')
    .first();

  // Step 3: Generate new schedule from new principal
  const r = loan.annual_rate_percent / 100 / 12;
  const remainingInstallments = await db('loan_repayment_schedule')
    .where({ loan_id: loanId, status: 'PENDING' })
    .count('id as count')
    .first();
  const remainingTenure = Number(remainingInstallments.count);

  let newTenure: number;
  let newEmi: bigint;

  if (option === 'REDUCE_TENURE') {
    // Keep EMI the same; compute new (shorter) tenure
    newEmi = loan.emi_amount;
    // Solve for n: P = EMI × (1 - (1+r)^-n) / r
    const emiNum = Number(newEmi);
    const pNum = Number(newPrincipal);
    newTenure = Math.ceil(
      -Math.log(1 - (pNum * r) / emiNum) / Math.log(1 + r)
    );
  } else {
    // Keep tenure the same; compute new (lower) EMI
    newTenure = remainingTenure;
    const emiDecimal = Number(newPrincipal) * r * Math.pow(1 + r, newTenure)
                       / (Math.pow(1 + r, newTenure) - 1);
    newEmi = BigInt(Math.round(emiDecimal));
  }

  // Step 4: Mark old future installments as superseded (new schedule version)
  const newVersion = loan.schedule_version + 1;
  await db('loan_repayment_schedule')
    .where({ loan_id: loanId, status: 'PENDING' })
    .update({ status: 'SUPERSEDED', schedule_version: loan.schedule_version });

  // Step 5: Generate and persist new schedule
  const newRows = generateAmortizationSchedule({
    principalPaise: newPrincipal,
    annualRatePercent: loan.annual_rate_percent,
    tenureMonths: newTenure,
    disbursementDate: prepaymentDate,
    firstEmiDate: nextInstallment.due_date,
    emiDay: loan.emi_day,
  });

  await db('loan_repayment_schedule').insert(
    newRows.map(row => ({ ...row, loan_id: loanId, schedule_version: newVersion }))
  );

  await db('loans').where({ id: loanId }).update({
    tenure_months: loan.tenure_months_original - (remainingTenure - newTenure),
    emi_amount: newEmi,
    schedule_version: newVersion,
    outstanding_principal: newPrincipal,
  });

  return newRows;
}
```

**Q11. How do you generate an amortization schedule for a floating-rate loan (linked to MCLR or repo rate)?**
A: Floating-rate loans have interest rates that reset periodically (monthly, quarterly, annually) based on a benchmark rate. The schedule cannot be fully generated upfront — it is generated in segments, with each segment using the current applicable rate.

**MCLR-linked loan example:**
- Base rate: MCLR (Marginal Cost of Funds-based Lending Rate) published monthly by each bank.
- Spread: fixed margin added to MCLR (e.g., MCLR + 2.5%).
- Reset frequency: typically annual for home loans.

```sql
CREATE TABLE loan_rate_history (
  id              UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  loan_id         UUID NOT NULL,
  effective_from  DATE NOT NULL,
  effective_until DATE,             -- NULL = currently applicable
  annual_rate     NUMERIC(8,4) NOT NULL,
  benchmark       TEXT NOT NULL,    -- 'MCLR_1Y', 'REPO_RATE', 'FIXED'
  benchmark_rate  NUMERIC(8,4),     -- the benchmark on the reset date
  spread          NUMERIC(8,4),     -- fixed spread over benchmark
  reset_trigger   TEXT NOT NULL,    -- 'SCHEDULED_RESET', 'RATE_CHANGE', 'RESTRUCTURING'
  PRIMARY KEY (loan_id, effective_from)
);
```

```typescript
function generateFloatingRateSchedule(
  loan: FloatingRateLoan,
  rateSegments: RateSegment[],  // [{from, until, annualRate}]
): ScheduleRow[] {
  const allRows: ScheduleRow[] = [];
  let outstanding = loan.principalPaise;
  let installmentNumber = 0;

  for (const segment of rateSegments) {
    const segmentInstallments = countInstallmentsInSegment(segment, loan.emiDay);
    const r = segment.annualRate / 100 / 12;

    // For each segment, EMI is recomputed based on current outstanding and remaining tenure
    const remainingTenure = loan.tenureMonths - installmentNumber;
    const segmentEmi = computeEmi(outstanding, r, remainingTenure);

    const segmentRows = generateSegmentRows(
      outstanding, segmentEmi, r, segmentInstallments,
      segment.from, loan.emiDay, installmentNumber,
    );

    allRows.push(...segmentRows);
    outstanding = segmentRows[segmentRows.length - 1].closingBalance;
    installmentNumber += segmentInstallments;
  }

  return allRows;
}
```

The engineering challenge: when a rate reset occurs mid-schedule, the system must:
1. Freeze the already-paid installments (immutable).
2. Recompute the EMI from the new rate on the current outstanding balance.
3. Generate new future installments.
4. Notify the borrower of the new EMI at least 30 days before it takes effect (RBI requirement).

## Section 4: Indian Fintech & Regulatory Context

**Q12. What RBI regulations govern amortization schedule disclosure, and what must the schedule contain?**
A: RBI's Fair Practices Code (FPC) for NBFCs and the Know Your Customer (KYC) Master Direction impose disclosure requirements on loan schedules:

**Mandatory disclosures at sanction / disbursement:**
- All-inclusive interest rate (APR — Annual Percentage Rate, not just the nominal rate).
- Annualised interest rate in percentage terms.
- Processing fee, prepayment charges, and penal interest rate — all as annualised rates.
- The full amortization schedule showing EMI, interest, and principal split for each installment.
- Total interest outgo over the loan tenure (sum of all interest components).

**Key Fact Statement (KFS) — RBI circular 2024:**
RBI mandated a standardised Key Fact Statement for retail and MSME loans below ₹50 lakh, effective October 2024. The KFS must be provided before loan execution and must include:
- Annual Percentage Rate (APR) — computed as the IRR of all cash flows (disbursement, all EMIs, all fees).
- Total amount payable (sum of all EMIs + upfront fees).
- Comparison: effective cost vs nominal rate.

**APR computation:**

```typescript
// APR = IRR (Internal Rate of Return) of all cash flows × 12 (to annualise)
// Cash flows: -P at time 0 (disbursement), +EMI at times 1..n (payments)
function computeAPR(
  principalPaise: bigint,      // amount received by borrower (net of fees)
  cashOutflows: Array<{ month: number; amount: bigint }>,  // EMIs + fees paid by borrower
): number {
  // Newton-Raphson to solve for monthly IRR
  const flows = [
    { t: 0, amount: -Number(principalPaise) },   // borrower receives principal
    ...cashOutflows.map(cf => ({ t: cf.month, amount: Number(cf.amount) })),
  ];

  let monthlyIrr = 0.01;  // initial guess: 1% per month
  for (let iteration = 0; iteration < 1000; iteration++) {
    const npv = flows.reduce((sum, cf) => sum + cf.amount / Math.pow(1 + monthlyIrr, cf.t), 0);
    const dnpv = flows.reduce(
      (sum, cf) => sum - cf.t * cf.amount / Math.pow(1 + monthlyIrr, cf.t + 1), 0
    );
    const newIrr = monthlyIrr - npv / dnpv;
    if (Math.abs(newIrr - monthlyIrr) < 1e-10) break;
    monthlyIrr = newIrr;
  }

  return monthlyIrr * 12 * 100;  // annualised APR as percentage
}
```

**Processing fee in APR:**
RBI requires that upfront fees (processing fee, insurance premium, documentation charges) be included in the APR calculation — they reduce the effective disbursement even though the nominal rate doesn't change. A 2% processing fee on a 12% loan raises the effective APR to approximately 13.5–14%.

**Q13. How do you handle GST on loan processing fees and EMI-linked charges in the schedule and ledger?**
A: Loan interest itself is exempt from GST (per Schedule III of CGST Act). However, fee-based charges are taxable services:

| Charge | GST applicability | Rate | SAC code |
|---|---|---|---|
| Processing fee | Taxable | 18% | 9971 |
| Prepayment / foreclosure charges | Taxable | 18% | 9971 |
| Late payment / penal charges | Taxable | 18% | 9971 |
| Bounce charges (dishonour fee) | Taxable | 18% | 9971 |
| Loan interest | Exempt | 0% | — |
| Insurance premium (if collected by lender as agent) | Taxable at insurance rates | 18% (non-life) | 9971 |

```typescript
function computeProcessingFeeWithGst(
  loanAmount: bigint,
  processingFeeRate: number,   // e.g., 0.02 for 2%
): { baseFee: bigint; gst: bigint; total: bigint } {
  const baseFee = BigInt(Math.round(Number(loanAmount) * processingFeeRate));
  const gst = BigInt(Math.round(Number(baseFee) * 0.18));
  return { baseFee, gst, total: baseFee + gst };
}
```

```
Dr  Bank Account (Asset)               ₹2,360   ← 2% of ₹1L + 18% GST; collected from borrower
  Cr  Processing Fee Income (Revenue)    ₹2,000
  Cr  GST Payable — Output (Liability)    ₹360

Loan disbursement (net of processing fee if deducted upfront):
Dr  Loan Receivable (Asset)           ₹1,00,000  ← face value of loan
  Cr  Bank Account (Asset)              ₹97,640   ← disbursed amount (₹1L - ₹2,360)
  Cr  Processing Fee Income (Revenue)    ₹2,000   ← if fee deducted from disbursement
  Cr  GST Payable — Output (Liability)    ₹360
```

**Note on APR when fee is deducted from disbursement:**
If the processing fee is deducted upfront from the disbursement (borrower receives ₹97,640 but is charged EMIs on ₹1,00,000), the effective disbursement in the APR calculation is ₹97,640 — raising the effective APR significantly. RBI requires this to be disclosed.

**Q14. What is the difference between flat rate and reducing balance EMI, and why does RBI mandate reducing balance disclosure?**
A: A flat-rate loan charges interest on the original principal for the entire tenure, even as the principal is being repaid. This results in a significantly higher effective cost than a reducing-balance loan with the same stated rate.

**Example — ₹1,00,000 loan, 12 months:**

| Method | Stated rate | Monthly interest | EMI | Total interest | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flat rate | 12% p.a. | ₹1,000 (fixed) | ₹9,333 | ₹12,000 | ~21.5% effective |
| Reducing balance | 12% p.a. | Declining | ₹8,885 | ₹6,619 | 12% effective |

The flat-rate borrower pays almost double the interest of the reducing-balance borrower despite the same "12% p.a." headline. This is why RBI's Fair Practices Code requires all lenders to disclose the annualised effective rate (APR) and mandates that retail loans use the reducing balance method. Many MFI (microfinance institution) loans and older consumer finance products were historically flat-rate — RBI has progressively tightened disclosure.

```typescript
function flatRateToReducingBalanceEquivalent(flatRate: number, tenureMonths: number): number {
  // Approximate conversion: effective reducing rate ≈ 1.83 × flat rate for 12-month loans
  // Exact: solve for r such that flat-rate EMI = reducing-balance EMI
  const flatMonthlyRate = flatRate / 12;
  const flatEmi = (1 + flatRate) / tenureMonths;  // normalized to principal = 1

  // Newton-Raphson to find reducing-balance monthly rate that gives same EMI
  let r = flatMonthlyRate * 1.8;
  for (let i = 0; i < 1000; i++) {
    const emi = r * Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths) / (Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths) - 1);
    const demi = (Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths) * (1 + r * tenureMonths) - Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths))
                 / Math.pow(Math.pow(1 + r, tenureMonths) - 1, 2);
    const newR = r - (emi - flatEmi) / demi;
    if (Math.abs(newR - r) < 1e-10) break;
    r = newR;
  }
  return r * 12 * 100;  // annualised effective rate
}
```

## Section 5: Performance and Edge Cases

**Q15. How do you bulk-generate amortization schedules for a large loan book, and what are the performance considerations?**
A: Generating schedules individually for each loan is fine at origination (one at a time) but becomes a bottleneck when:
- Onboarding a portfolio of acquired loans (bulk upload of 10,000 loans).
- Restructuring a cohort of loans simultaneously (COVID moratorium, rate reset).
- Rebuilding schedules after a bug fix.

**Bulk generation approach:**

```typescript
// Process in parallel batches
async function bulkGenerateSchedules(loans: LoanParameters[], batchSize = 500): Promise<void> {
  const batches = chunk(loans, batchSize);

  for (const batch of batches) {
    // Generate all schedules in-memory (CPU-bound; no I/O)
    const schedulesByLoan = batch.map(loan => ({
      loanId: loan.id,
      rows: generateAmortizationSchedule(loan),
    }));

    // Bulk insert — one DB round-trip per batch
    const allRows = schedulesByLoan.flatMap(({ loanId, rows }) =>
      rows.map(row => ({ ...row, loan_id: loanId }))
    );

    await db('loan_repayment_schedule').insert(allRows);
    // For 500 loans × 36 installments = 18,000 rows per batch insert
  }
}
```

**Performance targets:**
- Schedule generation (CPU): ~1ms per loan (36-month loan). 10,000 loans = ~10 seconds single-threaded; parallelise across CPU cores for faster bulk.
- DB insert: 18,000 rows per batch insert typically < 500ms on a standard PostgreSQL instance.
- Total for 10,000 loans: < 2 minutes with 4 parallel workers.

**Avoiding redundant recomputation:**
At loan servicing time (not origination), the schedule is already stored — it must never be regenerated from the formula unless explicitly triggered by a restructuring or prepayment. Any system that re-runs the generation formula on every read of the schedule is fragile: if the formula changes (e.g., rounding rule update), historical schedules would change retroactively.

**Q16. How do you validate a generated schedule, and what invariants must always hold?**
A: Every generated schedule must satisfy a set of mathematical invariants before persisting. Failing any invariant means a bug in the generation logic.

```typescript
function validateSchedule(rows: ScheduleRow[], originalPrincipal: bigint): ValidationResult {
  const errors: string[] = [];

  for (let i = 0; i < rows.length; i++) {
    const row = rows[i];

    // Invariant 1: EMI = interest + principal
    if (row.emi !== row.interest + row.principal) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: emi(${row.emi}) ≠ interest(${row.interest}) + principal(${row.principal})`);
    }

    // Invariant 2: closing_balance = opening_balance - principal
    if (row.closingBalance !== row.openingBalance - row.principal) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: closing_balance(${row.closingBalance}) ≠ opening - principal`);
    }

    // Invariant 3: opening_balance of row k = closing_balance of row k-1
    if (i > 0 && row.openingBalance !== rows[i-1].closingBalance) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: opening_balance(${row.openingBalance}) ≠ prev closing(${rows[i-1].closingBalance})`);
    }

    // Invariant 4: interest must be non-negative
    if (row.interest < 0n) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: negative interest ${row.interest}`);
    }

    // Invariant 5: principal must be positive (except stub row)
    if (!row.isStub && row.principal <= 0n) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: non-positive principal ${row.principal}`);
    }

    // Invariant 6: balances must be non-negative
    if (row.closingBalance < 0n) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: negative closing_balance ${row.closingBalance}`);
    }
  }

  // Invariant 7: first row opening_balance = original principal
  const firstRegular = rows.find(r => !r.isStub);
  if (firstRegular && firstRegular.openingBalance !== originalPrincipal) {
    errors.push(`First row opening_balance(${firstRegular.openingBalance}) ≠ principal(${originalPrincipal})`);
  }

  // Invariant 8: last row closing_balance = 0
  const lastRow = rows[rows.length - 1];
  if (lastRow.closingBalance !== 0n) {
    errors.push(`Last row closing_balance is ${lastRow.closingBalance}, expected 0`);
  }

  // Invariant 9: due dates must be strictly increasing
  for (let i = 1; i < rows.length; i++) {
    if (!isAfter(rows[i].dueDate, rows[i-1].dueDate)) {
      errors.push(`Row ${i+1}: due_date not after previous row`);
    }
  }

  // Invariant 10: total principal components = original principal
  const totalPrincipal = rows.filter(r => !r.isStub).reduce((s, r) => s + r.principal, 0n);
  if (totalPrincipal !== originalPrincipal) {
    errors.push(`Total principal(${totalPrincipal}) ≠ original(${originalPrincipal})`);
  }

  return { valid: errors.length === 0, errors };
}
```

async function restructureLoan(
  loanId: string,
  restructuringDate: Date,
  newAnnualRate: number,
  newTenureMonths: number,
  reason: string,
): Promise<void> {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    const loan = await trx('loans').where({ id: loanId }).forUpdate().first();

    // Step 1: Compute outstanding principal on restructuring date
    const outstandingPrincipal = await computeOutstandingPrincipal(loanId, restructuringDate, trx);

    // Step 2: Mark all pending installments of current version as SUPERSEDED
    await trx('loan_repayment_schedule')
      .where({ loan_id: loanId, status: 'PENDING', schedule_version: loan.schedule_version })
      .update({ status: 'SUPERSEDED' });

    const newVersion = loan.schedule_version + 1;

    // Step 3: Generate new schedule from outstanding principal at new rate and tenure
    const nextEmiDate = await getNextEmiDate(loanId, restructuringDate, trx);
    const newRows = generateAmortizationSchedule({
      principalPaise: outstandingPrincipal,
      annualRatePercent: newAnnualRate,
      tenureMonths: newTenureMonths,
      disbursementDate: restructuringDate,
      firstEmiDate: nextEmiDate,
      emiDay: loan.emi_day,
    });

    // Step 4: Persist new schedule
    await trx('loan_repayment_schedule').insert(
      newRows.map(row => ({ ...row, loan_id: loanId, schedule_version: newVersion }))
    );

    // Step 5: Record restructuring event
    await trx('loan_restructuring_events').insert({
      loan_id: loanId,
      restructuring_date: restructuringDate,
      old_annual_rate: loan.annual_rate_percent,
      new_annual_rate: newAnnualRate,
      old_remaining_tenure: loan.remaining_tenure_months,
      new_remaining_tenure: newTenureMonths,
      old_emi: loan.emi_amount,
      new_emi: newRows.find(r => !r.isStub)?.emi ?? 0n,
      outstanding_at_restructuring: outstandingPrincipal,
      reason,
      old_schedule_version: loan.schedule_version,
      new_schedule_version: newVersion,
      approved_by: 'CREDIT_COMMITTEE',
    });

    // Step 6: Update loan master
    await trx('loans').where({ id: loanId }).update({
      annual_rate_percent: newAnnualRate,
      remaining_tenure_months: newTenureMonths,
      emi_amount: newRows.find(r => !r.isStub)?.emi,
      schedule_version: newVersion,
      is_restructured: true,
    });
  });
}

## Section 6: Scenario / System Design Questions

**Q18. "Design the amortization schedule generation and storage system for an NBFC that originates 50,000 loans per month."**
A: At 50,000 loans/month with an average tenure of 24 months, the system generates 50,000 × 24 = 12,00,000 schedule rows per month; at steady state ~2.4 crore (24 million) rows total across all active loans.

**Architecture:**

```
Loan Origination System (LOS)
      ↓ (loan sanction event)
Schedule Generation Service
  ├── Input validation (rate, tenure, dates)
  ├── Stub period computation
  ├── EMI formula (Decimal.js)
  ├── Row generation + validation (10 invariants)
  └── Bulk insert to DB
      ↓
loan_repayment_schedule table
  ├── Partitioned by loan_id hash (8 partitions)
  ├── Index: (loan_id, status, due_date) — for NACH scheduler
  ├── Index: (due_date, status) — for bulk debit generation
  └── Index: (loan_id, installment_number) — for schedule display
      ↓
Downstream consumers:
  ├── NACH Debit Scheduler (reads due_date, amount for PENDING rows)
  ├── DPD Pipeline (reads earliest unpaid due_date)
  ├── Interest Accrual Engine (reads opening_balance per day)
  ├── Customer Portal (reads full schedule for display)
  └── ALM / Cash Flow Projection (aggregates future cash flows)
```

**Partitioning strategy:**
```sql
-- Hash-partition by loan_id: queries for a single loan always hit one partition
CREATE TABLE loan_repayment_schedule (
  -- ... columns
) PARTITION BY HASH (loan_id);

CREATE TABLE loan_repayment_schedule_p0 PARTITION OF loan_repayment_schedule
  FOR VALUES WITH (MODULUS 8, REMAINDER 0);
-- ... p1 through p7
```

**Schedule generation as an async job (not synchronous with loan sanction):**
```typescript
// Loan sanction → emit event → schedule generation worker picks it up
// Avoids blocking the sanction API on schedule generation (which involves DB writes for 24–60 rows)

loanSanctionedQueue.process(async (job) => {
  const { loanId, parameters } = job.data;
  const rows = generateAmortizationSchedule(parameters);
  const validation = validateSchedule(rows, parameters.principalPaise);

  if (!validation.valid) {
    throw new ScheduleGenerationError(loanId, validation.errors);
  }

  await db('loan_repayment_schedule').insert(
    rows.map(r => ({ ...r, loan_id: loanId }))
  );

  await db('loans').where({ id: loanId }).update({ schedule_generated: true });
  await notifyCustomer(loanId, 'SCHEDULE_READY');
});
```

**Q19. "A borrower disputes their amortization schedule, claiming the interest calculated for month 3 is ₹127 more than expected. How do you investigate?"**
A: Schedule disputes usually arise from one of four causes: wrong rate applied, wrong disbursement date causing a stub period the borrower didn't expect, wrong principal base (fee deducted from disbursement), or a rounding methodology the borrower computed differently.

**Step 1 — Retrieve the stored schedule**
```sql
SELECT * FROM loan_repayment_schedule
WHERE loan_id = '<id>' AND schedule_version = 1
ORDER BY installment_number;
```

Show the borrower the exact schedule row by row — opening balance, rate applied, interest computed, principal, closing balance.

**Step 2 — Reproduce the calculation manually**
```typescript
// For month 3:
const openingBalance = schedule[2].openingBalance;   // after 2 payments
const monthlyRate = loan.annual_rate_percent / 100 / 12;
const expectedInterest = BigInt(Math.round(Number(openingBalance) * monthlyRate));
const actualInterest = schedule[2].interest;
const delta = actualInterest - expectedInterest;
console.log({ openingBalance, monthlyRate, expectedInterest, actualInterest, delta });
```

**Step 3 — Check for stub period**
If the loan had a stub period (disbursement not on the 1st of the month), month 1 may have covered a partial period, changing the opening balance for month 2 and therefore the interest for month 3 compared to what the borrower computed assuming a full first period.

**Step 4 — Check rounding method**
If the borrower computed interest as `Number(outstanding) × rate` using a calculator that rounds differently, a ₹127 discrepancy on a ₹10 lakh loan at 1% monthly rate could arise purely from rounding. Show them your rounding method (ROUND_HALF_EVEN applied to paisa) and recompute their expected number using the same rule.

**Step 5 — Check for rate change (floating rate loans)**
If the loan is floating-rate and there was an MCLR reset between month 2 and month 3, the opening rate for month 3 changed. Check `loan_rate_history` for any rate changes before month 3's due date.

**Step 6 — Provide audit evidence**
Produce: (a) the original schedule generation parameters (principal, rate, dates, rounding method), (b) the stored `generated_at` timestamp and the deployed code version at that time, (c) the step-by-step computation for month 3 matching the stored row. If the stored row is correct, explain the computation. If it's wrong (a bug), trigger a schedule correction via the restructuring flow and credit the difference.

**Q20. "How would you build a cash flow projection system for an NBFC's Asset-Liability Management (ALM) report using amortization schedules?"**
A: ALM (Asset-Liability Management) requires matching the timing of cash inflows (loan repayments) against cash outflows (deposit maturities, borrowing repayments). The amortization schedule is the primary input for the asset side.

**The RBI ALM framework defines maturity buckets:**

| Bucket | Time range |
|---|---|
| Next day | 1 day |
| 2–7 days | Next 6 days |
| 8–14 days | Following week |
| 15–30 days | 2nd half of month |
| 31–90 days | 1–3 months |
| 91–180 days | 3–6 months |
| 181–365 days | 6–12 months |
| 1–3 years | 1–3 years |
| 3–5 years | 3–5 years |
| Over 5 years | Remaining |

```sql
-- Pre-aggregated cash flow projection by bucket
-- Recomputed nightly from loan_repayment_schedule
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW alm_cash_flow_projection AS
SELECT
  CASE
    WHEN due_date = CURRENT_DATE + 1                             THEN 'NEXT_DAY'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 2 AND CURRENT_DATE + 7  THEN '2_7_DAYS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 8 AND CURRENT_DATE + 14 THEN '8_14_DAYS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 15 AND CURRENT_DATE + 30 THEN '15_30_DAYS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 31 AND CURRENT_DATE + 90 THEN '31_90_DAYS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 91 AND CURRENT_DATE + 180 THEN '91_180_DAYS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 181 AND CURRENT_DATE + 365 THEN '181_365_DAYS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 366 AND CURRENT_DATE + 1095 THEN '1_3_YEARS'
    WHEN due_date BETWEEN CURRENT_DATE + 1096 AND CURRENT_DATE + 1825 THEN '3_5_YEARS'
    ELSE 'OVER_5_YEARS'
  END AS maturity_bucket,
  SUM(principal) AS principal_inflow,
  SUM(interest)  AS interest_inflow,
  SUM(emi)       AS total_inflow,
  COUNT(*)       AS installment_count
FROM loan_repayment_schedule
WHERE status = 'PENDING'
GROUP BY maturity_bucket;

-- Refresh nightly
REFRESH MATERIALIZED VIEW CONCURRENTLY alm_cash_flow_projection;
```

```typescript
// ALM report generation
async function generateAlmReport(reportDate: Date): Promise<AlmReport> {
  const assetCashFlows = await db('alm_cash_flow_projection').select('*');
  const liabilityCashFlows = await db('liability_cash_flow_projection').select('*');

  return ALM_BUCKETS.map(bucket => ({
    bucket: bucket.label,
    assets: {
      principalInflow: assetCashFlows.find(r => r.maturity_bucket === bucket.key)?.principal_inflow ?? 0n,
      interestInflow:  assetCashFlows.find(r => r.maturity_bucket === bucket.key)?.interest_inflow ?? 0n,
    },
    liabilities: {
      principalOutflow: liabilityCashFlows.find(r => r.maturity_bucket === bucket.key)?.total_outflow ?? 0n,
    },
    get gap() {
      return this.assets.principalInflow - this.liabilities.principalOutflow;
    },
    get cumulativeGap() { /* running sum */ },
  }));
}
```

The ALM gap (asset inflows minus liability outflows per bucket) reveals liquidity mismatches. RBI requires NBFCs with asset size ≥ ₹100 crore to submit the ALM return (NBS-ALM) quarterly. The accuracy of this return depends entirely on the accuracy and completeness of the amortization schedules in the system.

## Quick Reference Table

| Concept | Detail | Engineering action |
|---|---|---|
| **EMI formula** | `P × r × (1+r)^n / ((1+r)^n − 1)` | Use `Decimal.js`; never native float |
| **Reducing balance** | Interest on outstanding principal only | Per-installment: `interest = outstanding × r` |
| **Stub period** | Days from disbursement to first EMI (partial month) | Separate row; interest only; principal = 0 |
| **Last EMI adjustment** | Closing balance residual absorbed in final installment | `principal_last = outstanding_balance`; disclose in agreement |
| **Month-end clamping** | EMI day > days in month → use last day of month | Derive from original `emi_day`, not from previous date |
| **10 schedule invariants** | EMI = P + I; continuity; zero closing; total P = original | Validate before DB insert; throw on failure |
| **Immutability** | Generated schedule rows never updated | `schedule_version` increment on restructuring; old rows = SUPERSEDED |
| **Moratorium (interest-only)** | Principal unchanged; interest paid each month | `principal = 0n` in moratorium rows |
| **Moratorium (full)** | No payment; interest capitalised into principal | `outstanding += interest` each moratorium month |
| **Part-prepayment** | Reduce tenure or reduce EMI | New schedule version; outstanding recomputed on prepayment date |
| **Floating rate** | Segment-based schedule; EMI recomputed at each reset | `loan_rate_history` + segment generation; 30-day notice to borrower |
| **APR computation** | IRR of all cash flows × 12; includes all fees | Newton-Raphson; mandatory in KFS (RBI 2024) |
| **Flat rate vs reducing** | Flat 12% ≈ reducing 21.5% effective | RBI mandates reducing balance and APR disclosure |
| **GST on fees** | Processing, penal, foreclosure fees: 18% GST | Interest itself: GST-exempt (Schedule III) |
| **ALM buckets** | 10 RBI-defined time buckets; NBS-ALM quarterly | Materialised view from schedule; refresh nightly |
| **Bulk generation** | Batch of 500 loans × 36 rows = 18,000 rows per insert | Async queue; validate all 10 invariants before insert |

**Tip for the interview:** Amortization schedule questions at the 5 YOE level appear as "implement an EMI calculator," "design the loan repayment data model," and "why is our last EMI different from all the others?" The detail that separates senior answers is the stub period — almost every candidate ignores it and then can't explain why their closing balance is non-zero. Know that you must use `Decimal.js` (or equivalent) for all money arithmetic and be ready to explain why native float fails (`0.1 + 0.2 ≠ 0.3`). The 10 validation invariants, especially "total principal components = original principal" and "last closing balance = 0," demonstrate that you think about schedule generation as a system with correctness guarantees, not just a formula. Tie the schedule to its downstream consumers — NACH debit scheduler, DPD pipeline, interest accrual engine, ALM report — that shows you understand the schedule as a central data structure in the lending system, not just a customer-facing document.
