## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What are the five stages of the loan lifecycle, and what is the engineering responsibility at each stage?**
A: The loan lifecycle is the end-to-end sequence of states a loan traverses from the moment a borrower applies to the moment the obligation is fully extinguished. Each stage has distinct data requirements, business logic, regulatory obligations, and failure modes that engineering must handle.

**Stage 1 — Application**
The borrower submits a request for credit. Engineering responsibility: capture and validate applicant data, verify identity (KYC), pull credit bureau data, compute a preliminary eligibility determination, and persist the application in a state machine that can be audited.

**Stage 2 — Underwriting**
The lender evaluates creditworthiness and determines the risk-adjusted terms (rate, amount, tenure) on which it is willing to lend. Engineering responsibility: orchestrate data enrichment (bureau pulls, bank statement analysis, income verification), run decisioning models (scorecards, ML models), enforce policy rules, generate the credit decision, and produce a compliant adverse action notice if declined.

**Stage 3 — Disbursal**
Loan funds are transferred to the borrower after the borrower accepts the offer and completes any prerequisite steps (e-sign loan agreement, KYC completion, collateral submission). Engineering responsibility: generate and collect e-signed loan documents, trigger the fund transfer via the appropriate payment rail (ACH, NEFT/IMPS, wire), and activate the loan account in the loan management system (LMS).

**Stage 4 — Repayment**
The borrower makes scheduled payments (EMIs, installments) over the loan tenure. Engineering responsibility: generate the amortization schedule, collect payments via auto-debit mandates (NACH, ACH), process manual payments, apply payments correctly to principal and interest, handle prepayments and partial payments, and manage delinquency when payments are missed.

**Stage 5 — Closure**
The loan is fully repaid (or written off, or prepaid). Engineering responsibility: compute the final payoff amount (outstanding principal + accrued interest + prepayment penalty if applicable), process the final payment, release any collateral or liens, generate a No-Objection Certificate (NOC) or loan closure letter, and archive loan records per regulatory retention requirements.

**Why the lifecycle matters architecturally:** each stage produces state that the next stage depends on. An underwriting model depends on application data. Disbursal depends on underwriting output (approved amount, rate, tenure). Repayment schedule depends on disbursal date (the schedule starts when money moves, not when the loan is approved). Closure depends on the full repayment ledger. A design that doesn't model these dependencies explicitly creates data consistency bugs that are expensive to find and fix in production.

**Q2. What is the difference between a loan origination system (LOS), a loan management system (LMS), and a core banking system (CBS)?**
A: These three systems cover different lifecycle phases and are often distinct services, though smaller fintechs may combine them:

**Loan Origination System (LOS)**
- Scope: Application → Underwriting → Offer → E-sign → Disbursal trigger
- Owns: application records, underwriting decisions, credit bureau pulls, document generation, offer management
- Lifecycle phase: pre-disbursal
- Examples: Encompass (mortgage), LendingQB, Finflux, custom-built systems
- Data model anchor: `loan_application` table — the application ID is the primary key through this phase

**Loan Management System (LMS)**
- Scope: Disbursal → Repayment → Collections → Closure
- Owns: loan account, amortization schedule, payment ledger, delinquency tracking, collections workflows
- Lifecycle phase: post-disbursal
- Examples: Nucleus FinnOne, Finastra, LoanPro, Mambu, custom-built systems
- Data model anchor: `loan_account` table — activated at disbursal; the loan account ID is the primary key from this point

**Core Banking System (CBS)**
- Scope: Cross-cutting — GL entries, account balances, regulatory reporting
- Owns: general ledger, customer accounts, regulatory filings
- Lifecycle phase: all phases (every financial event hits the GL)
- Examples: Temenos T24, Finacle, Flexcube
- Data model anchor: `general_ledger_entry` — every disbursement, repayment, fee, and write-off creates GL entries

**The handoff problem:**
The transition from LOS to LMS at disbursal is one of the highest-risk moments in the loan lifecycle — it is where data is typically transferred between systems, often via an API call or a file export. Engineering must ensure this handoff is:
- **Idempotent**: if the disbursal triggers twice (network timeout, retry), the loan account is created exactly once.
- **Atomic with the fund transfer**: the loan account must not be activated if the fund transfer fails, and the fund transfer must not proceed if account activation fails.
- **Auditable**: the exact state of the application at the moment of handoff must be preserved for regulatory review.

**Q3. What is a loan state machine, and why must it be explicit in the data model?**
A: A loan passes through a defined set of states, and the transitions between states are governed by business rules, regulatory requirements, and external events (payment received, payment missed, borrower request). An explicit state machine:

```
APPLICATION STATES:
  draft → submitted → under_review → approved → offered → accepted
       → declined (terminal)
       → withdrawn (terminal)
       → expired (terminal — offer not accepted within window)

LOAN ACCOUNT STATES (post-disbursal):
  pending_disbursal → active → delinquent → defaulted → written_off (terminal)
                   → closed (terminal — fully repaid)
                   → prepaid_closed (terminal — prepaid in full)
                   → restructured → active (loop — after restructuring)
```

**Why explicit state matters:**

1. **Invalid transitions are bugs with financial consequences.** A loan transitioning from `active` directly to `closed` without processing all payments creates an accounting discrepancy. A loan in `defaulted` state receiving a payment must follow a specific application order (fees → interest → principal) that differs from `active` state. State-conditional logic that isn't enforced at the data layer will be violated.

2. **Regulatory audit trails require state history.** Regulators (RBI, OCC, FCA) require lenders to demonstrate that every state transition was triggered by a documented event, with a timestamp and actor. An audit trail without explicit state transitions cannot satisfy this requirement.

3. **Concurrent events cause race conditions.** A payment processing job and a delinquency flagging job running simultaneously on the same loan can produce an incorrect final state if transitions are not serialized. Explicit state machine + database-level locking (or optimistic concurrency with version numbers) prevents this.

**Implementation pattern:**
```sql
loan_applications (
  id              UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  state           VARCHAR(32) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'draft',
  state_version   INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 1,    -- optimistic concurrency
  state_changed_at TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
)

loan_state_transitions (
  id              BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY,
  loan_id         UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES loan_applications(id),
  from_state      VARCHAR(32) NOT NULL,
  to_state        VARCHAR(32) NOT NULL,
  triggered_by    VARCHAR(64) NOT NULL,           -- event name
  actor           VARCHAR(128),                   -- user_id, system_job, api_client
  metadata        JSONB,                          -- reason, notes, external references
  created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
)
-- Append-only. Never update or delete. This is the audit log.
```

## Section 2: Application Stage

**Q4. What data is collected during a loan application, and how is it validated?**
A: The application stage collects three categories of data:

**1. Identity Data (KYC)**
- Full legal name, date of birth, government ID (SSN in US; Aadhaar/PAN in India; NIN in UK)
- Address (current and permanent)
- Contact information (email, phone — verified via OTP)
- Purpose: identity verification, deduplication (is this the same person as an existing borrower?), watchlist screening (OFAC/sanctions, PEP lists)

**2. Financial Data**
- Employment status and employer details
- Monthly income (stated; later verified against bank statements or payslips)
- Existing liabilities (other loans, credit card outstanding — later verified against bureau)
- Bank account details (for disbursal and repayment mandate)

**3. Loan Request**
- Requested amount
- Requested tenure
- Loan purpose (personal, business, education, vehicle — affects product eligibility and regulatory classification)
- Collateral (for secured loans)

**Validation layers:**

*Syntactic validation* (client-side + server-side): format checks, required field presence, date range validation (DOB implies age eligibility), amount within product min/max limits.

*Identity verification*: third-party KYC providers (Onfido, Jumio, Digio in India) verify government ID documents. Liveness checks confirm the applicant is a real person. PAN/Aadhaar validation via NSDL/UIDAI APIs in India; SSN validation via Socure or Experian in US.

*Deduplication*: check whether the applicant already has an active loan, a pending application, or was previously declined within a cooling-off period. Match on: exact ID match, email + DOB fuzzy match, phone number. Return an error ("existing application found") rather than creating a duplicate.

*Negative list screening*: check applicant against internal blacklists (previous defaulters, fraudsters) and external watchlists (OFAC, UN sanctions, PEP databases). This is a compliance gate — the application cannot proceed if the applicant is flagged.

**Engineering the application form as a funnel:**
Applications are rarely completed in one session. The data model must support partial saves:
```sql
loan_applications (
  id              UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  borrower_id     UUID,                          -- null until identity is verified
  state           VARCHAR(32) DEFAULT 'draft',
  form_progress   JSONB,                         -- which sections are complete
  kyc_status      VARCHAR(16),                   -- pending, verified, failed
  data            JSONB,                         -- application data (encrypted at rest)
  created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ,
  submitted_at    TIMESTAMPTZ,                   -- null until fully submitted
  expires_at      TIMESTAMPTZ                    -- incomplete applications expire
)
```

**Q5. What is bureau pull, and how does it integrate into the application flow?**
A: A bureau pull (credit report pull) is a query to a credit bureau (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion in the US; CIBIL, Experian, CRIF in India) that returns the applicant's credit history, current outstanding obligations, payment track record, and a credit score.

**Hard pull vs. soft pull:**
- **Soft pull**: does not affect the applicant's credit score; used for pre-qualification and eligibility checks. The applicant may not be aware it happened. Used at the pre-application or initial eligibility stage.
- **Hard pull**: visible to all lenders who pull the bureau in the future; can slightly lower the applicant's credit score. Triggered when the applicant submits a formal application. Regulated — requires explicit borrower consent.

**Bureau pull integration:**
```python
def initiate_bureau_pull(application_id, applicant):
    # Bureau pulls are expensive ($0.50–$5.00 each) and slow (500ms–3s)
    # Do not pull synchronously in the application submission API response
    
    # 1. Check cache — did we pull this bureau for this PAN/SSN in the last 30 days?
    cached = bureau_cache.get(applicant.government_id, max_age_days=30)
    if cached:
        return cached
    
    # 2. Dispatch async job — application moves to 'under_review' immediately
    job = queue.enqueue(
        fetch_bureau_report,
        application_id=application_id,
        applicant_ssn=encrypt(applicant.ssn),
        bureau_providers=['equifax', 'experian'],  # pull from two for cross-validation
        hard_pull=True,
        consent_timestamp=applicant.consent_ts
    )
    
    return BureauPullInitiated(job_id=job.id)

def fetch_bureau_report(application_id, applicant_ssn, bureau_providers, ...):
    reports = []
    for provider in bureau_providers:
        report = bureau_client(provider).pull(decrypt(applicant_ssn))
        store_bureau_report(application_id, provider, report)  # persist raw response
        reports.append(report)
    
    # Parse and normalize bureau data into internal schema
    normalized = normalize_bureau_data(reports)
    update_application_bureau_data(application_id, normalized)
    trigger_underwriting(application_id)
```

**What the bureau report contains:**
- Credit score (FICO 8, FICO 9, VantageScore 3.0 in US; CIBIL score in India — range 300–900)
- Trade lines: every open and closed credit account (lender name, type, limit, balance, payment history)
- Derogatory marks: late payments (30/60/90 days), charge-offs, collections, bankruptcies, judgments
- Inquiries: all hard pulls in the last 24 months (signals how much new credit the borrower has been seeking)
- Public records: tax liens, bankruptcies

**Caching bureau data:** bureau data has a shelf life — a report pulled 30 days ago is still materially accurate; one pulled 90 days ago may not reflect a new delinquency. Cache aggressively (reduces cost), but always re-pull at the final underwriting stage if the initial pull is older than 30 days.

## Section 3: Underwriting Stage

**Q6. What is the underwriting process, and what is the difference between rule-based and model-based underwriting?**
A: Underwriting is the process of evaluating whether to extend credit and on what terms. It combines policy rules (hard cutoffs that cannot be overridden) with a credit risk model (a continuous score predicting probability of default) to produce a decision.

**Rule-based underwriting (policy rules / scorecards):**
A decision tree of hard cutoffs and scoring bands. Every rule is explicit, auditable, and explainable.

```
POLICY RULES (hard gates — instant decline if any fail):
  - Age < 18 → Decline
  - KYC verification failed → Decline
  - Bankruptcy in last 24 months → Decline
  - Active delinquency on any bureau trade line → Decline
  - CIBIL score < 650 → Decline
  - FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio) > 50% → Decline

SCORECARD (continuous scoring):
  Variables:
    Credit score band:        300–549 (-30pts), 550–649 (-10pts), 650–749 (+10pts), 750+ (+20pts)
    Employment type:          Salaried (+15pts), Self-employed (+5pts), Gig worker (-5pts)
    Income stability (months): < 6 (-10pts), 6–12 (0pts), > 12 (+10pts)
    Existing EMI burden:       0–20% (+10pts), 20–35% (0pts), 35–50% (-15pts)
    Bureau inquiry count (6m): 0–2 (+5pts), 3–5 (-5pts), 6+ (-15pts)

  Total score → Risk tier:
    >= 60pts: Tier A — approve at prime rate
    40–59pts: Tier B — approve at standard rate
    20–39pts: Tier C — approve at risk-adjusted rate (higher rate, lower amount)
    < 20pts:  Decline
```

**ML-based underwriting:**
A trained model (logistic regression, gradient boosting, neural network) ingests hundreds of features from bureau data, bank transaction history, and alternative data, and outputs a probability of default (PD) estimate. The PD maps to an interest rate via a pricing model.

| Property | Rule-Based | ML-Based |
|--|--|--|
| **Explainability** | Full — every decision has a traceable reason | Low — black box; requires SHAP/LIME for feature attribution |
| **Regulatory compliance** | Easy — adverse action reasons are explicit rules | Hard — regulators require explainable adverse action reasons |
| **Data requirement** | Low — works with sparse data | High — needs large labeled dataset (10K+ loans with outcome) |
| **Bias detection** | Explicit — rules are inspectable | Requires rigorous fairness audit (disparate impact testing) |
| **Accuracy** | Lower (misses complex patterns) | Higher (captures non-linear interactions) |
| **Update cycle** | Manual rule changes | Continuous retraining pipeline |
| **Appropriate stage** | Early-stage lender, new market entry | Mature lender with sufficient loan performance data |

Most production lending systems use a **hybrid**: hard policy rules (non-negotiable gates) + an ML model for scoring within the approved band, with human override capability for edge cases.

**Q7. What is FOIR / DTI, and how is it calculated in the underwriting engine?**
A: FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio) in India and DTI (Debt-to-Income Ratio) in the US measure the proportion of a borrower's gross monthly income that is already committed to existing debt obligations. It is the single most predictive underwriting variable after credit score.

**Formula:**
```
FOIR / DTI = (Total monthly debt obligations) / (Gross monthly income)

Total monthly debt obligations =
    Existing EMIs (from bureau trade lines) +
    Proposed EMI for the new loan +
    Other fixed obligations (rent if captured, alimony, child support)

Example:
  Gross monthly income:        ₹80,000
  Existing EMIs (bureau):      ₹15,000
  Proposed EMI (new loan):     ₹12,000
  Total obligations:           ₹27,000
  FOIR = 27,000 / 80,000 = 33.75%

Policy: FOIR ≤ 50% → eligible; FOIR > 50% → decline or reduce loan amount
```

**Engineering the FOIR calculation:**

```python
def compute_foir(application_id, proposed_loan_amount, proposed_tenure_months):
    app = get_application(application_id)
    bureau = get_bureau_data(application_id)
    
    # Income: verified vs. stated
    monthly_income = (
        app.verified_monthly_income     # from bank statement analysis
        if app.verified_monthly_income  # or payslip verification
        else app.stated_monthly_income * INCOME_HAIRCUT_FACTOR  # apply 20% haircut if unverified
    )
    
    # Existing obligations from bureau (all open installment + revolving accounts)
    existing_emi = sum(
        trade.monthly_payment
        for trade in bureau.open_trade_lines
        if trade.account_type in ('installment', 'mortgage', 'auto', 'student')
        and trade.monthly_payment is not None
    )
    
    # Revolving utilization converted to minimum payment
    revolving_min_payment = sum(
        trade.balance * 0.03   # approximate minimum payment as 3% of balance
        for trade in bureau.open_trade_lines
        if trade.account_type == 'revolving'
    )
    
    # Proposed EMI (computed from amortization formula)
    proposed_emi = compute_emi(
        principal=proposed_loan_amount,
        annual_rate=get_indicative_rate(application_id),
        tenure_months=proposed_tenure_months
    )
    
    total_obligations = existing_emi + revolving_min_payment + proposed_emi
    foir = total_obligations / monthly_income
    
    # Maximum eligible loan amount given FOIR constraint
    max_emi = monthly_income * FOIR_LIMIT - existing_emi - revolving_min_payment
    max_eligible_amount = compute_principal_from_emi(
        emi=max_emi,
        annual_rate=get_indicative_rate(application_id),
        tenure_months=proposed_tenure_months
    )
    
    return FOIRResult(foir=foir, max_eligible_amount=max_eligible_amount, proposed_emi=proposed_emi)
```

**Q8. What is adverse action, and what are the regulatory requirements around it?**
A: Adverse action is a formal notification sent to a loan applicant when their application is declined, when an approved offer is materially less favorable than requested (lower amount, higher rate), or when a credit account is terminated. In the US, it is governed by the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA). In India, RBI guidelines require reason disclosure.

**ECOA / Regulation B requirements (US):**
- Adverse action notice must be provided **within 30 days** of the credit decision.
- Must include: the specific reasons for the adverse action (up to 4 principal reasons), the name and address of the credit bureau whose report was used, and notice of the applicant's right to obtain a free copy of their credit report.
- Reasons must be specific and accurate — "credit score is below our threshold" is acceptable; "insufficient creditworthiness" is not.

**Standard adverse action reason codes (FCRA-compliant):**
```
01: Delinquent past or present credit obligations with others
02: Collection action or judgment
03: Bankruptcy
04: Insufficient credit file
05: No credit file
06: Too many inquiries in the last 12 months
07: Too many accounts with delinquency
08: Debt-to-income ratio too high
09: Derogatory public record or collection filed
10: Time since delinquency is too recent or unknown
```

**Engineering adverse action:**

The reason codes for a declined application must be **generated from the underwriting decision logic**, not manually assigned. This is an area where ML-based underwriting creates regulatory risk — a model that cannot explain its own output cannot generate compliant adverse action reasons.

```python
def generate_adverse_action_reasons(application_id, decision_result):
    reasons = []
    underwriting = decision_result.underwriting_detail
    
    # Policy rule failures → direct adverse action reasons
    if underwriting.bankruptcy_in_24_months:
        reasons.append(AdverseReason(code='03', text='Bankruptcy'))
    if underwriting.active_delinquency:
        reasons.append(AdverseReason(code='01', text='Delinquent credit obligations'))
    if underwriting.foir > FOIR_LIMIT:
        reasons.append(AdverseReason(code='08', text='Debt-to-income ratio too high'))
    if underwriting.credit_score < MIN_SCORE:
        reasons.append(AdverseReason(code='insufficient_score',
                                      text='Credit score below minimum threshold'))
    if underwriting.inquiry_count_6m > MAX_INQUIRIES:
        reasons.append(AdverseReason(code='06', text='Too many recent credit inquiries'))
    
    # For ML-based decline: use SHAP values to identify top negative features
    if decision_result.declined_by_model:
        shap_reasons = get_top_negative_shap_features(application_id, top_n=4)
        reasons.extend(map_shap_to_adverse_reason_codes(shap_reasons))
    
    # ECOA requires top 4 reasons; sort by impact and truncate
    return sorted(reasons, key=lambda r: -r.impact_score)[:4]
```

## Section 4: Disbursal Stage

**Q9. What are the steps between loan approval and fund transfer, and what can go wrong at each?**
A: Disbursal is not a single event — it is a sequence of steps, each of which can fail independently. Engineering must handle partial completion and ensure idempotency across the sequence.

**Step 1 — Offer acceptance**
The borrower receives the loan offer (amount, rate, tenure, fees) and must explicitly accept it. The offer has an expiry window (typically 7–30 days). If the borrower does not accept within the window, the offer expires and the application moves to terminal state `expired`.

*Failure mode*: borrower accepts the offer after the expiry timestamp (race condition in UI vs. backend expiry check). The system must check offer validity server-side at the moment of acceptance, not only when displaying the offer.

**Step 2 — E-sign loan agreement**
The borrower signs the loan agreement electronically. This creates a legally binding contract. E-sign providers: DocuSign, Adobe Sign, Digio (India), Leegality (India).

*Failure mode*: E-sign provider webhook is not received (network failure), leaving the application in a limbo state where the borrower signed but the system doesn't know. The system must poll the e-sign provider for pending document status on a schedule, not rely solely on webhooks.

**Step 3 — Repayment mandate registration**
For auto-debit (NACH in India, ACH in US), the borrower's repayment mandate must be registered with the bank *before* disbursal. Registering after disbursal creates a window where the first EMI cannot be collected automatically.

*Failure mode*: mandate registration fails (bank rejects the mandate — wrong account number, account closed). Disbursal must be held until mandate is confirmed, or the system must fall back to manual repayment with heightened monitoring.

**Step 4 — Pre-disbursal checks**
Final verification before money moves:
- KYC status still valid (bureau report not expired, identity still verified)
- Negative list check (borrower not added to internal blacklist since underwriting)
- Loan document signed and stored
- Repayment mandate registered (or manual repayment terms confirmed)
- Disbursal account verified (penny drop — small test transfer and verification)

**Step 5 — Fund transfer**
The actual money movement via the appropriate payment rail:
- NEFT/IMPS/UPI in India
- ACH in US
- Wire transfer for large amounts

*Failure mode*: payment rail failure (processor down, bank holiday). The system must handle the case where the disbursal job runs, triggers the transfer, but does not receive a confirmation. Idempotency keys prevent double-disbursal on retry.

**Step 6 — Loan account activation**
Once the fund transfer is confirmed, the LMS creates the loan account with the activated amortization schedule. The activation date is the actual disbursal date — if the money moved on Day 3 after approval, the schedule starts on Day 3, not the approval date.

*Failure mode*: fund transfer confirmed but LMS activation fails (LMS is down). The money has moved but there is no loan account. The system must retry LMS activation and alert operations if it cannot complete within SLA.

**Q10. How is the amortization schedule computed, and what are the edge cases?**
A: The amortization schedule is the complete table of payment dates, payment amounts, and their breakdown into principal and interest for every installment over the loan tenure. It is computed once at disbursal and stored — it is the reference truth for what the borrower owes and when.

**Standard EMI formula (reducing balance / flat rate):**

*Reducing balance (most common for personal and home loans):*
```
EMI = P × r × (1 + r)^n / ((1 + r)^n - 1)

Where:
  P = principal (disbursed amount)
  r = monthly interest rate = annual_rate / 12 / 100
  n = tenure in months

Example:
  P = ₹500,000, annual rate = 12%, tenure = 24 months
  r = 12 / 12 / 100 = 0.01
  n = 24
  EMI = 500,000 × 0.01 × (1.01)^24 / ((1.01)^24 - 1)
      = 500,000 × 0.01 × 1.2697 / 0.2697
      = ₹23,537
```

*Amortization table construction:*
```python
from decimal import Decimal, ROUND_HALF_UP

def build_amortization_schedule(principal, annual_rate_pct, tenure_months, disbursal_date):
    r = Decimal(str(annual_rate_pct)) / Decimal('1200')  # monthly rate
    n = Decimal(str(tenure_months))
    P = Decimal(str(principal))
    
    # EMI — use Decimal throughout to avoid floating point errors
    emi = (P * r * (1 + r)**n / ((1 + r)**n - 1)).quantize(
        Decimal('0.01'), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP
    )
    
    schedule = []
    balance = P
    
    for i in range(1, tenure_months + 1):
        interest = (balance * r).quantize(Decimal('0.01'), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP)
        principal_component = emi - interest
        
        # Last installment: adjust for accumulated rounding
        if i == tenure_months:
            principal_component = balance
            emi_this_month = balance + interest
        else:
            emi_this_month = emi
        
        balance = balance - principal_component
        
        due_date = compute_due_date(disbursal_date, installment_number=i)
        
        schedule.append(Installment(
            installment_number=i,
            due_date=due_date,
            emi_amount=emi_this_month,
            principal_component=principal_component,
            interest_component=interest,
            opening_balance=balance + principal_component,
            closing_balance=balance
        ))
    
    return schedule
```

**Critical edge cases:**

*Floating point arithmetic*: never use floating point (float64) for money calculations. Accumulated rounding errors across 240 installments (20-year mortgage) produce material discrepancies. Use `Decimal` (Python), `BigDecimal` (Java), or store amounts in integer cents/paise.

*First installment due date*: the first EMI due date is typically 30 days after disbursal, but this varies by product. A loan disbursed on the 28th of a month — does the first EMI fall on the 28th of next month, or the last day of next month if the month has fewer days? This must be specified in the product configuration and handled explicitly in `compute_due_date`.

*Mid-month disbursal and stub period interest*: if a loan is disbursed on the 15th but EMIs are due on the 1st of each month, the first billing period is shorter than 30 days. The interest for this stub period is often collected upfront (at disbursal) or added to the first EMI. The product must define which approach is used, and the schedule must implement it correctly.

*Moratorium period*: some loan products offer a grace period (1–6 months) where the borrower only pays interest (no principal). The amortization schedule must handle moratorium months (interest-only EMIs) followed by standard reducing-balance EMIs on the remaining principal.

*Rate changes (floating rate loans)*: for loans with a variable interest rate linked to a benchmark (LIBOR, SOFR, RBI repo rate), the amortization schedule must be recomputed when the rate changes. The system must track which schedule version applies to which payment period.

## Section 5: Repayment Stage

**Q11. How does payment processing work in a loan repayment system, and how are payments applied?**
A: Loan repayment involves two distinct problems: *collecting* the payment (triggering the debit from the borrower's account) and *applying* the payment (allocating the collected amount across fee, interest, and principal components in the correct order).

**Payment collection channels:**

| Channel | Mechanism | Latency | Failure rate |
|--|--|--|--|
| **Auto-debit (NACH/ACH)** | Bank mandate; system initiates debit on due date | T+1 (result next day) | 5–15% (NSF, mandate issues) |
| **UPI auto-pay (India)** | UPI recurring mandate | Near real-time | 3–8% |
| **Manual payment (borrower-initiated)** | Payment link, net banking, wallet | Real-time | Low (borrower chose to pay) |
| **PDC (Post-dated cheques)** | Legacy; physical cheques presented on due date | T+1 to T+2 | 2–5% |
| **Cash deposit** | Branch or agent collection | T+0 (manual entry) | Low |

**Payment application order:**

When a payment is received, it must be applied in a defined hierarchy. The order is specified in the loan agreement and regulated in some jurisdictions:

```
Standard application order (most common in retail lending):

1. Overdue fees / penal charges      ← clear all outstanding penalties first
2. Overdue interest                  ← clear all past-due interest
3. Overdue principal                 ← clear all past-due principal
4. Current installment interest      ← then service current period
5. Current installment principal     ← then reduce current principal
6. Prepayment (if excess)            ← any excess goes to future principal

Example:
  EMI due: ₹10,000 (₹3,500 interest + ₹6,500 principal)
  Overdue fee: ₹500
  Payment received: ₹12,000

  Applied as:
    ₹500   → Overdue fee (cleared)
    ₹0     → Overdue interest (none)
    ₹0     → Overdue principal (none)
    ₹3,500 → Current interest (cleared)
    ₹6,500 → Current principal (cleared)
    ₹1,500 → Prepayment (applied to next installment's principal)
```

**Q12. How does delinquency tracking work, and what triggers collections workflow?**
A: Delinquency begins the moment an installment is not paid by its due date. The delinquency tracking system must compute, in real time, how overdue each loan is, and trigger the appropriate workflow at each delinquency bucket.

**Delinquency buckets (Days Past Due — DPD):**
```
DPD 0:    Current — all payments on time
DPD 1–30: Early delinquency — first missed payment
DPD 31–60: Moderate delinquency
DPD 61–90: Serious delinquency
DPD 90+:  NPA (Non-Performing Asset) in India; Substandard in GAAP classification
DPD 180+: Default / Write-off candidate
```

**DPD computation:**
```python
def compute_dpd(loan_account_id, as_of_date):
    account = get_loan_account(loan_account_id)
    schedule = get_amortization_schedule(loan_account_id)
    payments = get_payments(loan_account_id)
    
    # Find the oldest unpaid installment
    for installment in schedule:
        if installment.due_date > as_of_date:
            break  # future installments are not yet due
        
        amount_paid_for_installment = sum(
            p.principal_applied + p.interest_applied
            for p in payments
            if p.installment_number == installment.installment_number
        )
        
        if amount_paid_for_installment < installment.emi_amount - PAYMENT_TOLERANCE:
            # This installment is unpaid or underpaid
            dpd = (as_of_date - installment.due_date).days
            return DPDResult(dpd=dpd, overdue_amount=installment.emi_amount - amount_paid_for_installment,
                             oldest_overdue_date=installment.due_date)
    
    return DPDResult(dpd=0, overdue_amount=0)
```

**Collections workflow triggers:**

| DPD Bucket | Automated Action | Human Action |
|--|--|--|
| DPD 1–3 | SMS/email reminder | None |
| DPD 4–7 | IVR call, WhatsApp message, retry NACH | None |
| DPD 8–15 | Soft collections call from agent | Agent outreach |
| DPD 16–30 | Payment link with waiver offer | Senior agent, manager callback |
| DPD 31–60 | Legal notice template, bureau reporting | Dedicated collector assigned |
| DPD 61–90 | Bureau reporting, penal interest escalation | Field agent (for secured loans) |
| DPD 90+ | NPA classification; provision in books | Legal/collections agency handoff |
| DPD 180+ | Write-off recommendation | Credit committee approval |

**Bureau reporting of delinquency:**
Regulated lenders are required to report delinquent accounts to credit bureaus monthly. In India, CIBIL reporting is mandatory for accounts with DPD > 30. Engineering must generate the bureau reporting file in the bureau's required format (METRO 2 in US; CIBIL's member format in India) and submit it within the monthly window.

**Q13. How are prepayments and foreclosure handled in the loan repayment system?**
A: A prepayment occurs when the borrower pays more than the scheduled EMI — either a partial prepayment (paying extra principal, reducing future EMIs or tenure) or full foreclosure (paying off the entire outstanding balance before maturity).

**Partial prepayment — two business decisions:**
1. **Reduce EMI (same tenure)**: the prepaid principal reduces the outstanding balance; the amortization schedule is recomputed with a lower EMI for the remaining tenure.
2. **Reduce tenure (same EMI)**: the prepaid principal reduces the outstanding balance; the amortization schedule is recomputed with the same EMI but fewer installments.

The product configuration must specify which option is offered, and whether the borrower can choose. The chosen option must be stored and the schedule must be regenerated:

```python
def process_prepayment(loan_account_id, prepayment_amount, option, effective_date):
    account = get_loan_account(loan_account_id)
    remaining_principal = compute_outstanding_principal(loan_account_id, as_of=effective_date)
    
    if prepayment_amount >= remaining_principal:
        # Full foreclosure
        return process_foreclosure(loan_account_id, effective_date)
    
    new_principal = remaining_principal - prepayment_amount
    remaining_tenure = account.original_tenure - current_installment_number(loan_account_id)
    
    if option == 'reduce_emi':
        new_emi = compute_emi(new_principal, account.annual_rate, remaining_tenure)
        new_schedule = build_amortization_schedule(new_principal, account.annual_rate,
                                                    remaining_tenure, effective_date)
    elif option == 'reduce_tenure':
        new_tenure = compute_tenure_for_same_emi(new_principal, account.annual_rate, account.emi)
        new_schedule = build_amortization_schedule(new_principal, account.annual_rate,
                                                    new_tenure, effective_date)
    
    # Archive old schedule; store new schedule with version number
    archive_schedule(loan_account_id)
    store_schedule(loan_account_id, new_schedule, version=current_version + 1)
    
    # Record prepayment in payment ledger
    record_payment(loan_account_id, amount=prepayment_amount, type='prepayment',
                   schedule_version_after=current_version + 1)
    
    return PrepaymentResult(new_emi=new_emi, new_tenure=new_tenure, new_schedule=new_schedule)
```

**Prepayment penalty:**
Many loan products charge a fee for prepayment (to compensate the lender for lost interest income). Regulated in many jurisdictions — RBI circular (2012) prohibits prepayment penalties on floating rate loans to individual borrowers. The system must enforce penalty rules based on loan product configuration:
```python
def compute_prepayment_penalty(loan_account_id, prepayment_amount, effective_date):
    account = get_loan_account(loan_account_id)
    months_active = (effective_date - account.disbursal_date).days / 30
    
    if account.rate_type == 'floating' and account.borrower_type == 'individual':
        return Decimal('0')  # RBI prohibition
    
    if months_active < account.lock_in_period_months:
        return prepayment_amount * account.prepayment_penalty_rate
    
    return Decimal('0')  # no penalty after lock-in period
```

## Section 6: Closure Stage

**Q14. What happens at loan closure, and what does the system need to produce?**
A: Loan closure occurs when the outstanding balance reaches zero — either through scheduled repayment completion, full prepayment/foreclosure, or (in the worst case) write-off. Closure triggers a sequence of system actions and document generation.

**Normal closure (full repayment):**

```
Step 1: Final installment payment processed
Step 2: Compute closure balance
        closing_balance = outstanding_principal + accrued_interest_to_date
                        + overdue_fees + prepayment_penalty (if applicable)
        If closing_balance == 0.00: proceed
        If closing_balance > 0.00 (rounding residual): auto-waive or collect

Step 3: Update loan account status → 'closed'
        Record closure_date, closure_reason = 'full_repayment'

Step 4: Release collateral / lien (for secured loans)
        - Trigger lien removal request to asset registry
        - For vehicle loans: RC book endorsement removal
        - For home loans: mortgage release deed filing

Step 5: Generate and deliver closure documents
        - No-Objection Certificate (NOC): confirms the loan is fully repaid
          and the lender has no further claim on the borrower
        - Loan Closure Letter: formal communication stating closure date and
          final balance
        - For home loans: original property documents returned to borrower

Step 6: Update credit bureau
        - Report account as 'closed' with final DPD status
        - Must be done within the monthly bureau reporting window

Step 7: Final GL entries
        - Reverse any remaining interest suspense (NPA-related)
        - Recognize deferred fee income
        - Close the loan account in the general ledger

Step 8: Archive loan records
        - Retain all documents, payment ledger, and audit log for regulatory
          minimum retention period (typically 5–10 years)
```

**NOC generation — engineering:**
The NOC is a legally significant document — it releases the borrower from any future claims. It must be:
- Generated from immutable data (the closed loan account's final state, not current state)
- Digitally signed (using the lender's signing certificate) to prevent tampering
- Delivered to the borrower via a verified channel (registered email, registered post, or DigiLocker in India)
- Stored permanently in the document management system

```python
def generate_noc(loan_account_id):
    account = get_closed_loan_account(loan_account_id)
    assert account.status == 'closed', "Cannot generate NOC for open loan"
    
    noc_data = {
        'loan_account_number': account.account_number,
        'borrower_name': account.borrower.legal_name,
        'loan_amount': account.disbursed_amount,
        'disbursal_date': account.disbursal_date,
        'closure_date': account.closure_date,
        'final_payment_reference': account.final_payment_reference,
        'generated_at': utcnow(),
        'generated_by': 'system',
    }
    
    pdf = render_noc_template(noc_data)
    signed_pdf = digital_sign(pdf, certificate=lender_signing_cert)
    
    doc_id = document_store.save(
        content=signed_pdf,
        metadata={**noc_data, 'document_type': 'NOC'},
        immutable=True                    # cannot be overwritten or deleted
    )
    
    deliver_noc(loan_account_id, doc_id)  # email + DigiLocker upload
    update_loan_account(loan_account_id, noc_doc_id=doc_id, noc_generated_at=utcnow())
    
    return doc_id
```

**Q15. What is a loan write-off, and how does it differ from closure through repayment?**
A: A write-off is an accounting action in which the lender removes a non-performing loan from its books as an asset, recognizing it as an irrecoverable loss. It does **not** mean the borrower is legally released from the debt — collections and recovery efforts typically continue after write-off.

**Write-off vs. waiver:**
- **Write-off**: accounting treatment only. The loan is removed from the lender's balance sheet. The debt still legally exists; the lender can still sue, sell the debt to a collections agency, or report the borrower to the bureau.
- **Waiver / settlement**: the lender formally agrees to accept less than the full outstanding amount as complete satisfaction of the debt. The borrower is legally released from the remaining balance. Requires the borrower's agreement and a settlement deed.

**Write-off trigger criteria (typical):**
- DPD ≥ 180 days (RBI NPA classification in India)
- OR credit committee determination that recovery is unlikely regardless of DPD
- OR borrower death with no recoverable estate
- OR borrower bankruptcy adjudicated

**Write-off accounting entries:**
```
At write-off (DPD 180+):
  DR: Loan Loss Provision                 ← already provisioned against the asset
  DR: P&L — Loan Write-off Expense        ← any unprovisioned portion hits P&L directly
  CR: Loan Receivable (loan principal)    ← removes from balance sheet

  DR: Interest Receivable Suspense        ← accrued interest already in suspense (NPA)
  CR: P&L — Interest Suspense Reversal    ← recognizes unrecoverable interest

Post-write-off recovery (if borrower pays after write-off):
  DR: Cash / Bank                         ← actual receipt
  CR: P&L — Recovery from Written-off     ← recognized as income in period of recovery
```

**System state after write-off:**
- Loan account state → `written_off` (terminal, but recoverable to `recovery_in_progress` if payments resume)
- Bureau reporting: report as written-off (settled for less, if applicable)
- Collections: handoff to third-party collections agency or legal team
- Document retention: all records retained — write-off does not authorize purging loan data

**Q17. "A borrower claims they made a payment that is not reflected in their account. Walk through your investigation."**
A: A missing payment claim is one of the most sensitive issues in lending — it has regulatory, legal, and customer trust implications. The investigation must be systematic and completed quickly (most regulators require resolution within 30 days; a good LMS should resolve in 24–48 hours).

**Step 1 — Gather borrower's evidence (first 2 hours)**
Request from the borrower:
- Bank account statement showing the debit (not just a screenshot — a statement is harder to fabricate)
- Transaction reference number / UTR (Unique Transaction Reference for IMPS/NEFT/UPI)
- Payment date, amount, and the account/VPA they paid to

**Step 2 — Search the payment ledger**
```sql
-- Search by amount + date range (±3 days for value date lag)
SELECT * FROM loan_payments
WHERE loan_account_id = [account]
AND amount_received = [claimed_amount]
AND payment_date BETWEEN [claimed_date - 3] AND [claimed_date + 3]
AND status != 'reversed';

-- Search by channel reference (UTR) across ALL loans (payment may have hit wrong account)
SELECT lp.*, la.account_number, la.borrower_id
FROM loan_payments lp
JOIN loan_accounts la ON la.id = lp.loan_account_id
WHERE lp.channel_reference = [borrower_utr];
```

**Step 3 — Check payment channel records**

*If payment was via NACH:*
- Pull the NACH batch file for that date; check if the borrower's account was included in the batch and what the batch return status was.
- If the batch shows `SUCCESS`: the payment should be in the ledger — check for a processing bug.
- If the batch shows `RETURNED`: the payment bounced (NSF, mandate issue) — the borrower's bank may have debited and then reversed, or the borrower is confusing a debit that was later reversed.

*If payment was via UPI/IMPS/NEFT:*
- Query the payment processor (Razorpay, PayU, bank API) with the UTR number.
- If the processor confirms receipt: check if the webhook was delivered and processed; look for webhook delivery failures in the event log.
- If the processor shows no record: the borrower may have paid to the wrong VPA/account number.

**Step 4 — Check for processing failures**

```sql
-- Were there any failed payment processing jobs around that date?
SELECT * FROM payment_processing_jobs
WHERE status = 'failed'
AND created_at BETWEEN [claimed_date] AND [claimed_date + 2];

-- Were there any webhook delivery failures?
SELECT * FROM webhook_events
WHERE event_type = 'payment.received'
AND created_at BETWEEN [claimed_date] AND [claimed_date + 2]
AND processing_status = 'failed';
```

**Step 5 — Resolution paths:**

| Finding | Action |
|--|--|
| Payment found in ledger, not reflected on statement | Statement generation bug; regenerate statement; update borrower |
| UTR found — payment credited to wrong loan account | Move payment to correct account; regenerate both accounts' statements |
| Webhook failed — payment received but not processed | Manually process the payment; fix webhook retry logic; issue receipt |
| NACH batch bounced — bank debited then reversed | Show borrower their bank's reversal entry; no action on loan account |
| UTR not found in processor | Payment may not have reached the system; request bank trace (takes 3–5 banking days) |
| Payment found at processor but not in DB | Critical: money received, not recorded; immediate escalation; manual posting |

**Step 6 — Regulatory requirement:**
In India (RBI guidelines), complaints about payment discrepancies must be acknowledged within 3 days and resolved within 30 days. Document every step of the investigation in the CRM ticket; the resolution trail is a regulatory artifact.

def assign_to_agent(case_id, bucket):

    eligible_agents = get_agents(bucket=bucket, capacity_available=True)

        # Queue the case; assign when agent becomes available
        enqueue_for_assignment(case_id, bucket)
        return
    
    # Round-robin with load balancing (assign to agent with fewest open cases)
    agent = min(eligible_agents, key=lambda a: a.open_case_count)
    assign_case(case_id, agent_id=agent.id)
    notify_agent(agent, case_id)

|--|--|--|--|--|--|
| **Primary system** | LOS | LOS + Decisioning Engine | LOS → LMS handoff | LMS + Payment Processor | LMS + Doc Management |
| **Key data artifacts** | Application form, KYC docs, bureau pull | Underwriting decision, adverse action notice | Loan agreement, disbursal UTR | Payment ledger, amortization schedule | NOC, closure letter, GL entries |
| **Critical constraint** | Deduplication, consent capture | Adverse action compliance, fairness/bias | Idempotency, atomic activation | Correct payment application order | Immutable final state, document retention |
| **Primary failure mode** | Duplicate applications, KYC failures | Wrong risk tier, non-compliant adverse action | Double-disbursal, failed LMS activation | Misapplied payment, DPD computation error | Premature closure, missing lien release |
| **Regulatory hook** | KYC/AML, credit consent | ECOA, FCRA, RBI Fair Practices Code | RBI disbursement guidelines | NPA recognition norms, bureau reporting | NOC delivery SLA, record retention |
| **State machine role** | draft → submitted → approved | approved → offered → accepted | accepted → pending_disbursal → active | active → delinquent → defaulted | → closed / written_off |
| **Key idempotency need** | Deduplication on re-submission | Re-runs of decisioning job | Fund transfer + LMS activation | NACH batch reprocessing | NOC generation |
| **Engineering complexity** | Medium | High (ML pipeline, fairness) | High (payment + state atomicity) | High (schedule versioning, DPD) | Medium (but legally consequential) |

---

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level in lending fintech, "what is a loan lifecycle" is the entry question — the depth interviewers probe is whether you understand the *engineering consequences at each handoff*: why the LOS-to-LMS handoff must be atomic and idempotent (double-disbursal is a regulatory and financial catastrophe), why the amortization schedule must use `Decimal` not `float` (48 months of floating-point rounding is material), why payment application order is contractually and legally defined (applying principal before fees on a defaulted loan can create regulatory liability), and why the write-off state does not release the borrower's legal obligation (collections must continue regardless of accounting treatment). Have a concrete story — a DPD computation bug that incorrectly classified loans as delinquent, an amortization rounding error you discovered in a schedule audit, a NACH failure handling gap you identified and fixed, or a bureau reporting pipeline you built or debugged. The ability to connect loan mechanics to system design (state machines with version numbers for optimistic concurrency, append-only payment ledgers for audit integrity, versioned amortization schedules for prepayment handling) is what distinguishes a lending platform engineer from someone who has read the RBI Fair Practices Code.
