## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is an insurance premium, and what are its four structural components?**
A: An insurance premium is the price a policyholder pays for a defined set of risk coverage over a specified period. Unlike most product pricing, premium pricing is probabilistic — the insurer is pricing a future event whose occurrence, timing, and cost are uncertain. The insurer must charge enough in aggregate across all policyholders that total collected premiums, plus investment income on those premiums, exceed total claims paid plus operating expenses.

The premium has four structural components:

**1. Pure Premium (Loss Cost)**
The actuarially expected claim cost for this specific risk, expressed per unit of exposure (per car per year, per ₹1,000 of sum insured, per ₹100 of payroll).

```
Pure Premium = Expected Claim Frequency × Expected Claim Severity

Where:
  Claim Frequency = probability that a claim occurs within the policy period
  Claim Severity  = expected cost of a claim, given that one occurs

Example (auto insurance):
  Frequency = 0.08 (8% of insured vehicles file a claim per year)
  Severity   = ₹45,000 (average claim cost when a claim occurs)
  Pure Premium = 0.08 × ₹45,000 = ₹3,600 per vehicle per year
```

**2. Expense Loading**
The insurer's operational costs allocated to the policy: acquisition costs (agent commission, marketing), administrative costs (underwriting, policy issuance, customer service), and overhead allocation. Expressed as a percentage of gross premium (fixed expense ratio) or as a flat amount per policy.

```
Expense Loading = (Fixed Expenses per Policy) + (Variable Expense Rate × Gross Premium)

Typical ranges:
  Agent commission:      8–20% of premium
  Administrative:        5–15% of premium
  Profit & contingency:  3–5% of premium
  Total expense loading: 20–40% of premium (varies by product and distribution)
```

**3. Risk Margin / Profit Loading**
A buffer above the pure premium to account for:
- **Parameter uncertainty**: the frequency and severity estimates themselves have estimation error.
- **Process risk**: random variation around the expected claims (even with perfect estimates, actual claims can exceed expectations by chance).
- **Catastrophe risk**: low-frequency, high-severity events (floods, pandemics, earthquakes) that can produce claims far above expectation in a single period.

**4. Investment Income Credit**
Insurers collect premiums at the start of the policy period and pay claims later — sometimes months or years later (liability insurance, workers' compensation). The float between premium collection and claim payment generates investment income. This "time value of money" is credited back to reduce the required premium:

```
Investment Income Credit = Pure Premium × (1 - (1 / (1 + r)^t))

Where:
  r = expected investment yield
  t = average time between premium collection and claim payment (years)

Example: Pure Premium = ₹3,600, r = 6%, t = 0.5 years
  Credit = ₹3,600 × (1 - (1/1.03)) = ₹3,600 × 0.029 ≈ ₹105
  Net premium after investment credit: ₹3,600 - ₹105 = ₹3,495
```

**Gross Premium formula:**
```
Gross Premium = (Pure Premium - Investment Income Credit + Expense Loading + Profit Loading)
              / (1 - Variable Expense Rate)

The denominator adjustment is because variable expenses (commission) are a percentage
of gross premium — which itself includes those expenses. The formula must solve for
gross premium simultaneously.
```

**Q2. What is the difference between rate making and premium calculation, and why does the distinction matter for engineers?**
A: Rate making and premium calculation are two distinct processes that operate at different timescales and levels of aggregation. Conflating them leads to system designs that cannot handle regulatory filing, product repricing, or real-time quote generation correctly.

**Rate making (actuarial, periodic):**
Rate making is the actuarial process of determining the base rates and rating factors that will apply to an entire book of business over a future rating period. It is performed periodically (annually or semi-annually for most lines) by actuaries using historical loss data, trend analysis, and regulatory filing. The output is a rate manual — a table of base rates and relativities by risk characteristic.

- Operates on aggregate historical data (years of claims experience).
- Produces rates intended to be adequate in aggregate — not necessarily exact for any individual policy.
- Goes through regulatory approval in most jurisdictions before implementation.
- Engineering system: rate filing and rate manual management (versioned, auditable).

**Premium calculation (real-time, per-policy):**
Premium calculation is the application of approved rates and factors to the specific characteristics of an individual risk to produce a price for that specific policy. It happens in real time at the point of quote, bind, and renewal.

- Operates on individual policy data.
- Applies the current approved rate manual (base rates × rating factors × discounts × surcharges).
- Must be deterministic, auditable, and reproducible — the same inputs must always produce the same premium.
- Engineering system: rating engine — the real-time service that computes premiums for quotes, endorsements, and renewals.

**Why the distinction matters for engineers:**

1. **Rate changes require a new rate manual version, not a code change.** The rating engine should be data-driven — base rates and factors stored in a database, not hardcoded. A rate increase should be a data deployment, not a code deployment.

2. **The rate manual is a regulatory artifact.** In India (IRDAI), most retail insurance rates are either filed (submitted to regulator for approval) or are free pricing (commercial lines). The system must track which rate manual version was in effect when any policy was issued — for future audits and claims disputes.

3. **Premium calculation must be reproducible.** If a policyholder files a complaint about their premium 2 years after issuance, the system must be able to reproduce the exact premium calculation that was performed at the time of issuance, using the exact rate manual that was in effect at that time.

**Q3. What is a rating factor (relativty), and how does the rating engine apply multiple factors?**
A: A rating factor (or relativity) is a multiplier that adjusts the base rate up or down to reflect the relative risk of a specific characteristic. A factor of 1.20 means that characteristic increases the base premium by 20%; a factor of 0.85 means it reduces it by 15%.

**Rating factor example (auto insurance):**

| Factor Category | Characteristic | Factor |
|--|--|--|
| **Base rate** (vehicle class A, Zone 1) | — | ₹3,000/year |
| **Age band** | 18–25 years | 1.45 |
| **Age band** | 26–35 years | 1.10 |
| **Age band** | 36–55 years | 1.00 (base) |
| **Age band** | 55+ years | 1.15 |
| **Claims history** | 0 claims in 3 years | 0.85 |
| **Claims history** | 1 claim in 3 years | 1.10 |
| **Claims history** | 2+ claims in 3 years | 1.35 |
| **Vehicle age** | < 2 years | 1.15 |
| **Vehicle age** | 2–5 years | 1.00 (base) |
| **Vehicle age** | 5–10 years | 0.92 |
| **Vehicle age** | > 10 years | 0.80 |
| **Zone** | Metro | 1.20 |
| **Zone** | Urban | 1.00 (base) |
| **Zone** | Rural | 0.80 |
| **NCB (No Claim Bonus)** | 1 claim-free year | 0.80 |
| **NCB** | 3 claim-free years | 0.65 |
| **NCB** | 5+ claim-free years | 0.50 |

**How multiple factors are applied:**

The standard method is multiplicative:
```
Premium = Base Rate × Factor₁ × Factor₂ × ... × Factorₙ

Example:
  Base rate:             ₹3,000
  × Age 24 (18-25):      × 1.45 = ₹4,350
  × 0 claims:            × 0.85 = ₹3,698
  × Vehicle age 3 years: × 1.00 = ₹3,698
  × Metro zone:          × 1.20 = ₹4,437
  × NCB 1 year:          × 0.80 = ₹3,550
  = Gross OD Premium:    ₹3,550
```

**Multiplicative vs. additive models:**
- **Multiplicative** (standard): factors are independent multipliers. The interaction between a 24-year-old driver and a metro zone is captured implicitly (both raise the premium multiplicatively). Used for most retail insurance lines.
- **Additive**: factors are added to a base rate as incremental amounts. Used less frequently; loses the proportional nature of risk adjustment.
- **GLM-based** (Generalized Linear Model): the actuary fits a statistical model to claims data; the model outputs the "correct" multiplicative factors for each characteristic simultaneously, controlling for correlations between characteristics. The gold standard for rate making in sophisticated insurers.

## Section 2: Life Insurance Premium Models

**Q4. How is a term life insurance premium calculated, and what actuarial inputs does it require?**
A: Term life insurance pays a fixed sum assured (SA) to the nominee if the insured dies within the policy term. The premium must cover the expected death benefit plus expenses and profit loading.

**Core actuarial inputs:**

**1. Mortality Rate (qₓ):**
The probability that a person aged x dies within the next year. Sourced from a mortality table — in India, the IRDAI-prescribed Indian Assured Lives Mortality (IALM) 2006-08 table is used for pricing (with adjustments for reinsurance rates). The "assured lives" table is different from the general population mortality table — it reflects the mortality experience of insured populations, which tends to be lower due to underwriting selection.

```
IALM mortality rates (sample):
  q₂₅ = 0.00097   (0.097% probability of death for 25-year-old)
  q₃₅ = 0.00167   (0.167% for 35-year-old)
  q₄₅ = 0.00391   (0.391% for 45-year-old)
  q₅₅ = 0.00978   (0.978% for 55-year-old)
```

**2. Interest Rate / Discount Rate:**
The assumed rate at which future premium inflows and claim outflows are discounted to present value. In India, typically 5–7% per annum for traditional life products (more conservative than actual yields, providing a safety margin).

**3. Expense assumptions:**
Per-policy acquisition cost, annual renewal administration cost, and claim settlement cost. Usually expressed as a percentage of premium or as a flat amount.

**4. Profit loading:**
Target profit margin for the insurer, typically 5–15% of premium.

**Net Single Premium (NSP) for a 1-year term:**
```
NSP₁ = SA × qₓ × v

Where:
  SA  = Sum Assured (death benefit)
  qₓ  = probability of death in the year for age x
  v   = discount factor = 1 / (1 + i) [discount back half a year,
                                        as deaths assumed mid-year]

Example: SA = ₹50,00,000, x = 35, q₃₅ = 0.00167, i = 6%
  NSP₁ = 50,00,000 × 0.00167 × (1/1.06)^0.5
        = 50,00,000 × 0.00167 × 0.9713
        = ₹8,109 (expected present value of claim cost for 1 year)
```

**Net Annual Premium for a multi-year term (level premium):**

For a 20-year term policy, the insurer must compute a level annual premium that, if collected from all surviving policyholders each year, is exactly sufficient (in expected value) to pay all claims over the 20 years.

```python
from decimal import Decimal

def compute_term_life_premium(sum_assured, entry_age, term_years, interest_rate,
                               mortality_table, expense_ratio, profit_loading):
    """
    Compute level annual net premium for a term life policy
    using present value of expected benefits / present value of expected premiums
    """
    i = Decimal(str(interest_rate))
    v = Decimal('1') / (Decimal('1') + i)      # annual discount factor
    SA = Decimal(str(sum_assured))
    
    # Compute Net Single Premium = PV of expected future benefits
    apv_benefit = Decimal('0')  # Actuarial Present Value of death benefits
    
    # Survival probability: probability of being alive at start of each year
    lx = Decimal('1')   # starts at 1 (everyone alive at entry)
    
    for year in range(term_years):
        age = entry_age + year
        q_x = Decimal(str(mortality_table[age]))   # mortality rate at age x
        
        # Expected claim in this year:
        # lx * q_x people die; benefit SA paid at end of year; discounted by (year+1) years
        expected_claim = lx * q_x * SA * (v ** (year + 1))
        apv_benefit += expected_claim
        
        # Update survival probability for next year
        lx = lx * (Decimal('1') - q_x)
    
    # Compute PV of expected future premium payments (annuity)
    # Premium paid at start of each year, only if alive
    apv_premiums = Decimal('0')
    lx = Decimal('1')
    
    for year in range(term_years):
        age = entry_age + year
        q_x = Decimal(str(mortality_table[age]))
        
        # Premium received at start of year: lx people survive to pay
        # Discounted back year periods
        apv_premiums += lx * (v ** year)
        
        lx = lx * (Decimal('1') - q_x)
    
    # Net Annual Premium = APV of Benefits / APV of Premium Payments
    net_annual_premium = apv_benefit / apv_premiums
    
    # Gross Annual Premium = Net Premium adjusted for expenses and profit
    # Expense loading: proportion of gross premium + flat per-policy amount
    gross_premium = (net_annual_premium + FLAT_EXPENSE_PER_POLICY) / (
        Decimal('1') - Decimal(str(expense_ratio)) - Decimal(str(profit_loading))
    )
    
    return gross_premium.quantize(Decimal('0.01'))
```

**Underwriting adjustments:**
The base premium assumes standard mortality. Applicants with medical conditions, hazardous occupations, or family history of certain diseases are rated-up via:
- **Extra premium**: flat additional amount per ₹1,000 sum assured per year (for conditions with elevated mortality).
- **Substandard rating**: applying a higher age (rating the 35-year-old as a 40-year-old) for the mortality lookup.
- **Lien**: reducing the death benefit in early years (e.g., 50% of SA in year 1, 100% from year 3) for borderline cases.
- **Exclusion**: excluding specific causes of death (e.g., excluding aviation death for a pilot).
- **Decline**: refusing coverage entirely for very high-risk applicants.

**Q5. How does the premium model differ for whole life, endowment, and ULIPs?**
A: Each product structure requires a different premium model because the cash flows (benefits and premium payments) have different timing and certainty profiles.

**Whole Life Insurance:**
The policy covers the insured for their entire life — the claim is certain (everyone dies eventually); only the timing is uncertain. The premium must fund a benefit that will definitely be paid at some future unknown date.

```
NSP (Whole Life) = SA × Σ (qₓ₊ₜ × ₜpₓ × vᵗ⁺¹), t = 0 to ω-x

Where:
  ₜpₓ = probability of surviving from age x to age x+t
  ω   = limiting age (maximum life table age, typically 100 or 115)
```

Because the benefit is certain and may be paid decades away, the whole life premium is substantially higher than term premium for the same SA, and the policy accumulates a policy reserve (the present value of future obligations less the present value of future premiums) that grows over time.

**Endowment Policy:**
Pays the SA on death within the term, OR on survival to the end of the term — whichever comes first. It combines term insurance (death benefit) with a savings element (survival benefit).

```
NSP (Endowment) = NSP (Term) + SA × ₙpₓ × vⁿ

Where:
  NSP (Term)    = PV of expected death benefits (as computed above)
  SA × ₙpₓ × vⁿ = PV of survival benefit (SA × probability of surviving n years × discount)
```

The survival benefit (SA × ₙpₓ × vⁿ) dominates the premium for most endowment products — especially for healthy young policyholders where mortality is low. Endowment premiums are significantly higher than term premiums for the same SA.

**Unit-Linked Insurance Plans (ULIPs):**
ULIPs separate the investment and insurance components explicitly. The policyholder's premium is split:

```
Gross Premium
  - Allocation Charge (% of premium not invested: typically 0–5%)
  = Investible Premium
  
The investible premium buys "units" in the chosen fund(s) at NAV.

Monthly charges deducted from the unit account:
  - Mortality Charge (MC): cost of pure life cover for the month
    MC = (Sum at Risk × Mortality Rate for age) / 1,000
    Sum at Risk = Sum Assured - Fund Value (the "net amount at risk")
    
  - Fund Management Charge (FMC): % of fund value per annum
    (IRDAI cap: 1.35% for equity funds; deducted daily from NAV)
    
  - Policy Administration Charge: flat monthly amount
  - Surrender / Partial Withdrawal Charge (if applicable)
  
Fund Value (at any time t):
  = (Units held × NAV at time t)
  
The insurer guarantees death benefit = max(Sum Assured, Fund Value)
If Fund Value > Sum Assured: Sum at Risk → 0; mortality charge → 0
```

Engineering implication for ULIPs: the premium model is partially a real-time computation. NAV changes daily; mortality charges are deducted monthly based on current fund value; sum at risk changes with every NAV movement. The system must run nightly batch jobs to:
1. Fetch current NAV for each fund.
2. Compute current fund value for each policy.
3. Compute mortality charge = (SA - fund_value) × qₓ/12 for the month.
4. Deduct charges by canceling units at today's NAV.
5. Update policy records.

## Section 3: General Insurance Premium Models

**Q6. How is a motor insurance (Own Damage) premium calculated in India, and what does the IRDAI framework prescribe?**
A: Motor insurance in India has two components:
- **Third Party (TP) liability**: mandatory; tariff-based (rates set by IRDAI; insurers cannot compete on price).
- **Own Damage (OD)**: covers damage to the insured vehicle; detariffed since 2007; insurers can price freely using filed rating plans.

**Third Party Premium (IRDAI tariff, 2023–24 rates):**

| Vehicle type | Engine CC | Annual TP Premium |
|--|--|--|
| Private car | ≤ 1,000 cc | ₹2,094 |
| Private car | 1,000–1,500 cc | ₹3,416 |
| Private car | > 1,500 cc | ₹7,897 |
| Two-wheeler | ≤ 75 cc | ₹538 |
| Two-wheeler | 75–150 cc | ₹714 |
| Two-wheeler | 150–350 cc | ₹1,366 |
| Two-wheeler | > 350 cc | ₹2,804 |

Engineering note: TP rates change annually (IRDAI circular in April). The system must apply the correct rate based on the rate effective date, not the current date. A policy renewing in May 2025 uses the 2025–26 tariff; a policy renewed in March 2025 uses the 2024–25 tariff. Version-control the tariff table.

**Own Damage (OD) Premium — standard industry model:**

```
OD Premium = IDV × OD Rate × Rating Factors - NCB Discount + Add-ons

Where:
  IDV (Insured Declared Value) = current market value of the vehicle
      = Manufacturer's listed price × Depreciation factor
      
  Depreciation by vehicle age:
    < 6 months:  5%   → IDV factor = 0.95
    6m–1 year:  15%   → IDV factor = 0.85
    1–2 years:  20%   → IDV factor = 0.80
    2–3 years:  30%   → IDV factor = 0.70
    3–4 years:  40%   → IDV factor = 0.60
    4–5 years:  50%   → IDV factor = 0.50
    > 5 years:  agreed value (negotiated)
    
  OD Base Rate: varies by insurer's filed rating plan (typically 1.5–3.5% of IDV)
  
  Rating Factors: age of vehicle, make/model, geographic zone, cubic capacity,
                  claims history, fuel type (EV gets discount)
  
  NCB (No Claim Bonus):
    1 claim-free year:  20%
    2 claim-free years: 25%
    3 claim-free years: 35%
    4 claim-free years: 45%
    5+ claim-free years: 50%
```

**OD Premium Calculation — Full Example:**

```python
def compute_od_premium(vehicle_data, policy_data, rating_plan_version):
    rating = get_rating_plan(rating_plan_version)
    
    # Step 1: Compute IDV
    ex_showroom_price = vehicle_data.ex_showroom_price
    vehicle_age_months = (today() - vehicle_data.registration_date).days / 30
    depreciation_factor = rating.get_depreciation_factor(vehicle_age_months)
    idv = ex_showroom_price * depreciation_factor
    
    # Step 2: Base OD rate (from rating plan, by vehicle segment)
    base_od_rate = rating.get_base_od_rate(
        vehicle_segment=vehicle_data.segment,       # hatchback, sedan, SUV
        engine_cc=vehicle_data.engine_cc,
        fuel_type=vehicle_data.fuel_type            # petrol, diesel, EV, CNG
    )
    
    base_od_premium = idv * base_od_rate
    
    # Step 3: Apply rating factors (multiplicative)
    age_factor     = rating.get_age_factor(vehicle_age_months)
    zone_factor    = rating.get_zone_factor(policy_data.registration_zone)
    make_factor    = rating.get_make_model_factor(vehicle_data.make, vehicle_data.model)
    
    od_premium = base_od_premium * age_factor * zone_factor * make_factor
    
    # Step 4: NCB discount
    ncb_rate = rating.get_ncb_rate(policy_data.ncb_years)
    ncb_discount = od_premium * ncb_rate
    od_premium_after_ncb = od_premium - ncb_discount
    
    # Step 5: Add-ons (each priced separately)
    addon_premium = Decimal('0')
    for addon in policy_data.selected_addons:
        addon_premium += rating.get_addon_premium(addon, idv, vehicle_data)
    
    # Step 6: Total OD premium + TP + add-ons + GST
    tp_premium = rating.get_tp_premium(vehicle_data.engine_cc, policy_data.policy_year)
    
    gross_od = od_premium_after_ncb + addon_premium
    gross_tp = tp_premium
    
    gst_od = gross_od * Decimal('0.18')             # 18% GST on OD
    gst_tp = gross_tp * Decimal('0.18')             # 18% GST on TP
    
    total_premium = gross_od + gross_tp + gst_od + gst_tp
    
    return PremiumBreakdown(
        idv=idv,
        base_od_premium=base_od_premium,
        od_premium_after_factors=od_premium,
        ncb_discount=ncb_discount,
        od_premium_after_ncb=od_premium_after_ncb,
        addon_premium=addon_premium,
        tp_premium=tp_premium,
        gst_od=gst_od,
        gst_tp=gst_tp,
        total_premium=total_premium,
        rating_plan_version=rating_plan_version,
        idv_used=idv,
        ncb_rate_applied=ncb_rate,
        computed_at=utcnow()
    )
```

**Q7. How is a health insurance premium calculated, and what makes it more complex than motor insurance?**
A: Health insurance premium modeling is more complex than motor for three reasons: medical inflation outpaces general inflation by 2–4× annually (making loss trend adjustment critical), healthcare costs vary enormously by geography and hospital tier, and the insured population's risk profile changes over time as policyholders age and accumulate health conditions.

**Health insurance premium components:**

**1. Base Premium by Age Band and Sum Insured:**
Health risk rises sharply with age. The base rate is typically structured as a matrix of age band × sum insured level:

| Age Band | SI: ₹3L | SI: ₹5L | SI: ₹10L | SI: ₹25L |
|--|--|--|--|--|
| 18–25 | ₹4,200 | ₹6,100 | ₹10,500 | ₹22,000 |
| 26–35 | ₹5,500 | ₹7,800 | ₹13,200 | ₹27,500 |
| 36–45 | ₹8,800 | ₹12,500 | ₹21,000 | ₹43,000 |
| 46–55 | ₹14,500 | ₹20,800 | ₹35,500 | ₹72,000 |
| 56–65 | ₹24,000 | ₹34,500 | ₹58,000 | ₹1,18,000 |

**2. Rating Factors:**
- **Geography**: tier-1 cities (Mumbai, Delhi) have higher hospital costs than tier-2/3 cities. Factor range: 0.75 (rural) to 1.30 (metro).
- **Family Floater structure**: a family floater (shared SI among members) is priced differently from individual policies. The eldest member drives the base rate; additional members added at discounted rates.
- **Deductible / Co-pay**: if the policyholder agrees to pay the first ₹25,000 of any claim (deductible) or 10% of each claim (co-pay), the premium is reduced. The reduction is calculated from actuarial data on claim distribution:
  ```
  Deductible Discount Factor = 1 - E[min(Claim, Deductible)] / E[Claim]
  ```
- **Pre-existing conditions**: disclosed conditions at underwriting trigger a waiting period (typically 2–4 years) or premium loading. Engineering must capture the disclosed conditions, apply the appropriate waiting period to each condition in the claims adjudication system, and apply the loading to the premium.
- **No-claim bonus**: some health products offer a sum insured restoration or premium discount for claim-free years.

**3. Medical Trend (Loss Trend) Adjustment:**
Healthcare costs inflate at 10–15% per year in India. The rate making process must trend historical claim costs forward to the midpoint of the future rating period:

```
Trended Loss Cost = Historical Loss Cost × (1 + medical_trend)^trend_period

Example:
  Historical loss cost from 2022–23 data: ₹8,500 per policy
  Medical trend: 12% per annum
  Rating period midpoint: 2 years from data midpoint
  Trended loss cost = ₹8,500 × (1.12)² = ₹10,664
```

**4. Morbidity and Utilization Patterns:**
Unlike mortality (binary: alive or dead), health claims require modeling:
- **Claim frequency**: how often does a member file a claim per year? (typically 0.15–0.30 for individual health plans)
- **Claim severity distribution**: claims are not normally distributed. Small claims (outpatient, minor procedures) are frequent; catastrophic claims (organ transplant, cancer treatment) are rare but high-cost. The severity is typically modeled as a log-normal or Pareto distribution.
- **Hospitalization rates**: by age band, by geography, by type of hospitalization (medical vs. surgical vs. day care).

**Group health insurance vs. individual:**
Group health policies (employer-purchased) are priced on the group's own experience (if the group is large enough):

```python
def compute_group_health_premium(group_data, experience_data):
    n = group_data.member_count
    
    if n < 100:
        # Too small for credible experience; use manual (market) rates
        return compute_manual_rate(group_data) * n
    
    elif n < 1000:
        # Blend group experience with manual rate (credibility weighting)
        credibility_factor = compute_credibility(n)  # typically Z = sqrt(n/1000) capped at 1
        group_loss_cost = experience_data.incurred_claims / experience_data.earned_exposure
        manual_loss_cost = get_manual_loss_cost(group_data)
        
        blended_loss_cost = (credibility_factor * group_loss_cost +
                             (1 - credibility_factor) * manual_loss_cost)
        return blended_loss_cost * n * (1 + expense_ratio + profit_loading)
    
    else:
        # Large group: price entirely on own experience
        loss_ratio = experience_data.incurred_claims / experience_data.earned_premium
        renewal_rate = current_rate * (loss_ratio / TARGET_LOSS_RATIO) * trend_factor
        return renewal_rate
```

## Section 4: Advanced Rating Models

**Q8. What is a Generalized Linear Model (GLM) in insurance rating, and how does it produce rating factors?**
A: A Generalized Linear Model is the statistical workhorse of actuarial ratemaking. It replaces the manual one-variable-at-a-time analysis of historical data (which cannot control for correlations between rating variables) with a multivariate regression that simultaneously estimates the effect of each rating variable while holding all others constant.

**Why GLMs replaced univariate analysis:**
Suppose you observe that urban policyholders have 30% higher claims than rural. But urban policyholders also tend to be younger, drive newer cars, and have higher sums insured. Without controlling for these correlated variables, you cannot separate the true zone effect from the confounding age and vehicle effects. A GLM estimates the "pure" zone relativity after controlling for all other variables in the model.

**GLM structure for insurance:**

```
Link Function:   log(E[Y]) = β₀ + β₁X₁ + β₂X₂ + ... + βₙXₙ

Where:
  Y  = response variable (claim frequency, or claim severity, or pure premium)
  Xᵢ = rating variable (age band, zone, vehicle type, etc.) — usually indicator variables
  βᵢ = coefficient for variable i
  
Error Distribution:
  Claim frequency: Poisson (count data)
  Claim severity:  Gamma (positive, right-skewed)
  Pure premium:    Tweedie (handles zero-inflated positive data — combines frequency + severity)
```

**How GLM output translates to rating factors:**

The model produces a coefficient βᵢ for each level of each rating variable (relative to the base level). The rating factor for that level is:

```
Rating Factor = exp(βᵢ)

Example GLM output for zone variable:
  Base level: Zone Urban (coefficient = 0, factor = 1.00)
  Zone Metro: β = 0.182, factor = exp(0.182) = 1.20
  Zone Rural: β = -0.223, factor = exp(-0.223) = 0.80
  Zone Remote: β = -0.357, factor = exp(-0.357) = 0.70
```

These factors are then rounded to suitable precision, smoothed across bands if necessary, and filed with the regulator as the approved rating factors.

**Engineering the GLM pipeline:**

```python
import statsmodels.api as sm
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

def fit_frequency_model(claims_data):
    """
    Fit a Poisson GLM for claim frequency
    Response: claim count per exposure unit
    """
    # Prepare data
    X = pd.get_dummies(claims_data[['age_band', 'zone', 'vehicle_segment',
                                    'ncb_years', 'fuel_type']], drop_first=True)
    X = sm.add_constant(X)
    
    # Poisson GLM with log link
    model = sm.GLM(
        endog=claims_data['claim_count'],
        exog=X,
        exposure=claims_data['earned_exposure'],   # account for exposure (policy-years)
        family=sm.families.Poisson(link=sm.families.links.log())
    )
    
    result = model.fit()
    
    # Extract rating factors
    factors = pd.DataFrame({
        'variable': result.params.index,
        'coefficient': result.params.values,
        'factor': np.exp(result.params.values),
        'p_value': result.pvalues.values,
        'significant': result.pvalues.values < 0.05
    })
    
    # Drop variables that are not statistically significant
    # (high p-value = no evidence this variable affects claim rate)
    significant_factors = factors[factors['significant']]
    
    return result, significant_factors

def validate_model(result, holdout_data):
    """
    Validate GLM on holdout data not used in fitting
    """
    predictions = result.predict(holdout_data)
    actual = holdout_data['claim_count']
    
    # Lift chart: rank predictions into deciles; check if top decile has highest actual
    holdout_data['predicted'] = predictions
    deciles = pd.qcut(holdout_data['predicted'], q=10, labels=False)
    lift = holdout_data.groupby(deciles).agg(
        mean_predicted=('predicted', 'mean'),
        mean_actual=('claim_count', 'mean')
    )
    
    # Gini coefficient: measures how well model discriminates between low and high risk
    gini = compute_gini(actual, predictions)
    
    return ModelValidation(lift_chart=lift, gini=gini,
                           aic=result.aic, deviance=result.deviance)
```

**Q9. What is a credibility model, and when is it used instead of a GLM?**
A: Credibility theory addresses the question: how much weight should we give to a specific risk's own historical experience versus the broader market (manual) rate? It is most commonly used in commercial lines pricing (where each risk is unique and may have its own loss history) and group insurance renewal pricing.

**The core credibility formula (Bühlmann):**
```
Credibility-Weighted Rate = Z × Observed Rate + (1 - Z) × Manual Rate

Where:
  Z = credibility factor, 0 ≤ Z ≤ 1
  
  Z = n / (n + k)

  n = number of observed claims (or policy-years)
  k = Bühlmann credibility parameter = Expected Value of Process Variance /
                                        Variance of Hypothetical Means
      (k is estimated from a portfolio of similar risks)
```

**Practical credibility formula for group insurance:**
```python
def compute_credibility_weighted_rate(group_id, experience_years=3):
    group = get_group(group_id)
    
    # Collect experience data (last 3 years, trend-adjusted)
    experience = get_trended_experience(group_id, years=experience_years)
    
    n = experience.total_member_years    # exposure in member-years
    K = GROUP_HEALTH_CREDIBILITY_K       # typically 1,000–3,000 for health
    
    Z = n / (n + K)
    
    # Observed loss rate (actual incurred claims per member-year, trended)
    observed_rate = experience.incurred_claims_trended / n
    
    # Manual rate: what the market charges for this group's demographic profile
    manual_rate = compute_manual_rate(group.demographics)
    
    # Credibility-weighted rate
    credibility_rate = Z * observed_rate + (1 - Z) * manual_rate
    
    # Apply expense loading and profit
    gross_rate = credibility_rate * (1 + expense_ratio + profit_loading)
    
    # Renewal premium
    renewal_premium = gross_rate * group.projected_member_years_next_year
    
    return RenewalQuote(
        manual_rate=manual_rate,
        observed_rate=observed_rate,
        credibility_factor=Z,
        credibility_rate=credibility_rate,
        gross_rate=gross_rate,
        renewal_premium=renewal_premium
    )
```

**When to use credibility vs. GLM:**
- **GLM**: when you have a large portfolio of retail policies (thousands to millions) and need to set rates that apply to all policyholders of a given characteristic. The GLM uses the collective experience of the entire portfolio to estimate each factor.
- **Credibility**: when each risk (or group) is individually rated, and you want to blend that risk's own experience with the market rate. Used in commercial lines, workers' compensation for large employers, group health renewal.

**Q10. What is telematics-based (Usage-Based Insurance) rating, and how does it change the premium engine?**
A: Usage-Based Insurance (UBI) uses real-time driving behavior data — collected via a smartphone app or OBD-II dongle — to modify the premium based on how the vehicle is actually driven, not just static characteristics like age and zone.

**UBI rating factors (behavioral telematics):**

| Behavior | Metric | Premium Impact |
|--|--|--|
| **Speeding** | % time driven > 20 kmph over limit | High risk — significant surcharge |
| **Hard braking** | Events per 100 km > threshold | High risk — moderate surcharge |
| **Hard acceleration** | Events per 100 km > threshold | Moderate risk |
| **Night driving** | % miles driven 11 PM–5 AM | Higher risk — surcharge |
| **Mileage** | Total km driven per year | More exposure = more risk |
| **Distraction** | Phone usage while driving | Very high risk |
| **Time of day** | Peak hour vs. off-peak driving % | Peak = higher accident rate |
| **Cornering** | G-force events per 100 km | Moderate risk signal |

**UBI premium computation architecture:**

```python
class UBIRatingEngine:
    def compute_telematics_score(self, policy_id, period_start, period_end):
        # Pull raw telematics events for the period
        events = telematics_service.get_events(
            device_id=get_device(policy_id),
            start=period_start,
            end=period_end
        )
        
        # Aggregate into behavioral metrics
        total_km = sum(t.distance_km for t in events)
        total_hours = sum(t.duration_hours for t in events)
        
        metrics = TelematicsMetrics(
            avg_speed_score     = compute_speed_score(events, total_km),
            hard_braking_rate   = count_hard_braking(events) / max(total_km / 100, 1),
            hard_accel_rate     = count_hard_accel(events) / max(total_km / 100, 1),
            night_driving_pct   = compute_night_pct(events, total_hours),
            phone_use_rate      = compute_phone_usage(events, total_hours),
            mileage_km          = total_km,
            peak_hour_pct       = compute_peak_hour_pct(events, total_hours)
        )
        
        # Composite telematics score (0–100; higher = safer driver)
        score = (
            WEIGHT_SPEED      * metrics.avg_speed_score +
            WEIGHT_BRAKING    * normalize_braking(metrics.hard_braking_rate) +
            WEIGHT_ACCEL      * normalize_accel(metrics.hard_accel_rate) +
            WEIGHT_NIGHT      * normalize_night(metrics.night_driving_pct) +
            WEIGHT_PHONE      * normalize_phone(metrics.phone_use_rate) +
            WEIGHT_MILEAGE    * normalize_mileage(metrics.mileage_km)
        )
        
        return TelemaTicsResult(score=score, metrics=metrics,
                                period_km=total_km)
    
    def compute_ubi_premium_adjustment(self, policy_id, telematics_score):
        # Map score to premium discount/surcharge
        # Score 80-100: safe driver discount (up to 30%)
        # Score 60-80:  standard premium
        # Score 40-60:  moderate surcharge (up to 15%)
        # Score < 40:   high-risk surcharge (up to 40%)
        
        if telematics_score >= 80:
            adjustment = -0.30 * ((telematics_score - 80) / 20)  # 0 to -30%
        elif telematics_score >= 60:
            adjustment = 0.0
        elif telematics_score >= 40:
            adjustment = 0.15 * ((60 - telematics_score) / 20)   # 0 to +15%
        else:
            adjustment = 0.15 + 0.25 * ((40 - telematics_score) / 40)  # 15% to 40%
        
        return adjustment
```

**Engineering challenges unique to UBI:**
- **Data volume**: a single vehicle generates 50–200 MB of raw GPS/accelerometer data per month. At 100,000 policies, that is 5–20 TB/month. The telematics pipeline must handle this at scale without impacting the real-time rating API.
- **Data quality**: GPS signal loss (tunnels, parking garages), OBD-II disconnection, and phone battery death create gaps. The rating model must handle incomplete telematics data gracefully — either using whatever data is available or falling back to standard rating for the period.
- **Privacy and consent**: telematics data is location data — DPDP Act (India) and GDPR (EU) classify it as sensitive personal data. Explicit, granular consent must be obtained and data minimized (aggregate behavior scores, not raw GPS traces, should be retained beyond the policy period).
- **Regulatorily compliant premium bounds**: even with UBI, IRDAI requires that the premium remain within bounds of what the base rate manual would charge — UBI cannot reduce the premium to zero or create arbitrarily large surcharges beyond filed limits.

## Section 5: Reinsurance and Portfolio Pricing

**Q11. How does reinsurance affect the premium a policyholder pays, and how is the reinsurance cost built into pricing?**
A: Reinsurance is insurance purchased by an insurer from a reinsurer to protect against large or catastrophic losses. It does not directly appear in the policyholder's premium, but it is a cost the insurer must recover in the gross premium it charges. Understanding reinsurance is essential for engineers building catastrophe-exposed products (home, commercial property, crop insurance).

**Types of reinsurance relevant to pricing:**

**Quota Share (proportional):**
The insurer cedes a fixed percentage of every premium and every loss to the reinsurer.
```
Example: 30% quota share
  Policy premium: ₹10,000
  Ceded to reinsurer: ₹3,000 (30%)
  Retained by insurer: ₹7,000 (70%)
  
  If claim occurs: ₹1,00,000
  Reinsurer pays: ₹30,000 (30%)
  Insurer pays: ₹70,000 (70%)
  
  Reinsurance cost in pricing: the insurer prices the full ₹10,000 premium
  but effectively only retains risk on ₹70,000 of exposure.
  The reinsurer pays a "ceding commission" back to the insurer to cover
  acquisition costs: typically 20–30% of ceded premium.
```

**Excess of Loss (XL / non-proportional):**
The reinsurer pays losses above a retention (attachment point) up to a limit.
```
Example: ₹50L xs ₹10L (reinsurer pays losses between ₹10L and ₹60L)
  
  Claim of ₹8L:   insurer pays ₹8L; reinsurer pays ₹0
  Claim of ₹25L:  insurer pays ₹10L; reinsurer pays ₹15L
  Claim of ₹70L:  insurer pays ₹10L; reinsurer pays ₹50L; insurer pays ₹10L above cap

Reinsurance cost: the insurer pays a "reinsurance premium" (Rate on Line — RoL)
  RoL = Reinsurance Premium / Reinsurance Limit
  Typical RoL for cat XL: 2–10% depending on attachment and peril

Building XL cost into policy pricing:
  Expected Reinsurance Cost per Policy = (Reinsurance Premium) / (Policy Count)
  This is added to the gross premium as a reinsurance cost loading.
```

**Catastrophe (Cat) Modeling:**
For natural catastrophe perils (earthquake, cyclone, flood), the expected claim is too rare to estimate from historical data alone. Insurers use probabilistic catastrophe models (RMS, AIR, CoreLogic, India-specific models for IRDAI filings) that:
1. Define the hazard (frequency and severity of storms, earthquakes by location).
2. Model vulnerability (how much damage does a building of this construction type sustain in a storm of given intensity).
3. Compute the loss distribution for the insurer's portfolio.

The output is an Occurrence Exceedance Probability (OEP) curve — the probability that an event produces a loss above a given level — and an Annual Average Loss (AAL) per policy. The AAL is incorporated into the premium:

```
Cat Loading in Premium = AAL × (1 + risk margin) + XL Reinsurance Cost Loading
```

**Q12. What is loss reserving, and why do engineers need to understand it for premium systems?**
A: Loss reserving is the estimation of the total amount an insurer will ultimately pay for claims that have already occurred but are not yet fully settled. It is a balance sheet obligation — the reserve appears as a liability on the insurer's balance sheet.

**Why engineers need to understand reserving:**
1. **Reported vs. Incurred**: claims data used for ratemaking must be put on an "ultimate" basis (total expected payments including future development of open claims). If you use only paid claims, you underestimate the true cost and underprice.
2. **IBNR (Incurred But Not Reported)**: some claims have occurred but the insurer hasn't yet been notified. The reserving actuary estimates IBNR using development patterns. If the premium system uses raw reported claim data without IBNR, it misprices.
3. **Claim development triangles**: the standard tool for estimating development. Understanding the triangle helps engineers build data pipelines that produce correctly formatted triangles for actuarial use.

**Chain Ladder development triangle:**
```
Accident   Claims Paid by Development Year (cumulative, ₹ thousands)
Year      12 months  24 months  36 months  48 months  Ultimate
2020        4,500      7,200      8,500      9,100      9,500 (estimated)
2021        5,100      8,100      9,600      ?
2022        5,800      9,200      ?
2023        6,400      ?

Development factors (link ratios):
  12→24: avg(7200/4500, 8100/5100, 9200/5800) = 1.602
  24→36: avg(8500/7200, 9600/8100) = 1.139
  36→48: 9100/8500 = 1.071
  48→Ult: 9500/9100 = 1.044

Ultimate estimate for 2023:
  = 6,400 × 1.602 × 1.139 × 1.071 × 1.044 = ₹12,900 thousand
  IBNR = 12,900 - 6,400 = ₹6,500 thousand
```

**Engineering the claims triangle pipeline:**
```sql
-- Claims triangle view for actuarial use
CREATE VIEW claims_triangle AS
SELECT
    accident_year,
    development_year,
    SUM(paid_amount) AS cumulative_paid,
    COUNT(DISTINCT claim_id) AS claim_count,
    SUM(case_reserve) AS open_reserve
FROM (
    SELECT
        EXTRACT(YEAR FROM claim.incident_date) AS accident_year,
        EXTRACT(YEAR FROM AGE(payment.payment_date, claim.incident_date))
            + 1 AS development_year,   -- 1 = first 12 months of development
        payment.amount AS paid_amount,
        claim.id AS claim_id,
        claim.current_reserve AS case_reserve
    FROM claims
    JOIN claim_payments payment ON payment.claim_id = claims.id
) sub
GROUP BY accident_year, development_year
ORDER BY accident_year, development_year;
```

```python
class RatingEngine:
    def compute_premium(self, inputs, rate_manual_version=None):
        # Resolve rate manual: use specified version or current effective
        rate_manual = self.get_rate_manual(
            product=inputs.product_code,
            version=rate_manual_version,
            effective_date=inputs.policy_start_date
        )
        
        trace = FactorTrace(rate_manual_id=rate_manual.id)
        
        # Base rate
        base_rate = rate_manual.get_base_rate(inputs.risk_segment)
        trace.add('base_rate', base_rate, inputs.risk_segment)
        
        premium = inputs.sum_insured * base_rate
        trace.add('base_premium', premium)
        
        # Apply each rating factor
        for factor_category in rate_manual.factor_categories:
            factor_key = inputs.get_factor_key(factor_category)
            factor_value = rate_manual.get_factor(factor_category, factor_key)
            
            premium = premium * factor_value
            trace.add(factor_category, factor_value, factor_key)
        
        # Discounts
        for discount in inputs.applicable_discounts:
            discount_value = rate_manual.get_discount(discount)
            premium = premium * (1 - discount_value)
            trace.add(f'discount_{discount}', -discount_value)
        
        # GST
        gst = premium * GST_RATE
        trace.add('gst', GST_RATE)
        
        total = premium + gst
        
        # Store audit record
        calculation = PremiumCalculation(
            inputs=inputs.to_dict(),
            factor_trace=trace.to_dict(),
            output={'net_premium': premium, 'gst': gst, 'total': total},
            total_premium=total,
            rate_manual_version=rate_manual.version
        )
        db.insert('premium_calculations', calculation)
        
        return PremiumResult(
            net_premium=premium,
            gst=gst,
            total_premium=total,
            factor_trace=trace,
            rate_manual_version=rate_manual.version
        )
```

**Q14. How does the rating engine handle mid-term policy changes (endorsements), and what are the premium adjustment rules?**
A: An endorsement is a change to an in-force policy — adding a driver, changing the insured vehicle, increasing sum insured, or changing the policy address. Each endorsement may require a premium adjustment (additional premium or return premium) for the remainder of the policy period.

**Pro-rata vs. short-rate adjustment:**

```
Pro-rata adjustment (most common — used for insurer-initiated changes):
  Additional/Return Premium = Full-year Premium Difference × (Remaining Days / 365)

  Example: Sum insured increased from ₹10L to ₹15L with 200 days remaining
    Full-year premium at ₹10L: ₹8,000
    Full-year premium at ₹15L: ₹11,500
    Annual difference: ₹3,500
    Additional premium = ₹3,500 × (200/365) = ₹1,918

Short-rate adjustment (used for policyholder-initiated cancellations):
  The refund is less than pro-rata — the insurer retains a short-rate penalty
  because it incurred acquisition costs that it cannot recover proportionally.
  
  Short-rate retention schedule (example):
    Days elapsed:  0–30:  20% of annual premium retained
    Days elapsed: 31–60:  30% retained
    Days elapsed: 61–90:  40% retained
    Days elapsed: 91–180: 50% retained
    Days elapsed: 181+:   65% retained
    
  Refund = Annual Premium × (1 - Short-rate Retention %)
```

**Endorsement premium engine:**
```python
def compute_endorsement_premium(policy_id, endorsement_changes, endorsement_date):
    policy = get_policy(policy_id)
    remaining_days = (policy.expiry_date - endorsement_date).days
    days_in_year = 365
    
    # Compute full-year premium BEFORE the change
    pre_change_inputs = build_rating_inputs(policy, as_of=endorsement_date)
    pre_change_premium = rating_engine.compute_premium(pre_change_inputs)
    
    # Compute full-year premium AFTER the change
    post_change_inputs = build_rating_inputs(policy, changes=endorsement_changes,
                                             as_of=endorsement_date)
    post_change_premium = rating_engine.compute_premium(post_change_inputs)
    
    # Annual premium difference
    annual_diff = post_change_premium.net_premium - pre_change_premium.net_premium
    
    # Pro-rata adjustment
    endorsement_premium = annual_diff * Decimal(remaining_days) / Decimal(days_in_year)
    gst = endorsement_premium * GST_RATE
    
    if endorsement_premium > 0:
        return EndorsementResult(
            type='additional_premium',
            net_premium=endorsement_premium,
            gst=gst,
            total=endorsement_premium + gst
        )
    else:
        return EndorsementResult(
            type='return_premium',
            net_premium=abs(endorsement_premium),
            gst=abs(gst),
            total=abs(endorsement_premium + gst)
        )
```

**Q16. "The actuarial team wants to A/B test a new GLM-based rating plan against the current rule-based plan. How do you engineer the experiment?"**
A: A rating plan A/B test in insurance is more constrained than a typical web A/B test because:
- Both plans must be IRDAI-filed and approved before use (in tariffed lines).
- The same policyholder cannot receive different rates at renewal — they have contractual renewal price expectations.
- The winner must be determined quickly enough to minimize adverse selection (if the new plan systematically underprices certain risk segments, those segments will buy; you'll discover this from losses, not from the A/B metric alone).

**Experiment design:**

```python
class RatingPlanExperiment:
    def __init__(self, control_version, treatment_version, allocation_pct=0.20):
        self.control = control_version       # current rule-based plan
        self.treatment = treatment_version   # new GLM-based plan
        self.allocation = allocation_pct     # 20% to treatment initially
    
    def assign_variant(self, quote_request):
        # Assignment must be:
        # 1. Deterministic for the same applicant (same person always gets same variant)
        # 2. Based on a stable applicant identifier, not quote ID
        # 3. Stratified by risk segment (to avoid random imbalance)
        
        applicant_hash = hash_sha256(quote_request.pan_or_mobile)
        segment_bucket = get_risk_segment(quote_request)
        
        # Stratified assignment: ensure equal representation across segments
        segment_allocation = self.get_segment_allocation(segment_bucket)
        
        if (int(applicant_hash, 16) % 100) < (segment_allocation * 100):
            return 'treatment'
        else:
            return 'control'
    
    def compute_both_premiums(self, quote_request):
        control_premium = rating_engine.compute_premium(
            quote_request, rate_manual_version=self.control
        )
        treatment_premium = rating_engine.compute_premium(
            quote_request, rate_manual_version=self.treatment
        )
        
        variant = self.assign_variant(quote_request)
        
        # Store both computations for analysis
        store_experiment_record(
            quote_id=quote_request.quote_id,
            experiment_id=self.id,
            variant=variant,
            control_premium=control_premium.total_premium,
            treatment_premium=treatment_premium.total_premium,
            premium_shown=control_premium if variant == 'control' else treatment_premium,
            risk_segment=get_risk_segment(quote_request)
        )
        
        # Return the assigned variant's premium
        return control_premium if variant == 'control' else treatment_premium
```

**Metrics for rating plan evaluation (beyond standard A/B metrics):**
- **Conversion rate by risk segment**: does the new plan change take-up rate for specific segments? A large increase in take-up for high-risk segments signals underpricing.
- **Predicted vs. actual loss ratio at 6 and 12 months**: the real test is whether the new plan's predictions are accurate.
- **Lift chart**: among policies that bound, does the treatment plan better rank-order risks (highest-predicted-risk policies actually have the highest loss ratios)?
- **Adverse selection detection**: monitor if the treatment plan is disproportionately attracting risks that the control plan would have priced higher (a sign the treatment is underpricing those risks).

**IRDAI compliance for the experiment:**
- Both rate manual versions must be filed with IRDAI before use (even for A/B testing — you cannot charge an unfiled rate to policyholders).
- The experiment must not persist — once a winner is declared, all new business transitions to the winning version.
- Existing policyholders must renew at the same version they were originally quoted under (no mid-policy rate changes).

**Q17. "Your premium for a specific risk segment has been consistently loss-making for 3 years. Walk through your analysis and remediation."**
A: A persistently loss-making segment is a pricing adequacy problem, a selection problem, or both. The diagnosis must separate these before the remediation can be correct.

**Step 1 — Quantify the loss and isolate the segment**
```sql
SELECT
    risk_segment,
    SUM(earned_premium)                                   AS earned_premium,
    SUM(incurred_claims_ultimate)                         AS ultimate_claims,
    SUM(incurred_claims_ultimate) / SUM(earned_premium)   AS loss_ratio,
    SUM(expenses_allocated)                               AS expenses,
    (SUM(incurred_claims_ultimate) + SUM(expenses_allocated))
        / SUM(earned_premium)                             AS combined_ratio,
    COUNT(DISTINCT policy_id)                             AS policy_count,
    AVG(incurred_claims_ultimate / earned_premium)        AS avg_per_policy_lr
FROM policy_performance
WHERE accident_year BETWEEN 2021 AND 2023
GROUP BY risk_segment
HAVING SUM(incurred_claims_ultimate) / SUM(earned_premium) > 1.0
ORDER BY loss_ratio DESC;
```

**Step 2 — Diagnose the cause:**

*Hypothesis A: Pure pricing inadequacy (rates are too low for this segment's actual risk)*
- Compare the segment's loss cost to what the current rating plan predicted.
- If predicted loss cost = ₹3,500/policy and actual = ₹5,200/policy: the plan is under-estimating risk by 49%. This is a rating plan deficiency.
- Root cause: the GLM/rating factors were calibrated on older data; the segment's risk has increased (urban congestion, inflation of spare parts costs, or fraud) and rates haven't kept pace.

*Hypothesis B: Adverse selection (the pricing is adequate in theory, but bad risks are buying this product while good risks are going elsewhere)*
- Compare the loss ratio of this segment to the same segment in the overall market (industry data from IRDAI's data hub).
- If the industry loss ratio for this segment is 65% but yours is 110%: you are attracting worse-than-average risks within the segment.
- Root cause: your rating plan is not granular enough — within the segment, you're pricing all risks the same, but competitors use more variables to identify and price the good risks better. The good risks go to competitors; the bad risks come to you.

*Hypothesis C: Claims handling or fraud problem*
- High loss ratio driven by large average severity rather than high frequency: possible fraud or leakage in claims (inflated repair bills, fake claims, collusion with workshops).
- Pull the claims data for this segment: look at average severity vs. industry benchmark; claim-to-premium ratio per workshop; suspicious patterns (same workshop, same agent, multiple claims per vehicle).

**Step 3 — Remediation by diagnosis:**

| Diagnosis | Remediation |
|--|--|
| Pricing inadequacy | File a rate increase for this segment; implement immediately on renewals; exit if rate increase insufficient |
| Adverse selection — insufficient rating variables | Add new rating variables (telematics, claims history at more granular level, anti-theft device); re-file the rating plan |
| Adverse selection — competitor undercut | Analyze where the good risks are going; decide if matching competitors is viable or exit the segment |
| Claims fraud | Enhanced fraud screening for this segment; audit workshop network; refer suspicious claims to SIU (Special Investigation Unit) |
| Medical / cost trend not reflected | Accelerate trend factor in rate filing; add claim inflation clause |

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level in insurtech, "how is a premium calculated" is the entry question — the depth interviewers probe is whether you understand the *engineering consequences*: why the rating engine must be data-driven not code-driven (a rate change is a data deployment — any insurer that requires a code release for every rate change is operationally crippled), why premium computations must be stored with a full factor trace (IRDAI can ask you to justify any premium charged to any policyholder, and "the system computed it" is not an acceptable answer), why ULIPs require nightly batch jobs that recompute mortality charges against the current NAV (the sum at risk changes every day, so the insurance cost changes every day), why motor TP rates are tariffed and cannot be competed on (IRDAI sets them — any system that attempts to apply a discount to TP premium is producing a non-compliant policy), and why GLM-derived rating factors must be statistically validated before being filed (a factor with a high p-value is noise, not signal, and basing rates on it creates both pricing inadequacy and regulatory risk). Have a concrete story — a rating plan versioning system you built, a UBI telematics pipeline you designed, a loss-making segment analysis you conducted, or a mid-term endorsement premium engine you implemented. The ability to connect actuarial concepts to system design choices (versioned rate manuals in a database, idempotent premium computation with full audit trace, credibility-weighted renewal pricing, claims triangle data pipelines for actuarial use) is what distinguishes an insurance platform engineer from someone who has read an actuarial textbook.
