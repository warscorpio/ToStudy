# Payment Lifecycle: Retry Logic for Payment Failures — Retryable vs Non-Retryable — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is the payment lifecycle, and where does retry logic fit in it?**
A: The payment lifecycle is the end-to-end sequence of states a payment traverses from initiation to final settlement. The canonical stages are:

- **Initiation** — the customer authorizes a charge; the merchant system creates a payment intent/order record.
- **Authorization** — the issuing bank approves or declines the hold on the customer's funds. No money moves yet.
- **Capture** — the merchant instructs the payment network to collect the authorized funds (can be immediate or deferred).
- **Settlement** — funds clear through the payment network (Visa/Mastercard/ACH) and are deposited into the merchant's account. Typically T+1 to T+2 days.
- **Reconciliation** — the merchant matches settled amounts against their internal order records and identifies discrepancies.
- **Dispute / Chargeback** — post-settlement, a cardholder disputes a charge; funds are held or reversed.

Retry logic primarily operates at the **Authorization** and **Capture** stages, and in some architectures at **Settlement** for ACH returns. The core challenge is that not all failures are equal — retrying a non-retryable failure wastes money (interchange fees, processor per-attempt fees), degrades customer experience, and in the worst case triggers fraud detection systems that flag the merchant.

**Q2. What is the difference between a hard decline and a soft decline?**
A: This is the foundational distinction in payment retry logic:

- **Hard decline (non-retryable)** — the issuing bank has made a permanent, definitive decision. The card cannot be charged — period. Retrying the same card with the same details will produce the same result. Examples: card reported stolen, account closed, card number invalid, transaction blocked by issuer fraud rules permanently.
- **Soft decline (retryable)** — the failure is transient or conditional. The same card *can* succeed under different conditions — at a different time, with a different amount, after the customer takes an action (like updating billing details or calling their bank). Examples: insufficient funds at this moment, temporary hold by issuer, card velocity limit hit, network timeout.

The business logic consequence: a hard decline should immediately surface to the customer with a request to use a different payment method. A soft decline should trigger automated retry logic with appropriate back-off, and only escalate to the customer after retry exhaustion.

**Q3. What are the main failure categories in a payment flow, and which layer owns each?**
A: Failures come from multiple layers, and the retry strategy differs by layer:

| Failure Layer | Example | Retryable? | Owner |
|--|--|--|--|
| **Network / Infrastructure** | TCP timeout to processor, DNS failure | Yes | Your system |
| **Payment Processor** | Processor gateway down, rate limit hit | Yes (with back-off) | Processor SLA |
| **Card Network** | Visa/Mastercard routing failure | Yes (rare) | Card scheme |
| **Issuing Bank — Soft** | Insufficient funds, temporary hold | Yes (time-delayed) | Issuer |
| **Issuing Bank — Hard** | Stolen card, account closed | No | Issuer |
| **Fraud / Risk Engine** | Your own risk model blocks the charge | Depends on rule | Your risk team |
| **Compliance / Regulatory** | Sanctions match, AML block | No | Legal/Compliance |

A key architectural principle: **failure classification must happen before retry logic executes**. Your system should parse the response code, map it to a category, and make a retry/no-retry decision in a single deterministic function — not ad-hoc throughout the codebase.

## Section 2: Decline Codes In Depth

**Q4. What are the standard ISO 8583 response codes, and which are retryable?**
A: ISO 8583 is the messaging standard used by card networks for authorization responses. The response code (Field 39) determines the outcome. Key codes:

**Non-retryable (hard declines):**
| Code | Meaning | Why Never Retry |
|--|--|--|
| `05` | Do Not Honor | Issuer permanent block; retrying triggers fraud flags |
| `14` | Invalid Card Number | Card number fails Luhn check or doesn't exist |
| `41` | Lost Card, Pick Up | Card reported lost; fraud risk if retried |
| `43` | Stolen Card, Pick Up | Card reported stolen; legal exposure if retried |
| `54` | Expired Card | Card expiry has passed |
| `57` | Transaction Not Permitted to Cardholder | Issuer blocked this transaction type for this card |
| `62` | Restricted Card | Blocked by issuer policy (e.g., geographic restriction) |

**Retryable (soft declines):**
| Code | Meaning | Retry Strategy |
|--|--|--|
| `51` | Insufficient Funds | Retry at billing cycle reset (e.g., 3 days later) |
| `61` | Exceeds Withdrawal Limit | Retry with lower amount, or next day |
| `65` | Exceeds Withdrawal Frequency Limit | Retry next day |
| `75` | PIN Tries Exceeded | Retry after 24h cooling period |
| `91` | Issuer or Switch Inoperative | Retry after 30–60 min (issuer system down) |
| `96` | System Malfunction | Retry after short back-off (network/processor issue) |

**Ambiguous (context-dependent):**
| Code | Meaning | Decision |
|--|--|--|
| `01` | Refer to Card Issuer | Soft — customer should call bank; retry after customer action |
| `04` | Pick Up Card | Hard for card-present; soft for card-not-present (CNP) — retry with 3DS |

**Q5. What are network-specific decline codes beyond ISO 8583, and how do Stripe and Braintree abstract them?**
A: Card networks (Visa, Mastercard) and processors layer proprietary codes on top of ISO 8583. Processors like Stripe translate these into developer-friendly codes:

**Stripe decline codes (mapped from issuer codes):**
- `card_declined` (generic) → inspect `decline_code` sub-field for specifics
- `insufficient_funds` → retryable; retry in 3–7 days
- `do_not_honor` → non-retryable; request new card
- `fraudulent` → non-retryable; risk team review required
- `expired_card` → non-retryable; prompt card update
- `incorrect_cvc` → retryable once with corrected CVC; non-retryable on second failure
- `card_velocity_exceeded` → soft; retry after 24h
- `lost_card` / `stolen_card` → non-retryable; potential fraud alert

**Key architectural lesson:** never hardcode processor-specific strings in business logic. Build a **normalization layer** that maps `stripe.decline_code`, `braintree.processor_response_code`, and `adyen.refusalReason` to your internal enum (`INSUFFICIENT_FUNDS`, `HARD_DECLINE`, `NETWORK_ERROR`, etc.). This makes processor migration and multi-processor routing feasible.

## Section 3: Retry Architecture

**Q6. What does a production-grade retry system for payment failures look like architecturally?**
A: A mature retry system has several components:

```
Payment Attempt
      │
      ▼
┌─────────────────────┐
│  Response Classifier │  ← Maps raw code → RetryDecision enum
└─────────┬───────────┘
          │
    ┌─────┴──────┐
    │             │
 RETRYABLE    NON-RETRYABLE
    │             │
    ▼             ▼
┌────────┐   ┌──────────────────┐
│ Retry  │   │ Customer Notif.  │
│ Queue  │   │ + Payment Method │
│(Delayed│   │ Update Flow      │
│ Jobs)  │   └──────────────────┘
└───┬────┘
    │
    ▼
┌─────────────────────┐
│  Back-off Scheduler  │  ← Exponential back-off with jitter
│  + Attempt Counter   │  ← Max attempts enforcement
│  + Idempotency Key   │  ← Prevents duplicate charges
└─────────┬───────────┘
          │
    Max attempts?
    ┌─────┴──────┐
   YES           NO
    │             │
    ▼             ▼
Customer      Re-attempt
Escalation    Authorization
```

Key components:
- **Response Classifier** — pure function, exhaustively tested with unit tests against every known decline code.
- **Retry Queue** — a durable message queue (SQS, Pub/Sub, Kafka) with delayed delivery. Never retry synchronously in the HTTP request cycle.
- **Idempotency Keys** — every retry attempt uses the same idempotency key as the original, preventing double-charges if the network timed out but the processor succeeded.
- **Attempt Ledger** — a DB table tracking attempt number, timestamp, response code, and outcome per payment. Required for debugging and regulatory audit trails.

**Q7. What back-off strategy should payment retries use, and why?**
A: Payment retries should use **exponential back-off with full jitter**, not linear or fixed intervals.

**Why exponential back-off:**
- Soft declines like `insufficient_funds` are unlikely to resolve in minutes. Retrying every 5 minutes for an hour wastes processor API calls and can trigger issuer velocity blocks.
- Issuer systems that are temporarily down (code `91`) need time to recover — a flood of retries during recovery extends the outage.

**Why jitter:**
- Without jitter, all payments that failed during a processor outage retry at exactly the same time when the back-off period expires — recreating the thundering herd that caused the original failure.
- Full jitter: `sleep(random_between(0, min(cap, base * 2^attempt)))`. Spreads retry load across the back-off window.

**Practical intervals by failure type:**

| Failure Type | Retry 1 | Retry 2 | Retry 3 | Max Attempts |
|--|--|--|--|--|
| Network timeout | 30s | 2min | 10min | 3 |
| Processor rate limit (429) | 1min | 5min | 15min | 3 |
| Insufficient funds | 3 days | 5 days | 7 days | 3–4 |
| Generic soft decline | 1 day | 3 days | 7 days | 3 |
| Issuer system down (91) | 30min | 2h | 6h | 4 |

**Q8. What is an idempotency key, and why is it non-negotiable in payment retry logic?**
A: An idempotency key is a unique, stable identifier attached to a payment request. If the same key is submitted twice, the processor returns the result of the first request rather than executing a second charge.

**Why it's critical for retries:**
Consider this failure mode: your system sends an authorization request, the network times out after 30 seconds, and your code classifies this as a `NETWORK_ERROR` (retryable). But the processor *received* the request and *succeeded* — it's the response that was lost. Without an idempotency key, retrying creates a duplicate charge. With one, the processor recognizes the duplicate and returns the original success response.

**Implementation:**
- Generate the idempotency key when the payment intent is created: `UUID v4` or `sha256(order_id + attempt_number + timestamp_floor_to_minute)`.
- Store it in your `payment_attempts` table.
- Pass it on every retry attempt for the *same* logical payment.
- Increment the key (or use `attempt_number` as a suffix) only when you *intentionally* want a new charge (e.g., after the customer updates their card).

**Stripe:** `Idempotency-Key` HTTP header.
**Braintree:** `transaction_id` with `submitForSettlement` idempotency.
**Adyen:** `reference` field (must be unique per merchant).

## Section 4: Subscription & Recurring Payment Retries

**Q9. How does retry logic differ for subscription billing versus one-time payments?**
A: Subscription billing has a fundamentally different retry calculus because the customer relationship is ongoing and the revenue is recurring:

**One-time payments:**
- Retry window is short (hours to 1–2 days).
- After exhaustion, order is cancelled; customer must re-initiate.
- Customer friction is acceptable — they can choose to retry.

**Subscription billing (Dunning):**
- Retry window is long (days to weeks within the billing cycle).
- After exhaustion, subscription is suspended or cancelled — high churn cost.
- Goal is to recover the payment *without* interrupting service.
- Sophisticated dunning sequences consider: day of month (retry near payday), time of day (retry when customers are likely awake), and previous successful payment day.

**Dunning logic design:**
```
Day 0:  Initial charge fails (soft decline)
Day 1:  Retry 1 — email customer about failed payment
Day 3:  Retry 2 — SMS reminder
Day 7:  Retry 3 — in-app notification, grace period begins
Day 14: Retry 4 — final warning, service degradation
Day 21: Subscription suspended — customer must update payment method
Day 30: Subscription cancelled — churn event
```

Tools: **Stripe Billing** (Smart Retries using ML), **Recurly** (Revenue Recovery), **Chargebee** (Dunning workflows). At scale, you build this yourself with a job scheduler (Sidekiq, Temporal, Celery) and a dunning state machine.

**Q10. What are Visa and Mastercard retry rules, and why do they matter for compliance?**
A: Card networks publish explicit rules on how many times a declined transaction may be retried. Violating these rules results in fines and potentially losing your ability to process cards.

**Visa Retry Rules (effective 2021+):**
- **Non-retryable declines** (codes `04`, `07`, `14`, `41`, `43`, `54`, `57`, `62`, `63`): **zero retries** permitted.
- **Retryable declines**: maximum **15 retries over 30 days** per card + merchant combination.
- After 15 retries, the transaction is blocked at the network level for 30 days.

**Mastercard Retry Rules:**
- Similar structure; maximum **10 retries** for recurring transactions.
- Specific codes flagged as "do not retry" (DNR): retrying a DNR code is a network violation.

**Practical implications:**
- Your retry system must track attempts at the `(card_fingerprint, merchant_id)` level, not just per order.
- A customer who fails 15 times across multiple orders is network-blocked, even if each individual order only retried 3 times.
- Store `card_fingerprint` (not PAN — PCI compliance) and enforce a global counter per card.
- Processors like Stripe enforce this automatically; if you're integrating directly with card networks or using less sophisticated processors, you own this logic.

## Section 5: Edge Cases & Failure Modes

**Q11. What is a partial authorization, and how do you handle it?**
A: A partial authorization occurs when the issuing bank approves a charge for *less than the requested amount* — common with prepaid cards and debit accounts with insufficient funds for the full amount. Example: customer has $47 on a prepaid card; you charge $60; the issuer approves $47.

**Response codes:** Visa uses `10` (Partial Approval); the approved amount is returned in the response.

**Handling strategies:**
- **Accept partial + collect remainder** — split the order into a partial payment and request the remainder via a secondary payment method. Complex UX; common in fuel stations (prepay scenarios).
- **Reverse and decline** — void the partial authorization and treat it as a full decline. Prompt the customer for a new card or to top up their balance.
- **Accept partial as full** — only viable if your business model allows it (e.g., "pay what you can" donations).

Most e-commerce systems choose strategy 2 (reverse and decline) because the UX of split payments is complex and error-prone. Your retry logic must handle partial authorization as a *special case* — it is neither a success nor a standard soft decline.

**Q12. What is authorization reversal vs. refund, and when does retry logic need to trigger a reversal?**
A: These are distinct operations with different mechanics:

- **Authorization reversal (void)** — cancels an authorization *before* capture. No money has moved; the hold on the customer's funds is released immediately. Zero-fee; preferred when you decide not to capture.
- **Refund** — reverses a *captured* or *settled* transaction. Money has moved; refund initiates the reverse flow. Takes 3–5 business days; may incur interchange fees.

**Retry logic implication:**
When your retry system decides to re-attempt with a different payment method (e.g., after a hard decline), it must first **reverse any existing authorization** on the failed card before charging the new one — otherwise the customer has funds held on two cards simultaneously. This is especially critical for high-value transactions (hotel holds, car rentals, large orders).

```
1. Authorization attempt on Card A → Hard Decline
2. System decides to retry with Card B (customer's backup card)
3. ← MUST reverse Card A authorization first (if partial auth was issued)
4. Authorization attempt on Card B → Success
5. Capture Card B
```

**Q13. What is the "do not honor" (code 05) trap, and how do you avoid it?**
A: `05 — Do Not Honor` is the issuer's generic refusal when it doesn't want to provide a specific reason (often to avoid tipping off fraudsters). It accounts for a significant percentage of all declines. The trap:

- `05` can be issued for both retryable conditions (temporary fraud scoring, daily limit reached) and permanent blocks.
- Naively classifying `05` as retryable causes your system to retry aggressively.
- Retrying `05` multiple times in quick succession signals suspicious behavior to the issuer's fraud system, resulting in the card being flagged — making future legitimate charges from this customer harder.

**Best practice:**
- Classify `05` as retryable **but with conservative back-off**: retry once after 24h, once after 72h. If it fails twice, escalate to the customer.
- Track `05` frequency per card — if a card generates `05` on multiple consecutive attempts, reclassify as effectively non-retryable and prompt for new payment method.
- Never retry `05` more than 2–3 times total (Visa network limits apply).

## Section 6: System Design Scenarios

**Q14. "Design a retry system for a SaaS billing platform that processes 1M subscriptions monthly."**
A: Key design decisions at scale:

**Storage:**
```sql
payment_attempts (
  id              UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  payment_id      UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES payments(id),
  card_fingerprint VARCHAR(64) NOT NULL,   -- for network retry tracking
  idempotency_key VARCHAR(128) NOT NULL UNIQUE,
  attempt_number  SMALLINT NOT NULL,
  processor       VARCHAR(32),             -- stripe, adyen, braintree
  raw_response    JSONB,
  response_code   VARCHAR(8),
  classified_as   VARCHAR(32),             -- HARD_DECLINE, SOFT_DECLINE, NETWORK_ERROR
  retryable       BOOLEAN NOT NULL,
  next_retry_at   TIMESTAMPTZ,
  created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ DEFAULT now()
)

-- Index for retry scheduler:
CREATE INDEX ON payment_attempts (next_retry_at) WHERE retryable = TRUE AND classified_as != 'SUCCESS';

-- Index for network compliance tracking:
CREATE INDEX ON payment_attempts (card_fingerprint, created_at);
```

**Retry scheduler (worker):**
- A cron job (every 5 minutes) or a queue consumer polls `payment_attempts WHERE next_retry_at <= now() AND retryable = TRUE`.
- Enforces max attempt count and network retry limits before dispatching.
- Uses a distributed lock (`SELECT FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED` in Postgres or Redis `SETNX`) to prevent duplicate processing in a multi-worker setup.

**Scale considerations:**
- At 1M subscriptions with ~10% monthly failure rate = 100K retries/month = ~3,300/day = ~2.3/min — comfortably handled by a single worker; no exotic infrastructure needed.
- At 10M subscriptions, shard the retry queue by `payment_id % N_workers`.
- Observability: emit a metric for every retry attempt with labels `(processor, response_code, attempt_number, outcome)`. Alert on retry success rate dropping below baseline — signals a processor or network degradation.

**Q15. "A payment is stuck in 'pending' and we don't know if the authorization succeeded. How do you handle this?"**
A: This is a **payment status unknown (PSU)** scenario — one of the most dangerous in payment systems. Causes: network timeout between your system and the processor, processor system failure after receiving but before confirming, your system crash mid-request.

**Never guess. Always query:**

```
Step 1: Query the processor's transaction status API with the original idempotency key.
  → If SUCCESS: mark as authorized in your DB. Do not re-authorize.
  → If FAILED: mark as failed. Apply retry logic as normal.
  → If NOT FOUND: the processor never received it. Safe to retry with new idempotency key.
  → If PENDING (processor also unsure): wait and poll with back-off.
```

**Architectural safeguard:**
- All payment requests should be written to your DB *before* the processor call, with status `PENDING`.
- A background reconciliation job polls all long-running `PENDING` records against the processor's reporting API.
- Set a maximum PSU window (e.g., 30 minutes). After that, assume failure and initiate retry — the idempotency key protects against double-charge if the original eventually surfaces.

**This is why idempotency keys are non-negotiable** — without them, querying "did this succeed?" is unanswerable and retrying is dangerous.

**Q16. "How would you build a multi-processor retry system that falls back to a secondary processor on failure?"**
A: Multi-processor routing (cascading) is a common pattern for improving authorization rates:

```
Attempt 1: Primary processor (Stripe)
  → Hard decline → stop, notify customer
  → Network error / Processor down → retry on Secondary processor (Adyen)
  → Soft decline → retry on Primary after back-off, OR cascade to Secondary

Attempt 2 (cascade): Secondary processor (Adyen)
  → Success → capture on Adyen
  → Decline → retry logic continues on Adyen
```

**Design considerations:**
- **Same idempotency key per processor** — the key is scoped to (order_id, processor_id), not globally. A charge on Stripe and a charge on Adyen with the same order are different transactions.
- **Cascade only on processor-side failures** — never cascade on issuer hard declines. The issuer sees the card fingerprint, not the processor — the decline will repeat on any processor.
- **Track attempts per processor** in your `payment_attempts` table.
- **Avoid double authorization** — if Stripe returned a timeout (PSU), query before cascading to Adyen.

**When cascading is appropriate:**
- Processor outage or degraded authorization rate (< 95% baseline).
- Processor doesn't support a payment method the customer used.
- Geographic routing: certain processors have higher auth rates in specific regions (Adyen in Europe, Stripe in North America).

**Q17. "Walk me through the retry logic for an ACH payment failure."**
A: ACH (Automated Clearing House) failures are fundamentally different from card declines — they occur *asynchronously*, days after the payment is initiated, via return codes.

**ACH timeline:**
```
Day 0:  ACH debit initiated (funds not yet moved)
Day 1:  Transaction enters clearing
Day 2:  Funds provisionally available to merchant
Day 3–5: Return window — RDFI (customer's bank) can reject
Day 60: Late return window closes (for certain codes)
```

**ACH return codes:**

| Code | Meaning | Retryable? |
|--|--|--|
| `R01` | Insufficient Funds | Yes — retry once after 5 days |
| `R02` | Account Closed | No — remove ACH mandate |
| `R03` | No Account / Unable to Locate | No — invalid account |
| `R04` | Invalid Account Number | No — invalid account |
| `R08` | Payment Stopped (customer stop order) | No — customer revoked authorization |
| `R10` | Customer Advises Not Authorized | No — dispute; do not retry |
| `R29` | Corporate Customer Advises Not Authorized | No — dispute |

**NACHA rules:**
- `R01` (Insufficient Funds): maximum **2 retries** within 180 days of the original authorization date.
- `R09` (Uncollected Funds): same 2-retry limit.
- Retrying beyond these limits is a NACHA violation.

**Key difference from card retries:**
- No synchronous response — your system must process ACH returns as *incoming events*, not polling. The bank sends you a return file; your system parses it and triggers the retry/dunning workflow.
- Build an ACH return processor as a separate consumer from your card decline retry system.

## Section 7: Observability & Compliance

**Q18. What metrics should you instrument on your payment retry system?**
A: Four categories of metrics:

**Volume metrics (counters):**
- `payment.attempts.total` by `(processor, response_code, classified_as)`
- `payment.retries.scheduled` — how many retries are in the queue
- `payment.retries.exhausted` — payments that failed all retries (churn signal)

**Rate metrics (gauges):**
- `payment.authorization_rate` = successful auths / total attempts — primary health metric
- `payment.retry_recovery_rate` = retries that eventually succeed / total retries scheduled
- `payment.hard_decline_rate` by `response_code` — spike signals fraud or card scheme change

**Latency metrics (histograms):**
- Time from initial failure to first retry
- Time from initial failure to final outcome (success or exhaustion)

**Compliance metrics:**
- `payment.network_retry_limit_breaches` — attempts that were blocked/fined for violating Visa/MC retry rules. Should always be zero.
- `payment.idempotency_collisions` — processor returned existing result for idempotency key. Expected on retries; unexpected on initial attempts (signals duplicate request bug).

**Alerting thresholds (examples):**
- Auth rate drops > 5% below 7-day rolling average → PagerDuty.
- Hard decline rate for a specific response code spikes 3× → investigate for card scheme change or fraud pattern.
- Retry queue depth grows unbounded → retry worker down or back-off too aggressive.

**Q19. What audit trail is required for payment retry logic in a regulated fintech environment?**
A: In regulated environments (PCI DSS, SOC 2, FCA, OCC), your retry system must produce a complete, immutable audit trail:

**Required records per attempt:**
- Timestamp of attempt (nanosecond precision)
- Idempotency key used
- Processor and processor's transaction ID
- Raw request (sanitized — no PAN, CVV; only card fingerprint)
- Raw response (full response body or structured fields)
- Internal classification decision (`HARD_DECLINE`, `SOFT_DECLINE`, etc.) and the code that produced it (for auditability of the classifier)
- Retry decision: retried / escalated / abandoned, and the rule that triggered it
- Actor: automated system (with version) or human override

**Immutability:**
- Append-only table — no `UPDATE` or `DELETE` on `payment_attempts`.
- Use a separate `payment_attempt_events` table for state transitions rather than mutating rows.
- For SOC 2: ensure logs are shipped to an immutable log store (CloudTrail, Splunk) that the application cannot modify.

**Retention:**
- PCI DSS: transaction logs retained minimum 1 year online, 3 years archived.
- FCA (UK): payment records 5 years minimum.
- Your legal team determines jurisdiction-specific requirements; your system must support configurable retention policies.
