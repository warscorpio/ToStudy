# Payment Lifecycle: Authorization → Capture → Settlement → Reconciliation — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is the payment lifecycle, and what are its four core phases?**
A: The payment lifecycle is the end-to-end flow of funds from a customer's payment instrument to a merchant's bank account. It is composed of four sequential phases:
- **Authorization** — the issuing bank verifies the cardholder's identity, checks available funds/credit, and reserves (but does not transfer) the amount. A successful auth produces an authorization code and reduces the cardholder's available balance.
- **Capture** — the merchant instructs the acquirer to actually collect the authorized amount. This is when the obligation to pay becomes firm; some merchants capture at auth time (single-message), others capture later (dual-message, e.g., after shipment).
- **Settlement** — the acquirer batches captured transactions and sends them to the card network (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay), which routes net amounts between issuing and acquiring banks via interbank clearing. Funds move from the issuer to the acquirer's account.
- **Reconciliation** — the merchant matches its internal order/transaction records against the settlement file received from the acquirer or payment gateway, identifies discrepancies (short settlements, failed refunds, chargeback deductions), and updates its books accordingly.

Each phase involves different actors, timing windows, and failure modes. A 5 YOE engineer must understand where funds actually sit at each phase and who bears the liability when something goes wrong.

**Q2. Who are the actors in a card payment ecosystem?**
A: The five primary actors:
- **Cardholder** — the customer who initiates the payment using a debit/credit card or UPI-linked instrument.
- **Merchant** — the business accepting the payment; operates a POS terminal or payment page integrated with a payment gateway.
- **Payment Gateway (PG)** — routes the transaction from the merchant to the acquirer; handles encryption, tokenization, and sometimes fraud scoring. Examples: Razorpay, PayU, Cashfree (India); Stripe, Braintree (global).
- **Acquiring Bank (Acquirer)** — the merchant's bank; sponsors the merchant into the card network and fronts settlement funds to the merchant (often before funds clear from the issuer).
- **Issuing Bank (Issuer)** — the cardholder's bank; owns the credit/debit account, performs auth decisions, and ultimately transfers funds during settlement.
- **Card Network (Scheme)** — the rail between acquirer and issuer: Visa, Mastercard, RuPay, Amex. Defines the protocol, sets interchange rates, and operates the clearing and settlement infrastructure.

In India, the RBI mandates that all payment aggregators (PAs) holding merchant funds be separately licensed under the Payment Aggregator guidelines (2020, updated 2023), adding a regulatory actor above the PG layer.

**Q3. What is the difference between authorization and capture, and why does separating them matter?**
A: Authorization is a *reservation* of funds — the issuer promises to pay if the merchant captures within the auth validity window (typically 7 days for debit, 30 days for credit, though this varies by network and issuer). No money moves during auth.

Capture is the *claim* on that reservation — the merchant formally requests collection of the authorized amount (or a partial amount). Money begins moving toward settlement only after capture.

Separating them matters for several real-world scenarios:
- **E-commerce with delayed fulfillment** — auth at checkout, capture only when the item ships. If it goes out of stock, the merchant releases the auth rather than issuing a refund.
- **Hotels and car rentals** — auth an estimated amount at check-in; capture the actual amount (potentially higher, for damages) at checkout.
- **Fraud prevention** — a high-risk order can be authorized, then held for manual review before capture. If fraud is confirmed, the auth is voided with zero financial impact.
- **Partial capture** — a merchant can capture less than the authorized amount (common in split shipments). Partial capture releases the remainder back to the cardholder's available balance.

In payment gateway APIs (Razorpay, Stripe), this manifests as two separate API calls: `POST /payments` (creates auth) and `POST /payments/:id/capture`.

**Q4. What happens during settlement, and what is the role of interchange?**
A: Settlement is the interbank fund transfer that completes the payment. The flow:

1. Merchant's acquirer batches all captured transactions from the day (or settlement cycle) and sends a clearing file to the card network.
2. The card network runs multilateral net settlement — it calculates each bank's net position (total inflows minus total outflows across all transactions) and sends a single net figure to each participant bank.
3. Banks settle their net positions via central bank settlement infrastructure — in India, via RBI's RTGS or NEFT; globally via central bank accounts at the Fed or ECB.
4. The acquirer credits the merchant's account, typically T+1 or T+2 days after capture, minus the merchant discount rate (MDR).

**Interchange** is the fee the acquirer pays to the issuer for each transaction — the largest component of the MDR. It compensates the issuer for credit risk, fraud losses, and the cost of extending credit. Interchange rates are set by the card networks and vary by:
- Card type (credit > debit, rewards card > basic card)
- Merchant category code (MCC) — groceries and utilities have lower interchange than luxury goods
- Transaction mode (card-present < card-not-present, due to higher CNP fraud risk)
- Geography — India's RuPay MDR on debit transactions is regulated/zero for certain categories by RBI mandate.

**Q5. What is reconciliation, and what are the main categories of discrepancies it catches?**
A: Reconciliation is the process of matching the merchant's internal transaction records (from its order management system or database) against the external settlement report provided by the acquirer or payment gateway. It answers: *did we receive the money we expected for every transaction we recorded?*

Main discrepancy categories:
- **Settlement shortfall** — the PG settled less than the captured amount. Usually explained by MDR deduction, refund netting, or chargeback deduction — but must be verified line-by-line.
- **Missing transactions** — a transaction appears in the merchant's DB as captured but is absent from the settlement file. Could be a capture that failed silently, a network timeout where the PG received the request but the merchant's callback didn't arrive, or a processing cutoff timing issue.
- **Duplicate credits** — rare, but a PG bug or retry logic error can settle the same transaction twice. Without reconciliation, the merchant receives extra funds without knowing.
- **Refund discrepancies** — a refund initiated by the merchant may be reversed by the PG (insufficient funds in the merchant's PG account), but the merchant's DB shows it as successful. The customer gets no money back; the merchant doesn't know.
- **Chargeback deductions** — the issuer reverses a transaction on behalf of the cardholder (disputed charge). The PG deducts the chargebacked amount from the next settlement. Without reconciliation, the merchant's books show the original settlement but not the deduction.
- **FX conversion mismatches** — for multi-currency merchants, the settled-in-local-currency amount may differ from the expected amount due to exchange rate fluctuation between auth and settlement.

## Section 2: Authorization In Depth

**Q6. What is the technical flow of a card authorization request?**
A: A card-not-present (CNP) authorization in an e-commerce context:

```
Customer Browser
    │  POST /checkout (card data or token)
    ▼
Payment Gateway (Razorpay / Stripe)
    │  Tokenizes PAN → network token (if applicable)
    │  Fraud score (velocity checks, BIN analysis)
    │  ISO 8583 / API auth request
    ▼
Acquirer Processing Host
    │  Adds acquirer routing info
    │  Forwards over card network rails (VisaNet / Banknet / RuPayNet)
    ▼
Card Network Switch
    │  Routes to issuer based on BIN (first 6–8 digits of PAN)
    ▼
Issuing Bank Authorization System
    │  Checks: available balance/credit limit
    │  Checks: card status (active, blocked, expired)
    │  Checks: 3DS / ACS for CNP (OTP in India — RBI mandate)
    │  Applies velocity rules and fraud models
    │  Returns: Approve (+ auth code) or Decline (+ response code)
    ▼
Response propagates back through network → acquirer → PG → merchant
    │  PG stores auth_id, auth_code, amount, expiry
    ▼
Merchant DB: payment record { status: 'authorized', auth_code: 'X9Y2Z1' }
```

Round-trip latency target: < 3 seconds end-to-end (RBI and network SLA). The issuer's authorization decision is synchronous; the PG returns the result to the merchant within this window.

**Q7. What are common authorization decline codes, and how should a system handle them?**
A: ISO 8583 defines dozens of response codes. The most important categories for a backend engineer:

| Response Code | Meaning | Merchant Action |
|---|---|---|
| `00` | Approved | Proceed to capture |
| `51` | Insufficient funds | Prompt customer to use different card |
| `54` | Expired card | Prompt card update |
| `05` | Do not honor | Generic decline — don't retry; show generic error |
| `14` | Invalid card number | Card data entry error — prompt re-entry |
| `57` | Transaction not permitted | Card blocked for this MCC or transaction type |
| `91` | Issuer unavailable | Soft decline — safe to retry after delay |
| `96` | System malfunction | Soft decline — retry with exponential backoff |
| `65` | Exceeds withdrawal limit | Hard decline for this card; suggest alternative |

Hard declines (`05`, `14`, `54`, `57`) must not be retried — the issuer has made a firm decision. Retrying hard declines triggers "excessive retry" penalties from card networks (Visa's Visa Retry Program, Mastercard's Transaction Processing Excellence). Soft declines (`91`, `96`) are safe to retry.

In a NestJS fintech backend:

```typescript
// payments/auth/decline-handler.ts
const HARD_DECLINE_CODES = new Set(['05', '14', '54', '57', '41', '43', '62']);
const SOFT_DECLINE_CODES = new Set(['91', '96', 'N7']);

export function classifyDecline(responseCode: string): 'hard' | 'soft' | 'unknown' {
  if (HARD_DECLINE_CODES.has(responseCode)) return 'hard';
  if (SOFT_DECLINE_CODES.has(responseCode)) return 'soft';
  return 'unknown'; // treat unknown as hard — safer than retrying
}

export async function handleDecline(
  paymentId: string,
  responseCode: string,
  retryCount: number,
): Promise<void> {
  const type = classifyDecline(responseCode);
  if (type === 'hard' || retryCount >= MAX_SOFT_RETRIES) {
    await paymentsRepo.updateStatus(paymentId, 'failed', { responseCode });
    await notificationService.notifyCustomer(paymentId, 'payment_failed');
    return;
  }
  // Soft decline: schedule retry with backoff
  const delay = Math.pow(2, retryCount) * 1000; // 1s, 2s, 4s
  await retryQueue.enqueue({ paymentId, responseCode }, { delay });
}
```

**Q8. What is 3D Secure (3DS), and why is it mandatory in India?**
A: 3D Secure is an authentication protocol layered on top of the authorization flow. It involves three domains: the merchant (acquirer domain), the card network (interoperability domain), and the issuer (issuer domain). The issuer's Access Control Server (ACS) authenticates the cardholder before authorization proceeds.

In India, the RBI mandates Additional Factor of Authentication (AFA) — colloquially "OTP" — for all card-not-present transactions. This is implemented via 3DS. The flow:

1. PG initiates 3DS via the Directory Server (DS) of the card network.
2. DS queries the issuer's ACS: is this cardholder enrolled?
3. ACS sends OTP to the registered mobile number; cardholder enters it on the bank's ACS page.
4. ACS returns an Authentication Value (AV / CAVV) to the PG.
5. PG includes CAVV in the authorization request.
6. Issuer validates CAVV and approves.

**Liability shift**: the critical business consequence of 3DS. If a transaction is authenticated via 3DS and later disputed as fraud, liability shifts from the merchant to the issuer. Without 3DS, the merchant bears chargeback liability for CNP fraud. This makes 3DS not just a compliance requirement but a financial risk management tool.

3DS 2.x (EMVCo standard) allows "frictionless" flows for low-risk transactions — the ACS authenticates using device fingerprinting and behavioral data without showing the OTP screen, reducing checkout abandonment.

**Q9. What is authorization hold expiry, and what are its engineering implications?**
A: An authorization creates a hold on the cardholder's funds. This hold expires if not captured within the network's validity window:
- Visa: 7 days (debit), 30 days (credit) — configurable per MCC
- Mastercard: 30 days (most card types)
- RuPay: 5–7 days (varies by issuer)

Engineering implications:
- A payment service must track `auth_expiry_at` and proactively handle expiries. A common bug is attempting to capture an expired auth — the capture returns a specific decline code (`4802` on Visa), and the merchant has lost the sale; the customer's card is no longer on session to re-auth.
- For long-fulfillment-cycle businesses (custom manufacturing, pre-orders), use incremental authorization (supported on Visa/MC for certain MCCs) to extend or increase the hold.
- Scheduled jobs must scan for authorizations approaching expiry (e.g., 24h before) and either trigger capture or send an alert.

```typescript
// Cron: runs every hour
@Cron('0 * * * *')
async handleExpiringAuthorizations(): Promise<void> {
  const expiryThreshold = addHours(new Date(), 24);
  const expiringAuths = await this.paymentsRepo.findByStatusAndExpiryBefore(
    'authorized',
    expiryThreshold,
  );
  for (const payment of expiringAuths) {
    await this.alertingService.sendAuthExpiryAlert(payment);
    // If business rule: auto-void rather than let it expire
    if (payment.fulfillmentStatus === 'cancelled') {
      await this.gatewayService.voidAuthorization(payment.gatewayPaymentId);
      await this.paymentsRepo.updateStatus(payment.id, 'voided');
    }
  }
}
```

## Section 3: Capture In Depth

**Q10. What is the difference between single-message and dual-message authorization?**
A:
- **Single-message (sale)** — authorization and capture are sent as a single request. The transaction is immediately queued for settlement. Used in point-of-sale (card-present) environments where the full amount is known at the moment of the transaction. Faster, simpler, but no window to adjust the amount.
- **Dual-message** — authorization and capture are separate requests. Authorization happens at checkout; capture happens later (on shipment, at end of day, or after fulfillment confirmation). Used in e-commerce, hotels, car rentals, and any context where the final amount may differ or fulfillment may not occur.

The choice affects database schema design:

```typescript
// Dual-message schema — two distinct lifecycle events
interface Payment {
  id: string;
  status: 'pending' | 'authorized' | 'captured' | 'settled' | 'failed' | 'voided' | 'refunded';
  authorizedAmount: number;    // amount reserved at auth
  capturedAmount: number;      // amount actually captured (may differ — partial capture)
  authCode: string;
  authExpiry: Date;
  capturedAt: Date | null;
  settlementId: string | null; // FK to settlement batch
  gatewayPaymentId: string;    // PG's reference
}
```

**Q11. How does partial capture work, and when is it used?**
A: Partial capture allows the merchant to capture less than the authorized amount. The unreserved remainder is released back to the cardholder's available balance. Networks support this natively.

Use cases:
- **Partial shipment** — a 3-item order, 2 items ship today. Merchant captures for 2/3 of the order value; the rest is released (and the customer is re-charged when the third item ships, requiring a new auth).
- **Discount applied post-auth** — a promo code applied after auth. Capture the discounted amount.
- **Hotel incidentals** — auth includes an incidentals hold; capture only the room rate if no incidentals were charged.

Overcapture (capturing more than authorized) is not permitted by card network rules and will be declined by the acquirer.

```typescript
// payments/capture/capture.service.ts
async capturePayment(
  paymentId: string,
  captureAmount: number,
): Promise<CaptureResult> {
  const payment = await this.paymentsRepo.findById(paymentId);

  if (payment.status !== 'authorized') {
    throw new PaymentStateError(`Cannot capture payment in status: ${payment.status}`);
  }
  if (captureAmount > payment.authorizedAmount) {
    throw new OvercaptureError(
      `Capture amount ${captureAmount} exceeds authorized amount ${payment.authorizedAmount}`,
    );
  }
  if (new Date() > payment.authExpiry) {
    throw new AuthExpiredError(`Authorization expired at ${payment.authExpiry}`);
  }

  const result = await this.gatewayService.capture(payment.gatewayPaymentId, captureAmount);

  await this.paymentsRepo.update(paymentId, {
    status: 'captured',
    capturedAmount: captureAmount,
    capturedAt: new Date(),
    captureReference: result.captureId,
  });

  return result;
}
```

**Q12. What is void (authorization reversal), and how does it differ from a refund?**
A:
- **Void (reversal)** — cancels an authorization before capture. The hold on the cardholder's funds is released immediately (or within hours, depending on issuer processing). No money ever moved, so no money moves back. Zero cost to the merchant (no interchange, no MDR).
- **Refund** — reverses a *captured* or *settled* transaction. Money has moved; a refund initiates a new credit flow back to the cardholder. Takes 3–7 business days to appear on the cardholder's statement. Merchant pays MDR on the original transaction and may pay a refund processing fee.

Engineering implication: always check if a transaction is pre-capture before issuing a refund. A void is always cheaper and faster than a refund. Build a `cancelPayment` service method that branches:

```typescript
async cancelPayment(paymentId: string): Promise<void> {
  const payment = await this.paymentsRepo.findById(paymentId);

  if (payment.status === 'authorized') {
    // Pre-capture: use void — free and immediate
    await this.gatewayService.voidAuthorization(payment.gatewayPaymentId);
    await this.paymentsRepo.updateStatus(paymentId, 'voided');
  } else if (payment.status === 'captured' || payment.status === 'settled') {
    // Post-capture: must refund
    await this.refundService.initiateRefund(paymentId, payment.capturedAmount);
  } else {
    throw new InvalidStateError(`Cannot cancel payment in status: ${payment.status}`);
  }
}
```

## Section 4: Settlement In Depth

**Q13. What is the settlement cycle, and how does it vary by payment rail in India?**
A: Settlement cycle is the time from transaction capture to funds credited to the merchant's bank account. It varies by rail:

| Payment Rail | Settlement Cycle | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Credit Card (Visa/MC) | T+2 | T = capture date; 2 business days |
| Debit Card (Visa/MC/RuPay) | T+1 | Faster due to lower credit risk |
| UPI | T+0 to T+1 | NPCI settles intraday; PG may hold until T+1 |
| Net Banking | T+1 to T+2 | Varies by bank |
| BNPL / EMI | T+3 to T+7 | Lending partner involvement adds cycle |
| International Cards | T+3 to T+5 | Cross-border correspondent banking adds steps |

In India, the RBI's Payment Aggregator guidelines require that payment aggregators not hold merchant funds beyond T+1 for most instruments. Violation triggers regulatory scrutiny.

**Q14. What is the settlement file, and what fields does a merchant's reconciliation system need from it?**
A: The settlement file (also called the settlement report or disbursement report) is a structured file (CSV, Excel, or API response) provided by the PG/acquirer that lists every transaction included in a settlement batch. Critical fields:

```typescript
interface SettlementRecord {
  pgTransactionId: string;          // PG's reference — join key to merchant's DB
  merchantOrderId: string;          // merchant's reference (if echoed by PG)
  transactionDate: Date;            // when the transaction was captured
  settlementDate: Date;             // when funds were disbursed
  grossAmount: number;              // captured amount
  mdrAmount: number;                // MDR/fee deducted by PG
  gstOnMdr: number;                 // GST on MDR (18% in India — TDS implications)
  netAmount: number;                // grossAmount - mdrAmount - gstOnMdr
  refundAmount: number;             // if a refund was netted in this settlement
  chargebackAmount: number;         // if a chargeback was deducted
  paymentMethod: string;            // card, UPI, netbanking, wallet
  utr: string;                      // Unique Transaction Reference — bank's reference for the credit
  settlementBatchId: string;        // groups multiple txns settled together
  currency: string;
}
```

The `utr` field is critical for the final reconciliation step: the merchant's bank statement shows a credit with a UTR; the settlement file maps UTR → individual transactions. Without UTR-level mapping, a merchant cannot reconcile at transaction granularity.

**Q15. What is nostro/vostro account management in the context of cross-border settlements?**
A: For international payments, fund movement involves correspondent banking relationships:
- **Nostro account** ("our account held by you") — the acquirer's bank maintains an account at a foreign correspondent bank to hold foreign-currency funds. When an Indian merchant is paid in USD, the USD sits in the acquirer's nostro USD account at a US correspondent bank.
- **Vostro account** ("your account held by us") — the mirror view; the US correspondent bank holds the acquirer's foreign funds.

The settlement flow for a cross-border transaction:
1. Foreign customer pays USD on Indian merchant's checkout.
2. Foreign issuer authorizes and settles USD to the card network's clearing account.
3. Card network credits the acquirer's nostro USD account via SWIFT.
4. Acquirer converts USD → INR at the spot rate (with a conversion markup — typically 1–3%).
5. INR is settled to the merchant's Indian bank account via RTGS.

Engineering implication: cross-border settlement records must capture both the foreign-currency amount and the INR settlement amount, the exchange rate applied, and the conversion date — all required for FEMA reporting and GST input tax credit.

## Section 5: Reconciliation In Depth

**Q16. What is a three-way reconciliation in payments, and why is it necessary?**
A: Three-way reconciliation matches three independent sources of truth:

1. **Merchant's internal transaction database** — every payment event recorded by the application (`order_payments` table: auth, capture, refund, status updates).
2. **Payment gateway settlement report** — the PG's record of what was captured and what was settled in each batch.
3. **Merchant's bank statement** — the actual bank credits, identified by UTR.

Two-way reconciliation (PG report ↔ bank statement) confirms the right money arrived from the PG. But it doesn't catch PG-side errors — a transaction the PG processed but didn't include in the settlement file, or a transaction the merchant captured but the PG silently dropped.

Three-way reconciliation adds the merchant's own DB as the ground truth, catching:
- Transactions in merchant DB but absent from PG report (capture API returned 200 but PG never processed it)
- Transactions in PG report but absent from merchant DB (PG processed a payment the merchant has no record of — possible data loss or a duplicate order)
- Amounts matching between merchant DB and PG report but differing from bank credit (PG deducted additional fees not disclosed in the report)

**Q17. How do you design a reconciliation pipeline for a high-volume fintech platform?**
A: A production-grade reconciliation pipeline for a lending/payment platform processing 100K+ transactions/day:

```typescript
// reconciliation/reconciliation.service.ts
@Injectable()
export class ReconciliationService {
  // Step 1: Ingest settlement file from PG (S3 drop or API pull)
  async ingestSettlementFile(pgId: string, settlementDate: Date): Promise<void> {
    const records = await this.pgAdapter.fetchSettlementReport(pgId, settlementDate);
    await this.settlementRepo.bulkUpsert(records); // idempotent — re-runs are safe
  }

  // Step 2: Match PG records against internal DB
  async runMatchingJob(settlementDate: Date): Promise<ReconciliationReport> {
    const internalTxns = await this.paymentsRepo.findCapturedByDate(settlementDate);
    const pgRecords = await this.settlementRepo.findBySettlementDate(settlementDate);

    const pgMap = new Map(pgRecords.map(r => [r.pgTransactionId, r]));
    const internalMap = new Map(internalTxns.map(t => [t.gatewayPaymentId, t]));

    const matched: MatchedRecord[] = [];
    const onlyInInternal: Payment[] = [];    // capture recorded, not in settlement
    const onlyInPg: SettlementRecord[] = []; // in settlement, not in our DB
    const amountMismatches: Mismatch[] = [];

    for (const [id, internal] of internalMap) {
      const pg = pgMap.get(id);
      if (!pg) {
        onlyInInternal.push(internal);
      } else if (pg.netAmount !== internal.capturedAmount - internal.expectedMdr) {
        amountMismatches.push({ internal, pg, delta: pg.netAmount - internal.capturedAmount });
      } else {
        matched.push({ internal, pg });
      }
    }

    for (const [id, pg] of pgMap) {
      if (!internalMap.has(id)) onlyInPg.push(pg);
    }

    // Step 3: Persist recon results
    await this.reconRepo.saveReport({
      settlementDate,
      matched: matched.length,
      exceptions: [...onlyInInternal, ...onlyInPg, ...amountMismatches],
    });

    // Step 4: Alert on exceptions
    if (onlyInInternal.length || onlyInPg.length || amountMismatches.length) {
      await this.alertingService.sendReconException({
        missingFromPg: onlyInInternal.length,
        missingFromInternal: onlyInPg.length,
        amountMismatches: amountMismatches.length,
      });
    }

    return { matched, onlyInInternal, onlyInPg, amountMismatches };
  }
}
```

Key design principles:
- **Idempotency** — the pipeline must be re-runnable. Settlement files can be re-fetched; `bulkUpsert` with `ON CONFLICT DO UPDATE` prevents duplicates.
- **Async exceptions** — discrepancies are written to an `exceptions` table for manual review; the pipeline never blocks waiting for human resolution.
- **Audit trail** — every reconciliation run is versioned. Re-running a prior day's reconciliation creates a new run record, not an overwrite.

**Q18. What is a chargeback, and how does it propagate through the payment lifecycle?**
A: A chargeback is a forced reversal initiated by the cardholder's issuer on the cardholder's behalf — typically for: unauthorized transaction (fraud), item not received, item not as described, or processing error. The flow:

1. Cardholder disputes a transaction with their bank.
2. Issuer investigates and, if the dispute is valid, initiates a chargeback with the card network.
3. Card network debits the acquirer's settlement account for the disputed amount plus a chargeback fee (typically ₹250–₹2000 or $15–$25).
4. Acquirer deducts this from the merchant's next settlement (or from the merchant's reserve account if settlement is insufficient).
5. Merchant receives chargeback notification and has a representment window (15–45 days) to submit evidence: delivery proof, signed authorization, fraud analysis.
6. If the merchant wins representment, the funds are re-credited. If the chargeback is upheld, the merchant permanently loses the funds plus fees.

Chargeback ratio exceeding 1% of transactions (Visa threshold) triggers "excessive chargeback program" — higher fees, increased scrutiny, and potential merchant account termination. For a fintech/lending platform, chargebacks on EMI disbursements or loan repayments require distinct handling from retail chargebacks, as the disputed amount may be a regulatory-sensitive loan repayment.

## Section 6: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q19. "Design a payment status state machine for a dual-message payment system."**
A: The state machine must be explicit, exhaustive, and enforce valid transitions at the persistence layer — not just in application logic.

```
                    ┌──────────────────────────────────────┐
                    │                                      │
  [initiated] ──auth_success──► [authorized] ──capture──► [captured] ──batch──► [settlement_pending]
      │                              │                         │                        │
  auth_failure                     void                    refund                 settlement_success
      │                              │                         │                        │
      ▼                              ▼                         ▼                        ▼
  [failed]                       [voided]               [refund_pending]           [settled]
                                                              │                        │
                                                    refund_success/fail           chargeback
                                                              │                        │
                                                              ▼                        ▼
                                                         [refunded]            [chargeback_raised]
                                                                                       │
                                                                              representment_won/lost
                                                                                       │
                                                                            ┌──────────┴──────────┐
                                                                            ▼                     ▼
                                                                    [chargeback_won]    [chargeback_lost]
```

Enforce at DB level with a transition table:

```sql
CREATE TABLE payment_status_transitions (
  from_status VARCHAR(30) NOT NULL,

  PRIMARY KEY (from_status, to_status)

INSERT INTO payment_status_transitions VALUES
  ('initiated',           'authorized'),
  ('initiated',           'failed'),
  ('authorized',          'captured'),
  ('authorized',          'voided'),
  ('authorized',          'failed'),

  ('captured',            'refund_pending'),
  ('settlement_pending',  'settled'),
  ('settled',             'refund_pending'),

  ('refund_pending',      'failed'),
```

Before every status update, validate the transition:

```typescript
async function transitionPaymentStatus(
  paymentId: string,
  toStatus: PaymentStatus,
  db: PrismaClient,
): Promise<void> {
  await db.$transaction(async (tx) => {
    const payment = await tx.payment.findUniqueOrThrow({
      where: { id: paymentId },
      select: { status: true },
    });

    const valid = await tx.paymentStatusTransition.findFirst({
      where: { fromStatus: payment.status, toStatus },
    });

    if (!valid) {
      throw new InvalidTransitionError(
        `Invalid transition: ${payment.status} → ${toStatus}`,
      );
    }

    await tx.payment.update({ where: { id: paymentId }, data: { status: toStatus } });
    await tx.paymentAuditLog.create({
      data: { paymentId, fromStatus: payment.status, toStatus, createdAt: new Date() },
    });
  });
}
```

**Q20. "You're building a reconciliation system. A PG's settlement file arrives daily at 9am via S3. How do you architect the pipeline?"**
A: Production architecture for a daily settlement reconciliation pipeline:

```
PG drops file → S3 bucket
    │
    ▼ (S3 event notification)
SQS queue (settlement-file-events)
    │
    ▼
Lambda / NestJS consumer (ReconciliationWorker)
    │  1. Download file from S3
    │  2. Parse CSV/Excel → SettlementRecord[]
    │  3. Validate schema (required fields, date range sanity)
    │  4. Bulk upsert into settlement_records table (Postgres)
    │  5. Trigger matching job
    ▼
ReconciliationMatchingJob (runs in the same process or as a separate job)
    │  1. Load settlement_records for the date
    │  2. Load captured payments for the date from payments table
    │  3. Run three-way match (settlement + payments + bank statement when available)
    │  4. Write exceptions to recon_exceptions table
    │  5. Publish summary metrics to CloudWatch
    │  6. If critical exceptions (>X missing txns, >₹Y amount mismatch): PagerDuty alert
    ▼
recon_exceptions table
    │
    ▼
Internal ops dashboard (shows exception details, allows manual resolution tagging)
```

Key resilience decisions:
- **S3 + SQS decoupling** — if the worker is down when the file arrives, SQS retains it. Message visibility timeout set to 15 minutes (longer than max job runtime).
- **Idempotency** — each file has a unique `batch_id` from the PG. Processing is gated on `WHERE batch_id NOT IN (SELECT batch_id FROM processed_batches)`.
- **Late file handling** — if the PG file doesn't arrive by 10am, a CloudWatch alarm fires. Recon should not silently skip a day.
- **Bank statement reconciliation** — bank statement is pulled via bank's SFTP or corporate banking API (e.g., ICICI CIB API, HDFC NetConnect). UTR from bank statement is joined against settlement_records.utr for the final confirmation that funds actually hit the account.

**Q21. "A merchant reports that a payment shows as 'success' in their app but the customer's card was never charged. How do you investigate?"**
A: This is one of the most common and insidious payment bugs — a status divergence between the merchant's DB and the actual card network state. Investigation steps:

**Step 1 — Identify the payment record**
```sql
SELECT id, status, gateway_payment_id, auth_code, captured_at, gateway_response
FROM payments
WHERE merchant_order_id = '<order_id>';
```

**Step 2 — Check gateway logs**
Query the PG's dashboard or API for the `gateway_payment_id`. Possible findings:
- PG shows `captured`: the charge happened — the customer's statement may just have a delay (1–2 days for card statement updates). Wait and re-check.
- PG shows `authorized` but not `captured`: the merchant's code marked it as success after auth, skipping the capture step. Bug in the status-update logic.
- PG shows `failed`: the PG returned a failure response, but the merchant's callback handler had a bug — it updated status to `success` regardless of the PG's response code.
- PG shows no record: the payment request never reached the PG. A network timeout caused the merchant's server to assume success.

**Step 3 — Root cause patterns and fixes**

| Root Cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| Callback handler ignores `status` field from PG | Parse and assert `status === 'captured'` before updating DB |
| Network timeout → assume success | Default to `pending`; confirm status via PG's `GET /payments/:id` poll |
| Auth marked as captured | Add state machine check; capture is a distinct API call |
| Webhook signature not validated | PG webhook → verify HMAC; reject unverified payloads |

**Step 4 — Immediate remediation**
- If charge never happened: mark payment as `failed`, refund nothing (nothing was charged), notify merchant to re-initiate.
- If charge happened but DB shows wrong state: update DB to reflect actual state; reconciliation will catch the discrepancy in the next run.
- Implement a **payment status sync job** that queries the PG for all `pending` or `authorized` payments older than 10 minutes and reconciles their status — a standard pattern for eventual consistency in payment systems.

**Q22. "How do you handle settlement delays and ensure a merchant isn't paid twice or not paid at all?"**
A: Settlement delay handling requires a combination of idempotency, audit logging, and automated exception management:

**Idempotent settlement crediting:**
```typescript
async function creditMerchantForSettlement(
  settlementBatchId: string,
  merchantId: string,
  netAmount: number,
  db: PrismaClient,
): Promise<void> {
  await db.$transaction(async (tx) => {
    // Idempotency check: has this batch already been credited?
    const existing = await tx.merchantSettlement.findUnique({
      where: { settlementBatchId_merchantId: { settlementBatchId, merchantId } },
    });
    if (existing) {
      logger.warn(`Settlement batch ${settlementBatchId} already credited for merchant ${merchantId}`);
      return; // idempotent — safe to call multiple times
    }

    // Credit merchant's ledger
    await tx.merchantLedger.create({
      data: { merchantId, amount: netAmount, type: 'settlement', referenceId: settlementBatchId },
    });

    // Mark batch as credited
    await tx.merchantSettlement.create({
      data: { settlementBatchId, merchantId, netAmount, creditedAt: new Date() },
    });
  });
}
```

**Delay detection:**
- Each settlement batch has an expected credit date. A cron job runs daily and checks:
  ```sql
  SELECT * FROM settlement_batches
  WHERE expected_credit_date < NOW() - INTERVAL '1 day'
  AND status != 'credited';
  ```
- Delayed batches trigger a PG investigation request and a hold on the merchant's account display (show "settlement processing" rather than "settled") to avoid merchant confusion.

**Q23. "How would you design the database schema for a payment system that needs to support full audit history, reconciliation, and chargebacks?"**
A: Core schema for a fintech payment system:

```sql
-- Core payment record
CREATE TABLE payments (
  id              UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  merchant_id     UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES merchants(id),
  order_id        UUID NOT NULL,
  status          VARCHAR(30) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'initiated',
  payment_method  VARCHAR(20) NOT NULL,           -- card, upi, netbanking

  captured_amt    NUMERIC(15, 2),
  currency        CHAR(3) NOT NULL DEFAULT 'INR',
  created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW(),
  updated_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW()
);

-- Immutable audit log (never UPDATE/DELETE — only INSERT)
CREATE TABLE payment_events (
  id          UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  payment_id  UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES payments(id),
  event_type  VARCHAR(50) NOT NULL,  -- auth_success, capture, refund_initiated, etc.

-- Settlement records (from PG settlement file)
CREATE TABLE settlement_records (

  mdr_amount          NUMERIC(15, 2) NOT NULL,

);

-- Chargebacks
CREATE TABLE chargebacks (
  id              UUID PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
  payment_id      UUID NOT NULL REFERENCES payments(id),

  disputed_amount NUMERIC(15, 2) NOT NULL,
  chargeback_fee  NUMERIC(15, 2) NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  reason_code     VARCHAR(20),
  due_date        DATE,                 -- representment deadline

);

-- Reconciliation exceptions
CREATE TABLE recon_exceptions (

  delta_amount    NUMERIC(15, 2),

  resolved_by     UUID,
  resolved_at     TIMESTAMPTZ,
  notes           TEXT,
  created_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT NOW()
);
```

## Quick Reference Table

| Phase | Actor | What Moves | Timing | Failure Mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Authorization** | Merchant → PG → Acquirer → Network → Issuer | Approval signal; funds reserved | Synchronous, < 3s | Decline codes, 3DS failure, network timeout |
| **Capture** | Merchant → PG → Acquirer | Obligation to pay; queued for clearing | Merchant-triggered; before auth expiry | Auth expired, overcapture, wrong status |
| **Settlement** | Acquirer → Network → Issuer → Acquirer → Merchant | Actual funds via interbank clearing | T+1 to T+2 (India) | Batch failure, correspondent bank delay, FX issues |
| **Reconciliation** | Merchant internal | No funds — data matching only | Daily (after settlement file) | Missing txns, amount mismatch, chargeback deduction |

| Concept | Definition | Engineering Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Authorization hold | Funds reserved but not transferred | Track expiry; void before expiry if cancelled |
| Interchange | Issuer's fee from acquirer per transaction | Part of MDR; varies by card type and MCC |
| MDR | Total merchant cost per transaction | Deducted from settlement; must be modelled in recon |
| 3DS / AFA | Cardholder authentication before auth | Mandatory CNP in India; liability shift on success |
| Void | Reversal before capture | Free, instant — always prefer over refund |
| Refund | Reversal after capture/settlement | 3–7 days, costs MDR, PG fee |
| Chargeback | Forced reversal by issuer | Merchant loses funds + fee; representment window |
| UTR | Unique Transaction Reference (bank) | Links bank credit to PG settlement batch |
| T+1 settlement | Funds credited next business day | RBI PA guideline compliance requirement |

Know the four phases cold, but what distinguishes a senior answer is understanding the *failure modes* at each phase — what can go wrong, who bears liability, and what your system must do to detect and recover.
