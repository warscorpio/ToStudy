# Partial Captures and Partial Refunds — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is a partial capture, and how does it differ from a full capture?**
A: A partial capture is when a merchant collects less than the authorized amount from a cardholder's reserved funds. In a standard dual-message flow, the authorization reserves a ceiling amount on the cardholder's card. A full capture claims the entire authorized amount. A partial capture claims a subset — say ₹800 of a ₹1000 authorization — and the remaining ₹200 is released back to the cardholder's available balance automatically by the card network.

Key distinctions:

| Property | Full Capture | Partial Capture |
|---|---|---|
| Amount claimed | 100% of authorized amount | < 100% of authorized amount |
| Remainder | No remainder | Released back to cardholder |
| Network support | Universal | Supported on Visa, Mastercard, RuPay; varies by acquirer config |
| Use case | Fixed-price transactions | Variable fulfillment, split shipments, adjustments |
| MDR charged on | Full authorized amount | Only captured amount |

The unreleased remainder does not require an explicit void API call in most networks — the partial capture implicitly releases the delta. However, some acquirer implementations require an explicit reversal for the remainder; always check your acquirer's documentation.

**Q2. What is a partial refund, and how does it differ from a full refund and from a void?**
A: A partial refund returns a subset of the captured (or settled) amount to the cardholder. It is initiated after capture — funds have already moved. Three related concepts compared:

- **Void (authorization reversal)** — cancels the entire authorization before any capture. No money ever moved. Free, instant, no interchange implications.
- **Full refund** — returns 100% of the captured amount after capture/settlement. A new credit transaction is initiated; takes 3–7 business days on the cardholder's statement. The merchant bears the original MDR and typically a refund processing fee.
- **Partial refund** — returns a specified subset of the captured amount. Same mechanics as a full refund (new credit transaction, 3–7 days) but only for the partial amount. MDR on the original capture is not reversed; the partial refund fee is charged on the refunded amount.

A partial refund cannot exceed the captured (or net settled) amount. Attempting to refund more than was captured is rejected by the PG. In a system with multiple partial refunds against a single payment, the sum of all refunds must be tracked and validated against the original captured amount.

**Q3. What business scenarios justify partial captures? Give concrete fintech/e-commerce examples.**
A: Partial capture is appropriate whenever the final transaction amount is not known at authorization time, or when fulfillment is partial:

- **Split shipment (e-commerce)** — a 3-item order is authorized for ₹3000. Only 2 items are in stock and ship today. The merchant captures ₹2000 and releases the ₹1000 remainder. When the third item ships, a new authorization is needed for ₹1000 (the original auth is exhausted).
- **Hotel check-out** — a hotel authorizes ₹10,000 at check-in (covering room + estimated incidentals). If the guest ordered ₹500 in room service, the hotel captures ₹8500 (room rate) + ₹500 = ₹9000 and releases ₹1000.
- **Car rental** — authorized for ₹15,000 including a damage deposit. No damage occurred. Captures ₹12,000 (rental fee); ₹3000 deposit is released via partial capture remainder release.
- **Pre-order with price drop** — a merchant pre-authorizes for ₹5000. By the time the item ships, a promotional price of ₹4500 applies. Partial capture for ₹4500; ₹500 returned to cardholder.
- **BNPL / EMI disbursement** — in a loan origination context, a lender may authorize a credit line draw and disburse only the requested tranche, partially capturing the authorized limit.
- **Fuel / pay-at-pump** — station authorizes ₹5000 as a ceiling; actual fuel dispensed costs ₹1847. Partial capture for ₹1847; remainder released.

**Q4. What business scenarios justify partial refunds?**
A: Partial refunds are appropriate when the merchant owes the customer a portion of the captured amount, but not the full amount:

- **Returned item in a multi-item order** — customer bought 3 items, returns 1. Merchant refunds the price of that item only.
- **Quality complaint with partial compensation** — product arrived damaged; merchant offers a 20% goodwill refund rather than a full return.
- **Cancelled add-on** — a base product + shipping + insurance were captured together. Customer cancels insurance only. Partial refund for the insurance premium.
- **Pricing error correction** — a ₹999 item was charged at ₹1099 due to a display bug. Merchant partially refunds the ₹100 overcharge.
- **Promo code applied post-payment** — customer forgot to apply a 15% discount code; merchant partially refunds the discount amount as goodwill.
- **Regulatory / RBI mandate** — in lending platforms, if a loan is prepaid or foreclosed, excess interest collected must be partially refunded per RBI Fair Practices Code.

**Q5. What is the relationship between partial captures, partial refunds, and the settlement lifecycle?**
A: The two operations occupy different phases of the payment lifecycle and have different financial impacts:

```
Authorization (₹1000 reserved)
        │
        ▼
Partial Capture (₹800 captured — ₹200 released to cardholder)
        │
        ▼
Settlement (₹800 less MDR credited to merchant — e.g., ₹784 at 2% MDR)
        │
        ├── [T+0] Partial Refund initiated: ₹200
        │         PG initiates credit to cardholder
        │         Merchant's PG ledger debited ₹200
        │
        ▼
Next settlement cycle: ₹200 netted against next day's settlement
        │
        ▼
Cardholder receives ₹200 credit (3–7 business days)
```

Critical point: the merchant's MDR on the ₹800 capture is **not reversed** when a ₹200 partial refund is issued. The merchant has permanently paid MDR on the captured amount. Some PGs offer "MDR reversal on refund" as a merchant feature, but this is a PG-level commercial arrangement, not a network-level guarantee. In the reconciliation pipeline, partial refunds appear as deductions in the settlement file — either netted against the current day's settlement batch or as a separate line item depending on PG implementation.

## Section 2: Partial Captures In Depth

**Q6. How do card networks handle the released remainder after a partial capture — is an explicit reversal required?**
A: Network behavior varies:

| Network | Remainder Release Mechanism | Explicit Reversal Required? |
|---|---|---|
| Visa | Partial capture implicitly releases remainder via the authorization system | No — automatic |
| Mastercard | Same as Visa — partial capture message carries final amount; network releases delta | No — automatic |
| RuPay | Depends on issuer implementation; most major issuers support implicit release | Usually no; confirm with acquirer |
| American Express | Requires explicit partial reversal message for the remainder | Yes |

Even where implicit release is supported, timing matters. The cardholder's available balance may not reflect the released amount immediately — some issuers process the release on the next business day. This creates customer service scenarios: a cardholder sees their balance reduced by ₹1000 (the auth), pays ₹800 at checkout, and doesn't see the ₹200 return for 24–48 hours. Your support team must be trained on this timing.

From an engineering perspective, always store both the authorized amount and the captured amount explicitly. Do not derive the released amount dynamically — store it:

```typescript
interface PaymentRecord {
  authorizedAmount: number;      // 1000 — what was reserved
  capturedAmount: number;        // 800  — what was claimed
  releasedAmount: number;        // 200  — authorizedAmount - capturedAmount (denormalized for query efficiency)
  captureReference: string;      // PG's capture transaction ID
  capturedAt: Date;
}
```

**Q7. Can a single authorization support multiple partial captures?**
A: Generally, **no** — standard card network rules allow only one capture per authorization. Once a partial capture is submitted, the authorization is consumed and cannot be partially captured again for the remainder.

The exception is **incremental authorization**, supported on Visa (for hotel, car rental, cruise MCCs) and Mastercard (similar). Incremental auth allows a merchant to:
1. Authorize an initial amount.
2. Extend or increase the authorization if the anticipated charge grows (e.g., guest extends hotel stay).
3. Then capture the final cumulative amount.

For split-shipment scenarios requiring multiple captures, the correct pattern is:
- Partial capture for shipment 1 (authorization is consumed for that amount).
- Issue a **new authorization** for the remaining items when ready to ship (customer must have card on file or be redirected to re-authorize).

This is a common gotcha — engineers assume that capturing ₹800 of a ₹1000 auth leaves a ₹200 "cap balance" to capture later. It does not. The remainder is released, not held for a future capture.

```typescript
// WRONG — attempting second capture on same auth
async function captureShipment2(paymentId: string, amount: number) {
  const payment = await repo.findById(paymentId);
  // payment.status is 'captured' after shipment 1 — this will be rejected
  return gateway.capture(payment.gatewayPaymentId, amount); // ❌ will fail
}

// CORRECT — new authorization for remaining items
async function authorizeForShipment2(
  orderId: string,
  customerId: string,
  amount: number,
  savedCardToken: string,
) {
  return gateway.createPayment({
    orderId: `${orderId}-shipment-2`,
    customerId,
    amount,
    paymentMethod: { type: 'saved_card', token: savedCardToken },
  });
}
```

**Q8. How do you validate a partial capture request in a backend service to prevent business logic errors?**
A: Five validation layers before issuing a capture to the PG:

```typescript
// payments/capture/capture.validator.ts
export class CaptureValidator {
  validate(payment: Payment, captureAmount: number): void {
    // 1. Status check — must be in authorized state
    if (payment.status !== 'authorized') {
      throw new InvalidPaymentStateError(
        `Cannot capture payment with status '${payment.status}'. Expected 'authorized'.`,
      );
    }

    // 2. Auth expiry check — avoid wasting a capture attempt on an expired auth
    if (new Date() >= payment.authExpiry) {
      throw new AuthorizationExpiredError(
        `Authorization expired at ${payment.authExpiry.toISOString()}. Re-authorization required.`,
      );
    }

    // 3. Amount bounds — must be positive and within authorized ceiling
    if (captureAmount <= 0) {
      throw new InvalidAmountError(`Capture amount must be greater than zero.`);
    }
    if (captureAmount > payment.authorizedAmount) {
      throw new OvercaptureError(
        `Capture amount ₹${captureAmount} exceeds authorized amount ₹${payment.authorizedAmount}.`,
      );
    }

    // 4. Minimum capture threshold — some acquirers reject captures below ₹1
    const MIN_CAPTURE_AMOUNT = 1.00;
    if (captureAmount < MIN_CAPTURE_AMOUNT) {
      throw new InvalidAmountError(
        `Capture amount ₹${captureAmount} is below minimum threshold ₹${MIN_CAPTURE_AMOUNT}.`,
      );
    }

    // 5. Currency consistency
    if (payment.currency !== 'INR') {
      // For cross-border: validate FX rate is within acceptable tolerance
      this.validateFxTolerance(payment, captureAmount);
    }
  }

  private validateFxTolerance(payment: Payment, captureAmount: number): void {
    const tolerance = 0.05; // 5% tolerance for FX fluctuation between auth and capture
    const maxAllowed = payment.authorizedAmountInSettlementCurrency * (1 + tolerance);
    if (captureAmount > maxAllowed) {
      throw new FxToleranceExceededError(
        `Capture amount exceeds authorized amount by more than ${tolerance * 100}% after FX conversion.`,
      );
    }
  }
}
```

**Q9. How does partial capture interact with fraud scoring and risk systems?**
A: Partial capture creates a divergence between the authorized amount and the captured amount that risk systems must be aware of:

- **Velocity rules** — risk engines track authorization amounts for velocity checks (e.g., "flag if total authorized amount from this card exceeds ₹50,000 in 24h"). A series of large authorizations with small partial captures can be used to probe card limits without triggering amount-based velocity rules. Risk systems must track captured amounts, not just authorized amounts.
- **Chargeback liability** — the chargeback amount is based on the **captured** amount, not the authorized amount. If ₹800 was captured and the customer disputes, the chargeback is for ₹800. Risk models that use authorized amount as a proxy for exposure will overstate the actual chargeback liability.
- **Pattern detection** — a merchant repeatedly authorizing ₹999 (just below a ₹1000 monitoring threshold) and capturing ₹100–₹200 is a red flag for both acquirer risk systems and card network compliance teams. Your internal fraud tooling should alert on this pattern.
- **Pre-auth abuse** — in hotel/rental contexts, some merchants authorize large amounts and never capture (using the auth as an implicit security deposit). Networks restrict this: Visa requires capture within the auth validity window; uncaptured auths beyond the window are automatically voided.

## Section 3: Partial Refunds In Depth

**Q10. How do you implement a partial refund service that is idempotent and handles concurrent refund requests safely?**
A: Partial refund idempotency is critical — a double-refund caused by a client retry is a direct financial loss. The implementation must be safe under concurrent requests and network failures:

```typescript
// payments/refund/refund.service.ts
@Injectable()
export class RefundService {
  constructor(
    private readonly paymentsRepo: PaymentsRepository,
    private readonly refundsRepo: RefundsRepository,
    private readonly gatewayService: GatewayService,
    private readonly db: PrismaClient,
  ) {}

  async initiatePartialRefund(
    paymentId: string,
    refundAmount: number,
    idempotencyKey: string,       // caller-supplied; UUID per refund intent
    reason: string,
  ): Promise<Refund> {
    return this.db.$transaction(async (tx) => {
      // 1. Idempotency check — return existing refund if key already processed
      const existing = await tx.refund.findUnique({ where: { idempotencyKey } });
      if (existing) return existing;

      // 2. Lock the payment row to prevent concurrent refund races
      const payment = await tx.$queryRaw<Payment[]>`
        SELECT * FROM payments WHERE id = ${paymentId} FOR UPDATE
      `;
      if (!payment[0]) throw new PaymentNotFoundError(paymentId);

      // 3. Validate refundable amount
      const totalRefunded = await tx.refund.aggregate({
        where: { paymentId, status: { in: ['pending', 'success'] } },
        _sum: { amount: true },
      });
      const alreadyRefunded = totalRefunded._sum.amount ?? 0;
      const refundable = payment[0].capturedAmount - alreadyRefunded;

      if (refundAmount <= 0 || refundAmount > refundable) {
        throw new InvalidRefundAmountError(
          `Refund amount ₹${refundAmount} exceeds refundable balance ₹${refundable}.`,
        );
      }

      // 4. Create refund record in 'pending' state before calling PG
      //    (never call external API inside a DB transaction — see note below)
      const refund = await tx.refund.create({
        data: {
          paymentId,
          amount: refundAmount,
          reason,
          idempotencyKey,
          status: 'pending',
          gatewayRefundId: null,
        },
      });

      return refund;
    });
    // Transaction commits here — refund is durably pending

    // 5. Call PG outside the DB transaction to avoid holding DB lock during HTTP call
    // This is handled by a background job that processes 'pending' refunds
  }

  // Called by a queue worker / cron — processes pending refunds
  async processRefund(refundId: string): Promise<void> {
    const refund = await this.refundsRepo.findById(refundId);
    const payment = await this.paymentsRepo.findById(refund.paymentId);

    try {
      const result = await this.gatewayService.issueRefund({
        gatewayPaymentId: payment.gatewayPaymentId,
        amount: refund.amount,
        idempotencyKey: refund.idempotencyKey,  // pass through to PG for their idempotency
      });
      await this.refundsRepo.update(refundId, {
        status: 'success',
        gatewayRefundId: result.refundId,
        processedAt: new Date(),
      });
    } catch (err) {
      await this.refundsRepo.update(refundId, {
        status: 'failed',
        failureReason: err.message,
      });
      throw err; // let queue worker retry
    }
  }
}
```

**Critical design note**: never call an external PG API inside a DB transaction. If the HTTP call succeeds but the DB commit fails, you have issued a refund with no record of it. The correct pattern is: persist the intent inside the transaction, then process externally after commit. A background job with idempotency on the PG call handles the execution.

**Q11. How do you track the refundable balance for a payment across multiple partial refunds?**
A: The refundable balance at any point is:

```
refundable_balance = captured_amount - SUM(refund.amount WHERE status IN ('pending', 'success'))
```

Including `pending` refunds in the deduction is critical. A refund initiated but not yet confirmed by the PG must reduce the refundable balance immediately — otherwise two concurrent refund requests for the same amount can both pass validation and result in a double refund.

Database query:

```sql
-- Refundable balance for a payment
SELECT
  p.captured_amount,
  COALESCE(SUM(r.amount) FILTER (WHERE r.status IN ('pending', 'success')), 0) AS total_refunded,
  p.captured_amount - COALESCE(SUM(r.amount) FILTER (WHERE r.status IN ('pending', 'success')), 0) AS refundable_balance
FROM payments p
LEFT JOIN refunds r ON r.payment_id = p.id
WHERE p.id = $1
GROUP BY p.id, p.captured_amount;
```

The `FOR UPDATE` row lock in the refund initiation transaction (see Q10) ensures that two concurrent refund requests cannot both read the same refundable balance and both pass validation before either commits.

**Q12. What happens when a partial refund fails at the PG layer after the merchant's DB has recorded it as initiated?**
A: This is a classic distributed system problem — the merchant's system has committed a refund record but the PG rejected the refund. The customer has not received their money, but the merchant's DB shows a refund. Handling:

**States a refund can be in:**

```
initiated (in merchant DB) → pending → [success | failed | gateway_rejected]
```

Recovery flow:

```typescript
// Retry strategy for failed refunds
async function retryFailedRefund(refundId: string): Promise<void> {
  const refund = await refundsRepo.findById(refundId);

  if (refund.retryCount >= MAX_REFUND_RETRIES) {
    // Escalate to manual ops — customer still owed money
    await refundsRepo.update(refundId, { status: 'manual_review' });
    await alertingService.escalateRefundFailure(refundId);
    return;
  }

  // PG-specific failure classification
  if (isInsufficientMerchantBalance(refund.failureReason)) {
    // Merchant's PG account has insufficient funds to cover the refund
    // Options: top up PG account, or settle directly via NEFT/IMPS to customer
    await alertingService.alertMerchantInsufficientBalance(refund);
    return;
  }

  if (isTransientPgError(refund.failureReason)) {
    // Network or PG system error — safe to retry
    const delay = exponentialBackoff(refund.retryCount);
    await retryQueue.enqueue({ refundId }, { delay });
    await refundsRepo.update(refundId, { retryCount: refund.retryCount + 1 });
  }
}
```

**Merchant balance insufficiency** is the most operationally painful failure mode. If a merchant's PG ledger balance is lower than the refund amount (due to recent chargebacks, low sales volume, or reserve holds), the PG cannot process the refund even if the original payment was captured months ago. The merchant must either top up their PG account or issue the refund via an alternative channel (bank transfer, wallet credit). RBI's PA guidelines require PAs to maintain adequate reserves to fund merchant refunds — but this is the PA's obligation, not the merchant's engineering concern.

**Q13. How do partial refunds appear in the settlement file, and how do you reconcile them?**
A: Partial refunds appear in settlement files in two ways depending on the PG:

- **Netted settlement** — the PG deducts the refund amount from the next day's settlement. The settlement file shows a line item: `refund_deduction: ₹200` against the original `pg_txn_id`. Net settlement to the merchant's bank account is reduced by that amount.
- **Separate line item** — the refund is a distinct record in the settlement file with `transaction_type: 'refund'` and a negative amount. The matching key is the `pg_refund_id` that was returned when the refund was initiated.

Reconciliation logic must handle both patterns:

```typescript
// reconciliation/matchers/refund-matcher.ts
export function matchRefunds(
  internalRefunds: Refund[],
  settlementLines: SettlementLine[],
): RefundMatchResult {
  const refundLines = settlementLines.filter(
    l => l.transactionType === 'refund' || l.refundDeduction > 0,
  );

  const matched: MatchedRefund[] = [];
  const unmatched: Refund[] = [];

  for (const refund of internalRefunds) {
    const line = refundLines.find(
      l => l.pgRefundId === refund.gatewayRefundId ||
           l.pgTxnId === refund.payment.gatewayPaymentId && l.refundAmount === refund.amount,
    );

    if (!line) {
      unmatched.push(refund); // refund initiated but not in settlement — investigate
    } else if (line.refundAmount !== refund.amount) {
      matched.push({ refund, line, discrepancy: line.refundAmount - refund.amount });
    } else {
      matched.push({ refund, line, discrepancy: 0 });
    }
  }

  return { matched, unmatched };
}
```

Common reconciliation discrepancies with refunds:
- Refund in merchant DB as `success` but absent from settlement file — PG confirmed the refund but hasn't included it in settlement yet (timing: refunds may appear 1–2 days after confirmation).
- Refund in settlement file but merchant DB shows `failed` — retry loop or PG retry succeeded after merchant marked it failed. Merchant's DB must be updated; otherwise the customer will be refunded twice if the ops team manually initiates another refund.

## Section 4: Edge Cases, Failure Modes & Advanced Scenarios

**Q14. How do you handle a partial refund request on a payment that has already been partially refunded?**
A: This is the multiple-partial-refund scenario — a single captured payment has had one or more partial refunds issued against it. The engineering requirement is that the sum of all partial refunds never exceeds the captured amount, and the system must enforce this atomically.

```typescript
// Example: ₹1000 captured, ₹300 already refunded, new request for ₹400

// State before second refund:
// payments: { id: 'P1', capturedAmount: 1000 }
// refunds: [{ paymentId: 'P1', amount: 300, status: 'success' }]

// Refundable: 1000 - 300 = ₹700 — ₹400 request is valid

// State before third refund (₹400 pending):
// refunds: [
//   { paymentId: 'P1', amount: 300, status: 'success' },
//   { paymentId: 'P1', amount: 400, status: 'pending' },
// ]
// Refundable: 1000 - 300 - 400 = ₹300 — a request for ₹350 would be rejected

// DB constraint to enforce the ceiling (belt-and-suspenders):
// CHECK constraint is database-level enforcement; application logic is first line
```

Add a PostgreSQL check constraint as a safety net — the application validates first, but the DB constraint catches any race condition that slips through:

```sql
-- Trigger-based enforcement (application-level validation is primary)
CREATE OR REPLACE FUNCTION check_refund_ceiling()
RETURNS TRIGGER AS $$
DECLARE
  total_refunded NUMERIC;
  captured_amt   NUMERIC;
BEGIN
  SELECT captured_amount INTO captured_amt
  FROM payments WHERE id = NEW.payment_id;

  SELECT COALESCE(SUM(amount), 0) INTO total_refunded
  FROM refunds
  WHERE payment_id = NEW.payment_id
    AND status IN ('pending', 'success')
    AND id != NEW.id;  -- exclude the current row being inserted

  IF (total_refunded + NEW.amount) > captured_amt THEN
    RAISE EXCEPTION 'Refund amount exceeds captured amount. Captured: %, Already refunded: %, Requested: %',
      captured_amt, total_refunded, NEW.amount;
  END IF;
  RETURN NEW;
END;
$$ LANGUAGE plpgsql;

CREATE TRIGGER enforce_refund_ceiling
BEFORE INSERT ON refunds
FOR EACH ROW EXECUTE FUNCTION check_refund_ceiling();
```

**Q15. What is the interaction between partial captures and GST reconciliation in India?**
A: GST adds a reconciliation dimension that pure payment engineers often miss. In India, the invoice amount and the payment amount must match for GSTR-1 reporting. Partial capture breaks this in two ways:

- **Invoice already raised for full order** — the merchant has issued a GST invoice for ₹1000 (including ₹152.54 GST at 18%). Only ₹800 is captured. The invoice must be revised (credit note issued for ₹200) to match the captured amount. If not, GSTR-1 and GSTR-2A will mismatch, triggering a GST audit flag.
- **MDR and GST on MDR** — the PG charges MDR on the captured amount and 18% GST on the MDR. This GST is an input tax credit (ITC) for the merchant. The settlement file must show: `mdr_amount`, `gst_on_mdr`, and the PG's GSTIN (for ITC claim). If the settlement file shows MDR based on the authorized amount rather than the captured amount, the ITC claim will be inflated — a compliance risk.

Engineering implication: the payment service must emit events that the invoicing/accounting service consumes to trigger invoice revision when a partial capture amount differs from the invoiced amount:

```typescript
// After successful partial capture — emit event for accounting service
await eventBus.publish('payment.partially_captured', {
  paymentId: payment.id,
  orderId: payment.orderId,
  authorizedAmount: payment.authorizedAmount,
  capturedAmount: captureAmount,
  delta: payment.authorizedAmount - captureAmount,
  capturedAt: new Date(),
});

// accounting-service/handlers/payment-partially-captured.handler.ts
```

**Q16. How do you handle partial captures and partial refunds in a marketplace or split-payment context?**
A: In a marketplace model (e.g., an aggregator platform with multiple sub-merchants), a single customer payment may be split across multiple sellers. Partial capture and partial refund in this context require split-payment-aware logic:

```
Customer pays ₹1000 for:
  - Seller A: ₹600
  - Seller B: ₹400

Authorization: ₹1000 (against the marketplace's acquiring account)
Partial capture: ₹800 (only Seller A's items shipped — ₹600; Seller B ships ₹200 of ₹400)

Internal split:
  - Seller A portion captured: ₹600
  - Seller B portion captured: ₹200 (partial)
  - Released: ₹200 (Seller B's unshipped ₹200)

Settlement split:
  - Marketplace receives ₹784 net (₹800 less 2% MDR)
  - Routes ₹600 * (784/800) = ~₹588 to Seller A ledger
  - Routes ₹200 * (784/800) = ~₹196 to Seller B ledger
```

Partial refund in this context:

```typescript
interface MarketplaceRefund {
  paymentId: string;
  totalRefundAmount: number;
  splits: Array<{
    sellerId: string;
    refundAmount: number;  // must sum to totalRefundAmount
  }>;
}

async function initiateMarketplacePartialRefund(
  refundRequest: MarketplaceRefund,
): Promise<void> {
  // Validate total
  const splitTotal = refundRequest.splits.reduce((sum, s) => sum + s.refundAmount, 0);
  if (splitTotal !== refundRequest.totalRefundAmount) {
    throw new InvalidRefundSplitError('Split amounts must sum to total refund amount.');
  }

  // Validate each seller's refundable balance
  for (const split of refundRequest.splits) {
    const sellerBalance = await sellerLedgerRepo.getRefundableBalance(
      refundRequest.paymentId,
      split.sellerId,
    );
    if (split.refundAmount > sellerBalance) {
      throw new InvalidRefundAmountError(
        `Seller ${split.sellerId} refundable balance ₹${sellerBalance} < requested ₹${split.refundAmount}.`,
      );
    }
  }

  // Single PG refund for the total; internal ledger splits handled separately
  await refundService.initiatePartialRefund(
    refundRequest.paymentId,
    refundRequest.totalRefundAmount,
    generateIdempotencyKey(refundRequest),
    'marketplace_partial_refund',
  );

  // Update individual seller ledgers
  for (const split of refundRequest.splits) {
    await sellerLedgerRepo.debitForRefund(refundRequest.paymentId, split.sellerId, split.refundAmount);
  }
}
```

**Q17. What are the card network rules and penalties around misuse of partial capture?**
A: Card networks have explicit rules that merchants and acquirers must follow:

- **No overcapture** — capturing more than the authorized amount is a hard violation. Acquirers must reject overcapture at their processing host. Merchants caught repeatedly attempting overcapture can face fines and eventual merchant account termination.
- **Capture within auth validity window** — partial or full capture must happen before the auth expires. Expired-auth capture attempts are declined; the merchant loses the sale.
- **One capture per authorization** — standard rules prohibit split captures against a single auth (see Q7). Using multiple partial captures against one auth (if an acquirer erroneously allows it) violates network rules and can result in duplicate billing chargebacks.
- **Pre-auth abuse** — authorizing amounts significantly larger than the expected final charge (to give a wide partial-capture window) is a violation in non-permitted MCCs. For example, a restaurant authorizing ₹10,000 for a ₹200 meal is not permitted outside specific card programs.
- **Refund-to-original-card rule** — refunds must be returned to the same card that was charged. A merchant cannot issue a partial refund to a different card or a bank account if the original payment was by card. Violation triggers regulatory and network sanctions. The exception is when the original card is expired or cancelled — acquirers have specific procedures for alternative refund channels in that case.
- **Timely refund processing** — card networks require refunds to be submitted within 30 days of the original transaction. A merchant that delays refunds beyond this window is in violation; some networks extend this to 180 days but with additional documentation requirements.

## Section 5: Scenario / System Design Style Questions

**Q18. "Design a partial refund API for a high-scale fintech platform. What are the key design decisions?"**
A: Full design for a production-grade partial refund API:

**API contract:**
```
POST /v1/payments/:paymentId/refunds
Content-Type: application/json
Idempotency-Key: <uuid>

{
  "amount": 200.00,
  "currency": "INR",
  "reason": "item_returned",
  "metadata": {
    "orderId": "ORD-12345",
    "returnId": "RET-789"
  }
}

Response 201:
{
  "refundId": "REF-abc123",
  "paymentId": "PAY-xyz",
  "amount": 200.00,
  "status": "pending",
  "estimatedArrival": "2026-08-25",
  "createdAt": "2026-08-20T10:30:00Z"
}
```

**Key design decisions:**

1. **Idempotency via `Idempotency-Key` header** — server stores `(idempotency_key, response_body)` in Redis with 24h TTL. Identical key returns cached response without reprocessing. Required for client retry safety.

2. **Async processing** — refund initiation is synchronous (validates, persists, returns `pending`). PG call is async via a job queue (SQS + worker). This prevents client timeout on slow PG responses and allows retry without client involvement.

3. **Status polling endpoint** — since processing is async, expose `GET /v1/refunds/:refundId` for status polling, and send webhook callbacks on `refund.success` / `refund.failed` events.

4. **Refund ledger** — maintain a `refunds` table separate from `payments`. Never update the original payment's `captured_amount` — it represents what was captured, not what was net after refunds. The refundable balance is computed at query time.

5. **PG idempotency pass-through** — forward the merchant's `Idempotency-Key` (or a derived key) to the PG's refund API. This prevents double-refund even if the worker crashes and retries after the PG has already processed the refund.

6. **Rate limiting** — limit refund requests per payment to prevent abuse (e.g., max 10 refunds per payment, max 5 refund requests per second per merchant).

7. **Audit trail** — every state transition of a refund is logged to a `refund_events` table (immutable append-only log). Required for dispute resolution and RBI audit readiness.

**Q19. "A customer service team reports that a partial refund was processed twice for the same return. How do you investigate and prevent recurrence?"**
A: Double refund root cause investigation:

**Step 1 — Confirm the double refund**
```sql
SELECT r.id, r.amount, r.status, r.idempotency_key, r.gateway_refund_id, r.created_at
FROM refunds r
WHERE r.payment_id = '<payment_id>'
  AND r.amount = <refund_amount>
ORDER BY r.created_at;
```
If two rows exist with different `idempotency_key` values and both show `success` — two distinct refund intents were created and both processed.

**Step 2 — Identify how two intents were created**
Common causes:
- **CS team manually re-triggered** — the CS tool did not check for existing pending/successful refunds before allowing a new refund request.
- **Client retry with new idempotency key** — the client (frontend or internal tool) generated a fresh UUID on retry instead of reusing the original key.
- **Webhook double-delivery** — a PG webhook for `refund.success` was delivered twice; the handler processed it twice without deduplication.
- **Race condition** — two concurrent requests (customer's UI + CS tool) both passed validation before either committed to the DB (missing row lock).

**Step 3 — Immediate remediation**
- Contact PG to reverse one of the refunds if within the PG's reversal window (rare — most PGs don't support refund reversal; the merchant must reclaim via the customer).
- Flag the duplicate refund in the internal ledger as `duplicate` for accounting reconciliation.
- Create a manual debit note for the merchant's finance team to account for the erroneous credit.

**Step 4 — Prevent recurrence**
```typescript
// Fix 1: CS tool checks for existing refunds before allowing new request
async function canInitiateRefund(paymentId: string, amount: number): Promise<boolean> {
  const refundable = await getRefundableBalance(paymentId);
  return refundable >= amount;
}

// Fix 2: Stable idempotency key derived from return ID, not random UUID
function generateRefundIdempotencyKey(paymentId: string, returnId: string): string {
  return `refund:${paymentId}:${returnId}`; // deterministic — same input always same key
}

// Fix 3: Webhook deduplication
async function handleRefundWebhook(event: PgWebhookEvent): Promise<void> {
  const dedupeKey = `webhook:${event.pgRefundId}`;
  const already = await redis.set(dedupeKey, '1', 'NX', 'EX', 86400); // NX = only set if not exists
  if (!already) {
    logger.warn(`Duplicate webhook for refund ${event.pgRefundId} — skipping`);
    return;
  }
  await processRefundConfirmation(event);
}
```

## Quick Reference Table

| Property | Partial Capture | Partial Refund |
|---|---|---|
| Phase | Before settlement (post-auth) | After capture (post-settlement or same day) |
| Amount constraint | ≤ authorized amount; > 0 | ≤ (captured amount − prior refunds); > 0 |
| Money movement | Remainder released to cardholder; merchant receives captured amount | Credit initiated to cardholder; merchant's PG ledger debited |
| MDR impact | MDR charged on captured amount only | MDR on original capture not reversed (unless PG commercial agreement) |
| Timing | Synchronous (within auth validity window) | Async; 3–7 business days to cardholder |
| Network support | One capture per auth; no second partial capture | Multiple partial refunds allowed up to captured ceiling |
| Void vs refund | Use void if pre-capture — free and instant | Partial refund is the only option post-capture |
| Idempotency key | Not typically required by PG for capture | Critical — prevents double refund on retry |
| GST implication | Invoice must be revised for delta amount | Credit note may be required for refunded amount |
| Chargeback risk | Chargeback based on captured amount | Issued refund reduces chargeback exposure for that amount |
| DB pattern | Update `captured_amount`; store `released_amount` | Append to `refunds` table; never mutate `captured_amount` |

**Tip for the interview:** Partial capture and partial refund questions surface in system design rounds as concurrency and idempotency problems ("how do you prevent double refund?"), in domain knowledge rounds as lifecycle questions ("what happens to the remaining ₹200 after a partial capture?"), and in debugging scenarios ("a refund was processed twice — how do you investigate?"). The senior differentiator is demonstrating that you understand the *financial* consequences of engineering decisions: MDR is not reversed on refund, multiple partial refunds require atomic balance tracking, and calling external PG APIs inside a DB transaction is a consistency anti-pattern. In Indian fintech specifically, layer in GST credit note implications and RBI's Fair Practices Code requirement for timely refunds on prepaid loans — that's the domain depth that separates a backend engineer from a payments engineer.
