# Payment Lifecycle: Double-Entry Bookkeeping — Debits, Credits & Ledger Balance Integrity — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

> **Domain:** Fintech / Payment Systems Architecture

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is double-entry bookkeeping, and why does every payment system eventually need it?**

A: Double-entry bookkeeping is an accounting system in which every financial transaction is recorded as two equal and opposite entries — a **debit** to one account and a **credit** to another — such that the sum of all debits always equals the sum of all credits. The system has been the foundation of commercial accounting since Luca Pacioli formalized it in 1494, and it remains the basis of every modern financial ledger, from a bank's core banking system to a fintech startup's wallet.

Every payment system eventually needs it because single-entry systems — a simple `payments` table with a balance column — cannot answer the questions that regulators, auditors, and engineers eventually ask:

- Where did this money come from?
- Where did it go?
- Can I prove that no money was created or destroyed in transit?
- If the system crashed mid-transaction, can I tell which side of the transfer completed?

A single-entry system can tell you a balance. A double-entry system can prove that the balance is correct, trace every rupee through every state change, and self-verify through the invariant that **assets = liabilities + equity** at all times. When an auditor asks "prove that your ledger hasn't been tampered with," the answer is a double-entry system — because any tampering with a single entry breaks the balance invariant and is immediately detectable.

---

**Q2. What do "debit" and "credit" mean in accounting, and why are they confusing to engineers?**

A: Debit and credit are among the most widely misunderstood terms in fintech engineering, because their everyday meaning (debit = money out, credit = money in) is the opposite of their accounting meaning for certain account types.

**The accounting rule:**

| Account type | Debit effect | Credit effect | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Asset** (cash, receivables) | Increases ↑ | Decreases ↓ | Debit |
| **Liability** (payables, deposits) | Decreases ↓ | Increases ↑ | Credit |
| **Equity** (retained earnings) | Decreases ↓ | Increases ↑ | Credit |
| **Revenue** (fees earned) | Decreases ↓ | Increases ↑ | Credit |
| **Expense** (fees paid, losses) | Increases ↑ | Decreases ↓ | Debit |

**Why engineers get confused:** When a bank says "we've credited your account," they mean your balance went up — from their perspective, your account is a **liability** (they owe you the money), and crediting a liability increases it. From your perspective as an account holder, it feels like a positive. Neither is wrong — they are looking at the same entry from opposite sides of the balance sheet.

**Practical mnemonic for payment systems:**

- Money flows **into** an account → **debit the account** if it is an asset (your cash account goes up).
- Money flows **out of** an account → **credit the account** if it is an asset (your cash account goes down).
- Think of debit as "left side of the T-account" and credit as "right side" — the rules follow mechanically from that.

In payment system code, many teams sidestep this confusion by not using the words "debit" and "credit" at all — instead using `amount` with a sign convention (positive = inflow, negative = outflow) and storing the account type separately. This is a valid pragmatic choice as long as the sign convention is documented and enforced consistently.

**Q3. What is the fundamental equation of double-entry bookkeeping, and what does it guarantee?**

A: The fundamental accounting equation is:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

Expanded:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity + Revenue − Expenses
```

Or equivalently:

```
Sum of all Debit balances = Sum of all Credit balances
```

**What it guarantees:**

Every valid transaction in a double-entry system leaves this equation balanced. If a transaction moves ₹10,000 from a customer's wallet to a merchant's wallet:

- Customer wallet (asset, debit normal): decreases → credit ₹10,000.
- Merchant wallet (asset, debit normal): increases → debit ₹10,000.
- Net effect on the equation: zero. The total of all asset balances is unchanged; no money was created or destroyed.

**The self-verification property:** If the sum of all debits ≠ sum of all credits in your ledger, there is a bug, a missing entry, or data corruption. This check runs in O(1) if you maintain a running total, or O(n) as a full ledger audit. No single-entry system can offer this property — there is no equivalent consistency check on a `balance` column.

**In SQL:**

```sql
-- This should always return 0 in a correct double-entry ledger
SELECT SUM(
  CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT'  THEN  amount_paise
       WHEN entry_type = 'CREDIT' THEN -amount_paise
  END
) AS balance_check
FROM ledger_entries;
```

If this returns non-zero, there is a corrupted entry or a missing counterpart somewhere in the ledger.

**Q4. What is a ledger, an account, and a journal entry? How do these terms map to database tables?**

A: These are the core data structures of double-entry bookkeeping:

**Ledger:** The complete record of all financial transactions in the system. In database terms, the ledger is the set of all `ledger_entries` rows. It is the source of truth for every balance and every audit.

**Account:** A named bucket that accumulates debits and credits over time. An account has a type (asset, liability, equity, revenue, expense), a normal balance direction, and a current balance derived from its entries. In database terms, an account is a row in an `accounts` table; its balance is `SUM` of its entries.

**Journal entry (or transaction):** A group of two or more ledger entries that together form one balanced financial event. Every journal entry must have `SUM(debits) = SUM(credits)`. In database terms, a journal entry is a row in a `journal_entries` table; its child rows are the individual `ledger_entries`. The parent-child relationship enforces that entries are always reviewed and audited as a group.

**Mapping to tables:**

```sql
-- Account: a named bucket with a type
CREATE TABLE accounts (
    account_id    UUID    PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    name          TEXT    NOT NULL,                     -- "Customer Wallet: user_123"
    account_type  TEXT    NOT NULL,                     -- 'ASSET' | 'LIABILITY' | 'EQUITY' | 'REVENUE' | 'EXPENSE'
    normal_balance TEXT   NOT NULL,                     -- 'DEBIT' | 'CREDIT'
    currency      CHAR(3) NOT NULL,
    created_at    TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

-- Journal entry: the event header (one per financial transaction)
CREATE TABLE journal_entries (
    journal_entry_id  UUID        PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    description       TEXT        NOT NULL,             -- "Payment: order_abc"
    reference_id      UUID        NOT NULL,             -- idempotency: payment_id, refund_id
    reference_type    TEXT        NOT NULL,             -- 'PAYMENT' | 'REFUND' | 'FEE' | 'TRANSFER'
    created_at        TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    UNIQUE (reference_id, reference_type)               -- one journal entry per business event
);

-- Ledger entry: one row per account touched by the journal entry
CREATE TABLE ledger_entries (
    entry_id          UUID        PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    journal_entry_id  UUID        NOT NULL REFERENCES journal_entries(journal_entry_id),
    account_id        UUID        NOT NULL REFERENCES accounts(account_id),
    entry_type        TEXT        NOT NULL CHECK (entry_type IN ('DEBIT', 'CREDIT')),
    amount_paise      BIGINT      NOT NULL CHECK (amount_paise > 0),  -- always positive; direction from entry_type
    created_at        TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

CREATE INDEX idx_ledger_account ON ledger_entries (account_id, created_at DESC);
CREATE INDEX idx_ledger_journal ON ledger_entries (journal_entry_id);
```

**Key design decision:** `amount_paise` is always positive; the direction is encoded in `entry_type`. This avoids signed amount confusion (is a negative debit a credit? Is a negative credit a debit?). The sign convention is explicit, not implicit.

## Section 2: Account Structure & Chart of Accounts

**Q5. What is a Chart of Accounts, and how do you design one for a lending / payments platform?**

A: The **Chart of Accounts (CoA)** is the complete list of accounts used in a system, organized by type. It is the schema of your financial model — before you write a single journal entry, you must define what accounts exist and what they represent.

**For a lending/payments platform, a minimal CoA:**

```
ASSETS
  1001  Cash — Bank Settlement Account       (money received from gateway, pending disbursement)
  1002  Cash — Escrow Account                (funds held on behalf of merchants/customers)
  1010  Customer Wallet — {user_id}          (one account per customer, or pooled with subledger)
  1020  Merchant Wallet — {merchant_id}      (one account per merchant)
  1030  Receivables — Loan Principal         (amounts owed to the platform by borrowers)
  1031  Receivables — Interest               (interest accrued but not yet collected)
  1040  Suspense Account                     (temporary holding for unmatched transactions)

LIABILITIES
  2001  Payable — Customer Deposits          (platform owes this to customers — their wallet balance)
  2002  Payable — Merchant Settlements       (platform owes this to merchants — pending payouts)
  2010  Payable — Refunds                    (approved refunds not yet disbursed)
  2020  Tax Payable — GST                    (collected GST owed to the government)

EQUITY
  3001  Retained Earnings
  3002  Platform Capital

REVENUE
  4001  Fee Revenue — Transaction Fees       (charged per payment)
  4002  Fee Revenue — Platform Fees          (monthly / annual subscription)
  4003  Interest Income                      (earned from loans disbursed)

EXPENSES
  5001  Payment Processing Cost              (fees paid to gateway / network)
  5002  Bank Charges
  5003  Loan Loss Provision                  (expected credit losses)
```

**Design principles:**
- Start with the minimum set of accounts that models your actual money flows. Add accounts as new flow types emerge; don't pre-create accounts you don't need.
- Use a **subledger** pattern for high-cardinality accounts (one wallet per customer): one parent account (`1010 Customer Wallets`) in the CoA, with individual customer balances tracked in a subledger table keyed by `user_id`. The parent account's balance is the sum of all customer wallet balances.
- The **Suspense Account** is important: any transaction whose classification is uncertain goes to suspense immediately, with a follow-up process to reclassify it. Do not leave unclassified transactions as gaps in the ledger.

**Q6. What is a subledger, and when do you need one?**

A: A **subledger** is a subsidiary ledger that provides detail behind a single account in the general ledger. The general ledger account holds the aggregate total; the subledger holds the individual breakdown.

**When you need a subledger:** Any time a single logical account in your CoA must track balances for a large number of individual entities — customers, merchants, loans. Creating one general ledger account per customer (`1010-user_00001`, `1010-user_00002`, ...) would make the CoA unmanageable and balance sheet aggregation expensive. Instead:

- General ledger: `1010 Customer Wallets` — balance = total of all customer funds held.
- Subledger: `customer_wallet_entries` table — one row per transaction per customer, enabling per-customer balance lookup.

**The invariant:** `SUM(subledger balances) = general ledger account balance`. This must be true at all times and is verified by a reconciliation job.

**Schema:**

```sql
-- General ledger account (in accounts table): "1010 Customer Wallets"
-- Subledger table for per-customer detail
CREATE TABLE customer_wallet_entries (
    entry_id         UUID        PRIMARY KEY DEFAULT gen_random_uuid(),
    journal_entry_id UUID        NOT NULL REFERENCES journal_entries(journal_entry_id),
    user_id          UUID        NOT NULL REFERENCES users(user_id),
    entry_type       TEXT        NOT NULL CHECK (entry_type IN ('DEBIT', 'CREDIT')),
    amount_paise     BIGINT      NOT NULL CHECK (amount_paise > 0),
    balance_after    BIGINT      NOT NULL,   -- running balance for this user (denormalized for speed)
    created_at       TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

-- Reconciliation check: this should equal the general ledger account balance
SELECT SUM(
  CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END
)
FROM customer_wallet_entries;
```

**Subledger reconciliation job (runs every N minutes):**

```typescript
async function reconcileCustomerWallets(): Promise<void> {
  const [glBalance, slBalance] = await Promise.all([
    getGeneralLedgerBalance('1010'), // account_id for Customer Wallets
    db.raw(`
      SELECT SUM(
        CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END
      ) AS total FROM customer_wallet_entries
    `).then(r => BigInt(r.rows[0].total ?? 0)),
  ]);

  if (glBalance !== slBalance) {
    await alertingService.critical('SUBLEDGER_RECONCILIATION_FAILURE', {
      generalLedger: glBalance,
      subledger:     slBalance,
      diff:          glBalance - slBalance,
    });
  }
}
```

**Q7. What is the difference between a general ledger and a subledger, and what reconciliation discipline is required between them?**

A:

| Property | General Ledger (GL) | Subledger (SL) |
|---|---|---|
| **Granularity** | Account-level totals | Individual entity detail (per customer, per merchant) |
| **Rows** | One row per account | One row per transaction per entity |
| **Used for** | Balance sheet, P&L, trial balance | Customer statements, merchant payouts, dispute investigation |
| **Balance** | Aggregate (sum of all SL entries) | Per-entity running total |
| **Verification** | Trial balance: sum(debits) = sum(credits) | SL total must equal GL account balance |
| **Update frequency** | Every journal entry | Every journal entry (same transaction) |

**Reconciliation discipline:**

The GL and SL must be updated in the same database transaction. If they diverge, it means a partial write occurred — a bug that is extremely difficult to correct after the fact.

```typescript
async function recordPayment(payment: Payment): Promise<void> {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // 1. Create journal entry header
    const [journalEntry] = await trx('journal_entries').insert({
      description:    `Payment: ${payment.orderId}`,
      reference_id:   payment.paymentId,
      reference_type: 'PAYMENT',
    }).returning('*');

    // 2. General ledger entries (two sides of the double entry)
    await trx('ledger_entries').insert([
      {
        journal_entry_id: journalEntry.journal_entry_id,
        account_id:       ACCOUNTS.CUSTOMER_WALLETS,   // "1010"
        entry_type:       'CREDIT',                    // customer's asset decreases
        amount_paise:     payment.amountPaise,
      },
      {
        journal_entry_id: journalEntry.journal_entry_id,
        account_id:       ACCOUNTS.MERCHANT_WALLETS,   // "1020"
        entry_type:       'DEBIT',                     // merchant's asset increases
        amount_paise:     payment.amountPaise,
      },
    ]);

    // 3. Subledger entries (per-entity detail) — same transaction
    await trx('customer_wallet_entries').insert({
      journal_entry_id: journalEntry.journal_entry_id,
      user_id:          payment.customerId,
      entry_type:       'CREDIT',
      amount_paise:     payment.amountPaise,
      balance_after:    await getNewBalance(trx, payment.customerId, 'CREDIT', payment.amountPaise),
    });

    await trx('merchant_wallet_entries').insert({
      journal_entry_id: journalEntry.journal_entry_id,
      merchant_id:      payment.merchantId,
      entry_type:       'DEBIT',
      amount_paise:     payment.amountPaise,
      balance_after:    await getNewBalance(trx, payment.merchantId, 'DEBIT', payment.amountPaise),
    });
  });
  // If any step throws, the entire transaction rolls back — GL and SL stay in sync
}
```

## Section 3: Journal Entry Patterns for Common Payment Flows

**Q8. Walk through the journal entries for a customer paying a merchant on your platform.**

A: A payment from a customer to a merchant touches four accounts: the customer's wallet (asset), the merchant's wallet (asset), the platform's fee revenue (revenue), and the payment processing cost (expense).

**Scenario:** Customer pays ₹1,000.00 (100,000 paise) for a product. Platform charges a 2% transaction fee (2,000 paise). Gateway charges a 1.5% processing fee (1,500 paise).

**Step 1 — Customer initiates payment:**

```
Journal Entry: "Customer payment initiated"
Reference: payment_id = PAY-001

  DEBIT   Customer Wallet (1010)         100,000 paise  [customer's asset decreases]
  CREDIT  Merchant Payable (2002)         98,000 paise  [platform owes merchant after fee]
  CREDIT  Fee Revenue (4001)               2,000 paise  [platform earns transaction fee]

  Check: Debits (100,000) = Credits (98,000 + 2,000) ✓
```

**Step 2 — Platform pays gateway processing fee:**

```
Journal Entry: "Gateway processing fee"
Reference: payment_id = PAY-001, type = PROCESSING_FEE

  DEBIT   Payment Processing Cost (5001)   1,500 paise  [expense recognized]
  CREDIT  Bank Settlement Account (1001)   1,500 paise  [cash out to gateway]

  Check: Debits (1,500) = Credits (1,500) ✓
```

**Step 3 — Merchant settlement (payout):**

```
Journal Entry: "Merchant payout"
Reference: payout_id = PAYOUT-001

  DEBIT   Merchant Payable (2002)          98,000 paise  [liability reduced]
  CREDIT  Bank Settlement Account (1001)   98,000 paise  [cash out to merchant]

  Check: Debits (98,000) = Credits (98,000) ✓
```

**Net effect on the balance sheet:**
- Customer Wallet: −100,000 paise (customer's balance reduced).
- Merchant receives: 98,000 paise (in their bank account).
- Platform earns: 2,000 paise fee revenue, minus 1,500 paise processing cost = 500 paise net.
- Bank account: −1,500 (gateway fee) −98,000 (merchant payout) = −99,500 paise outflow, offset by the 100,000 paise collected from the customer's wallet.

**Q9. Walk through the journal entries for a refund.**

A: A refund reverses the original payment. It does not delete the original journal entries — it creates new, opposite entries. The audit trail shows both the original payment and the refund.

**Scenario:** Full refund of PAY-001 (100,000 paise). The platform absorbs the gateway processing fee (1,500 paise) — the merchant does not receive the fee back.

**Step 1 — Refund approved:**

```
Journal Entry: "Refund approved"
Reference: refund_id = REF-001

  DEBIT   Merchant Payable (2002)          98,000 paise  [reverse the payable to merchant]
  DEBIT   Fee Revenue (4001)                2,000 paise  [reverse the revenue earned]
  CREDIT  Customer Wallet (1010)          100,000 paise  [customer's balance restored]

  Check: Debits (98,000 + 2,000) = Credits (100,000) ✓
```

**Step 2 — Refund disbursed to customer:**

```
Journal Entry: "Refund disbursed"
Reference: refund_id = REF-001

  DEBIT   Refunds Payable (2010)          100,000 paise  [liability settled]
  CREDIT  Bank Settlement Account (1001)  100,000 paise  [cash out to customer]

  Check: Debits (100,000) = Credits (100,000) ✓
```

**What does NOT get reversed:** The gateway processing fee (1,500 paise) was already paid to the gateway. It remains as an expense. The net cost of the refund to the platform is 1,500 paise — the unrecoverable processing cost. Some platforms record this as a separate "refund cost" expense entry for cleaner P&L reporting.

**Important:** Never DELETE or UPDATE journal entries to process a refund. Append-only is a hard requirement. Every entry ever made must be readable in the audit log. Reversals are always new entries.

**Q10. Walk through the journal entries for a loan disbursement and repayment.**

A: A lending platform has additional account types: loan principal receivable (the platform lent money and expects it back), interest receivable (the fee for lending), and interest income (the revenue recognized when interest is collected).

**Scenario:** Platform disburses a ₹50,000 loan (5,000,000 paise) at 12% annual interest. Monthly EMI = ₹4,438 (443,800 paise) comprising ₹4,167 principal (416,700 paise) + ₹500 interest (50,000 paise), rounded per the earlier currency handling guide.

**Step 1 — Loan disbursement:**

```
Journal Entry: "Loan disbursed"
Reference: loan_id = LOAN-001

  DEBIT   Receivables — Loan Principal (1030)   5,000,000 paise  [asset: platform is owed this]
  CREDIT  Bank Settlement Account (1001)        5,000,000 paise  [cash out to borrower]

  Check: Debits = Credits ✓
```

**Step 2 — Interest accrual (end of month, before EMI):**

```
Journal Entry: "Interest accrual — Month 1"
Reference: loan_id = LOAN-001, period = 2024-01

  DEBIT   Receivables — Interest (1031)    50,000 paise  [asset: platform is owed interest]
  CREDIT  Interest Income (4003)           50,000 paise  [revenue recognized on accrual basis]

  Check: Debits = Credits ✓
```

**Step 3 — EMI received:**

```
Journal Entry: "EMI received — Month 1"
Reference: loan_id = LOAN-001, emi_number = 1

  DEBIT   Bank Settlement Account (1001)      443,800 paise  [cash in from borrower]
  CREDIT  Receivables — Loan Principal (1030) 393,800 paise  [principal portion reduces receivable]
  CREDIT  Receivables — Interest (1031)        50,000 paise  [interest portion clears accrued interest]

  Check: Debits (443,800) = Credits (393,800 + 50,000) ✓
```

Note: The principal portion (393,800) does not equal the EMI amount (443,800) minus the interest (50,000) = 393,800 ✓. The principal reduces each month as the outstanding balance decreases (reducing-balance loan).

**Step 4 — Loan loss provision (if EMI is missed):**

```
Journal Entry: "Loan loss provision — LOAN-001 overdue Day 30"
Reference: loan_id = LOAN-001, provision_date = 2024-02-30

  DEBIT   Loan Loss Provision Expense (5003)   443,800 paise  [expected loss recognized as expense]
  CREDIT  Loan Loss Provision — Reserve        443,800 paise  [contra-asset reduces receivable]

  Check: Debits = Credits ✓
```

**Q11. How do you handle a transaction that is received but not yet classified? What is a suspense account?**

A: A **suspense account** is a temporary holding account for transactions whose classification is uncertain or incomplete. It is the accounting equivalent of a processing queue — money enters the suspense account immediately (so the ledger stays balanced), and a follow-up process moves it to the correct account once the classification is determined.

**When suspense is needed:**
- An inbound bank transfer arrives with an amount but no matching order ID.
- A webhook from the payment gateway is received but the corresponding `payment_id` cannot be found in the database (race condition — the payment record hasn't been created yet).
- A currency conversion is pending — money has left the source currency account but hasn't yet arrived in the target currency account (float in transit).

**Suspense entry pattern:**

```
Journal Entry: "Unmatched inbound transfer"
Reference: bank_ref = NEFT-20240115-001

  DEBIT   Bank Settlement Account (1001)    500,000 paise  [cash received]
  CREDIT  Suspense Account (1040)           500,000 paise  [unclassified; pending investigation]

  Check: Debits = Credits ✓
```

**Resolution entry (once classified):**

```
Journal Entry: "Suspense resolved — matched to order ORD-999"
Reference: bank_ref = NEFT-20240115-001

  DEBIT   Suspense Account (1040)           500,000 paise  [clear suspense]
  CREDIT  Customer Wallet (1010)            500,000 paise  [credit the correct account]

  Check: Debits = Credits ✓
```

**Operational discipline for suspense:**
- The suspense account balance must be zero at the end of every business day. A non-zero suspense balance means unresolved items are aging.
- Alert if any suspense entry is older than 24 hours.
- Never leave money in suspense permanently — it masks errors and creates reconciliation failures.

## Section 4: Ledger Integrity & Invariants

**Q12. What database-level constraints enforce double-entry integrity, and what application-level checks supplement them?**

A: Integrity must be enforced in layers — the database is the last line of defense, but application-level checks catch problems earlier with better error messages.

**Database-level constraints:**

```sql
-- 1. Entry type must be DEBIT or CREDIT — no other values
ALTER TABLE ledger_entries
  ADD CONSTRAINT chk_entry_type CHECK (entry_type IN ('DEBIT', 'CREDIT'));

-- 2. Amount must be positive — direction is in entry_type, not the sign
ALTER TABLE ledger_entries
  ADD CONSTRAINT chk_amount_positive CHECK (amount_paise > 0);

-- 3. Journal entry must be balanced — enforced by a CHECK on an aggregate
--    (PostgreSQL doesn't support aggregate CHECKs natively; use a trigger instead)

-- Trigger: reject any ledger_entries INSERT/UPDATE that leaves the journal unbalanced
CREATE OR REPLACE FUNCTION enforce_journal_balance()
RETURNS TRIGGER AS $$
DECLARE
  debit_total  BIGINT;
  credit_total BIGINT;
BEGIN
  SELECT
    COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT'  THEN amount_paise END), 0),
    COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'CREDIT' THEN amount_paise END), 0)
  INTO debit_total, credit_total
  FROM ledger_entries
  WHERE journal_entry_id = NEW.journal_entry_id;

  -- Only enforce balance when the journal entry is explicitly "closed"
  -- (use a status column on journal_entries; trigger fires on status update)
  IF debit_total != credit_total THEN
    RAISE EXCEPTION 'Journal entry % is unbalanced: debits=% credits=%',
      NEW.journal_entry_id, debit_total, credit_total;
  END IF;

  RETURN NEW;
END;
$$ LANGUAGE plpgsql;

-- 4. Idempotency: one journal entry per business event
ALTER TABLE journal_entries
  ADD CONSTRAINT uq_journal_reference UNIQUE (reference_id, reference_type);

-- 5. Append-only enforcement (no updates or deletes on ledger_entries)
CREATE RULE no_update_ledger AS ON UPDATE TO ledger_entries DO INSTEAD NOTHING;
CREATE RULE no_delete_ledger AS ON DELETE TO ledger_entries DO INSTEAD NOTHING;
```

**Application-level checks (before DB write):**

```typescript
function validateJournalEntry(entries: LedgerEntry[]): void {
  // Must have at least 2 entries
  if (entries.length < 2) {
    throw new LedgerError('Journal entry must have at least 2 ledger lines');
  }

  // Sum of debits must equal sum of credits
  const debitTotal  = entries
    .filter(e => e.entryType === 'DEBIT')
    .reduce((sum, e) => sum + e.amountPaise, 0n);
  const creditTotal = entries
    .filter(e => e.entryType === 'CREDIT')
    .reduce((sum, e) => sum + e.amountPaise, 0n);

  if (debitTotal !== creditTotal) {
    throw new LedgerError(
      `Unbalanced journal entry: debits=${debitTotal} credits=${creditTotal}`
    );
  }

  // All entries must be same currency
  const currencies = new Set(entries.map(e => e.currency));
  if (currencies.size > 1) {
    throw new LedgerError('Multi-currency journal entries require an FX conversion entry');
  }

  // No zero-amount entries
  if (entries.some(e => e.amountPaise === 0n)) {
    throw new LedgerError('Journal entry lines must have non-zero amounts');
  }
}
```

**Q13. What is the trial balance, and how do you run one programmatically?**

A: The **trial balance** is a summary report that lists all accounts and their net balances, with total debits and total credits at the bottom. If the totals are equal, the ledger is arithmetically correct (though not necessarily free of errors — see Q14). A trial balance is the first thing an auditor looks at and the first diagnostic to run when investigating a discrepancy.

**Trial balance query:**

```sql
SELECT
    a.account_id,
    a.name,
    a.account_type,
    a.normal_balance,
    COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN le.entry_type = 'DEBIT'  THEN le.amount_paise ELSE 0 END), 0) AS total_debits,
    COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN le.entry_type = 'CREDIT' THEN le.amount_paise ELSE 0 END), 0) AS total_credits,
    COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN le.entry_type = 'DEBIT'  THEN le.amount_paise
                      WHEN le.entry_type = 'CREDIT' THEN -le.amount_paise END), 0)        AS net_balance
FROM accounts a
LEFT JOIN ledger_entries le ON a.account_id = le.account_id
GROUP BY a.account_id, a.name, a.account_type, a.normal_balance
ORDER BY a.account_id;

-- Footer check (must be 0)
SELECT
  SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT'  THEN  amount_paise
           WHEN entry_type = 'CREDIT' THEN -amount_paise END) AS grand_total
FROM ledger_entries;
-- grand_total = 0 → ledger is balanced ✓
-- grand_total ≠ 0 → error exists
```

**Programmatic trial balance in TypeScript:**

```typescript
interface TrialBalanceLine {

}

  `, [asOf ?? new Date()]);
```

Run the trial balance:
- At the end of every business day (scheduled job).
- Before and after any bulk migration or data correction.
- As part of CI on a test ledger with known transactions (regression test that the ledger invariant holds).

**Q14. What types of errors does a balanced trial balance NOT catch?**

A: A trial balance proves arithmetic correctness — that the sum of debits equals the sum of credits. It does not prove semantic correctness. The following error types produce a balanced trial balance despite being wrong:

| Error type | Example | Why trial balance misses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Compensating errors** | Two entries each wrong by ₹1,000 in opposite directions | The errors cancel; the total is still balanced |
| **Wrong account** | Fee revenue posted to interest income | Both are credit-normal accounts; balance is unaffected |
| **Duplicated entry** | Same payment journalized twice | Both the duplicate debit and credit are present; balance holds |
| **Omitted entry** | A fee expense never recorded | Both the debit and credit are absent; balance holds |
| **Amount transposition** | ₹1,998 entered as ₹1,989 | If both sides use the same wrong amount, balance holds |
| **Wrong period** | December revenue posted in January | Amounts are correct; only the timestamp is wrong |

**Defenses against these errors:**

- **Idempotency constraint** (`UNIQUE (reference_id, reference_type)`) catches duplicate journal entries.
- **Reconciliation against external sources** (gateway settlement reports, bank statements) catches omitted entries and wrong amounts.
- **Account-type validation** at the application layer catches wrong-account entries (e.g., reject posting a revenue entry to an asset account without explicit override).
- **Period-close controls** (locking entries before a cutoff date) catch wrong-period entries.
- **Automated reconciliation jobs** that verify subledger totals against GL balances catch omissions and compensating errors over time.

**Q15. What is an immutable ledger, and how do you implement one in PostgreSQL?**

A: An **immutable ledger** is one where entries are never updated or deleted — only appended. Every correction is a new entry, not a modification of an existing one. Immutability is a regulatory requirement in financial systems (audit trail must be complete and tamper-evident) and a safety property (no bug or operator error can silently alter historical records).

**Enforcement at multiple layers:**

**1. PostgreSQL row-level security / rules:**
```sql
-- Prevent updates and deletes at the DB level
CREATE RULE no_update_ledger_entries
  AS ON UPDATE TO ledger_entries DO INSTEAD
  RAISE EXCEPTION 'Ledger entries are immutable — use a reversal entry instead';

CREATE RULE no_delete_ledger_entries
  AS ON DELETE TO ledger_entries DO INSTEAD
  RAISE EXCEPTION 'Ledger entries cannot be deleted';
```

**2. Application-layer enforcement:**
```typescript
// The ledger service exposes only append operations
class LedgerService {
  async appendJournalEntry(entry: NewJournalEntry): Promise<JournalEntry> {
    validateJournalEntry(entry.lines);
    return db.transaction(async (trx) => {
      // ... insert journal_entries and ledger_entries
    });
  }

  // No update() or delete() methods exist
}
```

**3. Cryptographic integrity (for high-assurance systems):**
```sql
-- Each entry's hash includes the previous entry's hash — a hash chain
ALTER TABLE ledger_entries ADD COLUMN entry_hash TEXT;

-- entry_hash = SHA256(previous_hash || journal_entry_id || account_id || entry_type || amount || created_at)
-- Computed and stored at insert time by the application
-- Verification: recompute the chain and compare; any modification breaks the chain
```

**4. Database-level audit via WAL:**
- Enable PostgreSQL's logical replication / WAL archiving — every change to every table is recorded in the write-ahead log, which is itself immutable (rotated but not editable).
- Ship WAL to an immutable store (S3 with object lock) for long-term audit evidence.

**Correction pattern (when an error is found):**
```
WRONG:  UPDATE ledger_entries SET amount_paise = 50000 WHERE entry_id = 'abc';

RIGHT:
  1. Void entry: create a new journal entry that exactly reverses the wrong entry.
  2. Correction entry: create a new journal entry with the correct amounts.
  3. Both the original, the void, and the correction remain in the ledger permanently.
  4. Add a `voided_by` reference on the original entry (but do not change its values).
```

## Section 5: Balance Integrity & Negative Balance Prevention

**Q16. How do you prevent a negative balance in a wallet while maintaining double-entry integrity?**

A: Preventing overdraft (negative balance) requires a check-before-write pattern with concurrency control — the balance check and the debit must be atomic, or two concurrent debits can both pass the check and together overdraw the account.

**Pattern — SELECT FOR UPDATE (pessimistic lock):**

```typescript
async function debitWallet(
  userId: string,
  amountPaise: bigint,
  journalEntry: NewJournalEntry,
): Promise<void> {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // 1. Lock the user's most recent subledger entry — prevents concurrent debits
    const latest = await trx.raw(`
      SELECT balance_after
      FROM customer_wallet_entries
      WHERE user_id = $1
      ORDER BY created_at DESC
      LIMIT 1
      FOR UPDATE
    `, [userId]);

    const currentBalance = BigInt(latest.rows[0]?.balance_after ?? 0);

    if (currentBalance < amountPaise) {
      throw new InsufficientFundsError(
        `Balance ${currentBalance} paise is less than debit ${amountPaise} paise`
      );
    }

    const newBalance = currentBalance - amountPaise;

    // 2. Write journal entry (GL) and subledger entry atomically
    await appendJournalEntryInTrx(trx, journalEntry);
    await trx('customer_wallet_entries').insert({
      journal_entry_id: journalEntry.journalEntryId,
      user_id:          userId,
      entry_type:       'CREDIT',  // credit reduces the asset (wallet)
      amount_paise:     amountPaise,
      balance_after:    newBalance,
    });
  });
}
```

**Pattern — Optimistic locking (higher throughput):**

```typescript
async function debitWalletOptimistic(
  userId: string,
  amountPaise: bigint,
  expectedVersion: number, // current version number from a prior read
): Promise<void> {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // Update only if version matches — otherwise, a concurrent transaction won
    const result = await trx.raw(`
      UPDATE wallet_balances
      SET balance_paise = balance_paise - $1,
          version       = version + 1
      WHERE user_id = $2
        AND version   = $3
        AND balance_paise >= $1
      RETURNING balance_paise
    `, [amountPaise, userId, expectedVersion]);

    if (result.rowCount === 0) {
      throw new ConcurrentModificationError(
        'Balance changed concurrently or insufficient funds — retry'
      );
    }
  });
}
```

**The double-entry constraint:** The debit to the customer wallet must always be accompanied by a credit to another account (merchant wallet, suspense, or fee revenue) in the same transaction. The balance check and the double-entry write are a single atomic unit — you never write half a journal entry.

**Q17. How do you compute a wallet balance efficiently? Running balance vs. sum-on-read tradeoffs.**

A:

**Option A — Sum-on-read:**
```sql
SELECT
  SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END) AS balance
FROM customer_wallet_entries
WHERE user_id = $1;
```
- Always correct — reflects every entry ever made.
- Slow at scale: O(n) where n = number of entries for the user (a user with 5 years of daily transactions has ~1,825 rows; a trading platform user may have millions).
- No denormalization needed.

**Option B — Running balance (denormalized):**
```sql
-- balance_after is stored on each entry (as in the schema above)
SELECT balance_after
FROM customer_wallet_entries
WHERE user_id = $1
ORDER BY created_at DESC
LIMIT 1;
```
- O(1) — one index lookup.
- Risk: if `balance_after` is ever written incorrectly, the stored balance is wrong (independent of the actual entries).
- Requires the `balance_after` to be computed and written atomically with the entry.

**Option C — Materialized balance with periodic verification:**
```sql
CREATE TABLE wallet_balances (
    user_id          UUID    PRIMARY KEY,
    balance_paise    BIGINT  NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
    last_entry_id    UUID    REFERENCES customer_wallet_entries(entry_id),
    version          INT     NOT NULL DEFAULT 0
);
```
- The `wallet_balances` row is updated atomically with every entry.
- A periodic job verifies: `wallet_balances.balance_paise = SUM(entries)` for a random sample of users. Discrepancies trigger an alert and a recomputation from the source entries.
- Best for high-read, high-write scenarios.

**Recommendation:** Use Option B (running balance on subledger entries) for most systems. The `balance_after` is written in the same transaction as the entry — it is as reliable as the entry itself. Back it with a nightly reconciliation that spot-checks `balance_after` against the computed sum for a random sample of accounts.

## Section 6: System Design Scenario Questions

**Q18. "Design the ledger for a peer-to-peer transfer between two users on your platform."**

A: A P2P transfer moves money from one user's wallet to another. In double-entry terms, it is a debit to the sender's wallet and a credit to the receiver's wallet — two subledger entries and two GL entries, all in one journal entry.

**Journal entry:**

```
Journal Entry: "P2P Transfer"
Reference: transfer_id = TRF-001

  DEBIT   Customer Wallet (1010) [sender: user_A]     50,000 paise
  CREDIT  Customer Wallet (1010) [receiver: user_B]   50,000 paise

  Check: Debits (50,000) = Credits (50,000) ✓
```

Note: Both entries touch the same GL account (`1010 Customer Wallets`) but different subledger entries. The GL account balance is unchanged (money moved between two users but stayed within the platform). The subledger entries show user_A decreasing and user_B increasing by the same amount.

**Implementation with concurrent safety:**

```typescript
async function executeP2PTransfer(
  senderId:    string,
  receiverId:  string,
  amountPaise: bigint,
  transferId:  string,
): Promise<void> {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // Lock both accounts in a consistent order (lower UUID first) to prevent deadlock
    const [firstId, secondId] = [senderId, receiverId].sort();
    await trx.raw(
      `SELECT balance_after FROM customer_wallet_entries
       WHERE user_id IN ($1, $2) ORDER BY user_id DESC, created_at DESC
       LIMIT 2 FOR UPDATE`,
      [firstId, secondId]
    );

    const senderBalance = await getCurrentBalance(trx, senderId);
    if (senderBalance < amountPaise) {
      throw new InsufficientFundsError();
    }

    // Journal entry header (idempotency: one per transfer_id)
    const [je] = await trx('journal_entries').insert({
      description:    `P2P Transfer from ${senderId} to ${receiverId}`,
      reference_id:   transferId,
      reference_type: 'P2P_TRANSFER',
    }).returning('*');

    // GL entries
    await trx('ledger_entries').insert([
      { journal_entry_id: je.journal_entry_id, account_id: ACCOUNTS.CUSTOMER_WALLETS, entry_type: 'DEBIT',  amount_paise: amountPaise },
      { journal_entry_id: je.journal_entry_id, account_id: ACCOUNTS.CUSTOMER_WALLETS, entry_type: 'CREDIT', amount_paise: amountPaise },
    ]);

    // Subledger entries (per-user detail)
    const senderNewBalance   = senderBalance - amountPaise;
    const receiverBalance    = await getCurrentBalance(trx, receiverId);
    const receiverNewBalance = receiverBalance + amountPaise;

    await trx('customer_wallet_entries').insert([
      { journal_entry_id: je.journal_entry_id, user_id: senderId,   entry_type: 'CREDIT', amount_paise: amountPaise, balance_after: senderNewBalance },
      { journal_entry_id: je.journal_entry_id, user_id: receiverId, entry_type: 'DEBIT',  amount_paise: amountPaise, balance_after: receiverNewBalance },
    ]);
  });
}
```

**Deadlock prevention:** Always acquire row locks in a consistent order (sorted by ID). If transaction T1 locks user_A then user_B, and T2 locks user_B then user_A, a deadlock is possible. Sorting ensures both transactions lock in the same order, eliminating the cycle.

**Q19. "Your ledger shows a ₹50,000 discrepancy between the GL and the subledger. How do you diagnose and fix it?"**

A: A GL vs. subledger discrepancy means the general ledger account balance does not equal the sum of individual subledger entries. Structured diagnosis:

**Phase 1 — Quantify and localize:**

```sql
-- Compute GL balance for the Customer Wallets account
SELECT
  COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END), 0)
  AS gl_balance
FROM ledger_entries
WHERE account_id = '1010';

-- Compute subledger balance
SELECT
  COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END), 0)
  AS sl_balance
FROM customer_wallet_entries;

-- Identify the first point where they diverge (binary search by date range)
SELECT
  date_trunc('day', created_at) AS day,
  SUM(CASE WHEN entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN amount_paise ELSE -amount_paise END) AS net
FROM customer_wallet_entries
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;
-- Compare this cumulative sum to the GL's daily net to find the day the divergence started
```

**Phase 2 — Find the orphaned or missing entries:**

```sql
-- Find GL entries with no corresponding subledger entry (or vice versa)
SELECT le.journal_entry_id, le.entry_type, le.amount_paise
FROM ledger_entries le
WHERE le.account_id = '1010'
  AND NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1 FROM customer_wallet_entries cwe
    WHERE cwe.journal_entry_id = le.journal_entry_id
  );
```

**Phase 3 — Root cause categories:**

| Finding | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| GL entry exists, no subledger entry | Code path that wrote GL entries but crashed before subledger write (partial transaction rollback failure) |
| Subledger entry exists, no GL entry | Code path that wrote to subledger only (missing double-entry discipline in a new feature) |
| Both exist but amounts differ | A bug that computed amounts differently for GL vs. subledger |
| Duplicate subledger entry | Idempotency failure — same journal entry written twice to subledger |

**Phase 4 — Correct without modifying existing entries:**

```sql
-- Correction: add the missing subledger entry as a new row
-- (do NOT update or delete any existing rows)
INSERT INTO customer_wallet_entries (
  journal_entry_id, user_id, entry_type, amount_paise, balance_after, created_at
)
SELECT
  le.journal_entry_id,
  <correct_user_id>,  -- determined from the payment record
  le.entry_type,
  le.amount_paise,
  <recomputed_balance_after>,
  le.created_at
FROM ledger_entries le
WHERE le.entry_id = '<orphaned_gl_entry_id>';
```

**Phase 5 — Prevent recurrence:**
- Add the reconciliation job from Q6 that runs every N minutes and alerts on any GL/SL divergence above a threshold.
- Add an integration test that writes a payment through the full stack and then asserts `gl_balance = sl_balance`.
- Add the DB trigger from Q12 that rejects unbalanced journal entries at the DB layer.

**Q20. "A new engineer proposes using a single `balance` column on the users table instead of a ledger. How do you respond?"**

A: Don't dismiss it outright — for a prototype or an internal tool with no financial consequences, a balance column is fine. But for a production payment system, it fails on correctness, auditability, and recoverability. Walk through each dimension:

**Correctness failure — no atomicity guarantee across users:**

A P2P transfer needs to atomically decrement user A's balance and increment user B's. With two separate `UPDATE` statements, a crash between them leaves the system in an inconsistent state — money subtracted from A but not added to B (or vice versa). A journal entry with two ledger lines in a single transaction is atomic; two balance column updates are not (even in a transaction, they're separate operations with a window of inconsistency in the application code).

**Auditability failure — no history:**

A balance column tells you the current balance. It tells you nothing about how the balance got there. When a customer disputes a charge ("I never authorized that ₹5,000 deduction"), you have no evidence: the balance column shows ₹95,000 but has no record of the ₹5,000 change. A ledger shows every entry, every journal reference, every timestamp, and every counterpart account. Regulators and auditors require this.

**Recoverability failure — no self-verification:**

If a bug corrupts a balance column (e.g., a float rounding error writes ₹999.99 instead of ₹1,000), there is no way to detect it without an external reference. A double-entry ledger self-verifies: the trial balance must sum to zero. Any corruption or missing entry is immediately detectable.

**Concurrency failure — race conditions:**

```javascript
// Race condition with balance columns — classic read-modify-write:
const user = await db.findUser(id);         // reads balance = 1000
// Another request reads balance = 1000 here
user.balance -= 500;
await db.saveUser(user);                    // writes 500
// Other request also writes 500 — both debits of 500 produce balance = 500, not 0
```

A ledger with `SELECT FOR UPDATE` or optimistic locking eliminates this race.

**The right response:** "A balance column is a cache of the ledger — it's fine as a read optimization if backed by a proper ledger. What we should not do is use it as the source of truth. I'd propose a ledger as the source of truth, with an optional materialized balance column for read performance, verified periodically against the ledger sum."

**Q21. "How would you migrate an existing system that uses balance columns to a double-entry ledger without downtime?"**

A: This is a multi-phase migration that takes weeks, not days. The core challenge is that the old system and new system must be consistent at every point — there is no acceptable window where both are partially active.

**Phase 1 — Dual-write (no downtime, no risk):**

Deploy code that writes to both the old balance column AND the new ledger on every transaction. The old system remains the source of truth. The ledger is write-only — no reads yet.

```typescript
async function updateBalance(userId: string, delta: bigint, type: 'DEBIT' | 'CREDIT') {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // Old path: update balance column
    await trx('users').where({ user_id: userId })
      .increment('balance_paise', type === 'DEBIT' ? Number(delta) : -Number(delta));

    // New path: append to ledger (best-effort; don't fail the transaction if ledger write fails)
    await appendLedgerEntry(trx, userId, delta, type).catch(err => {
      logger.error({ err, userId }, 'Ledger write failed — old system remains source of truth');
    });
  });
}
```

**Phase 2 — Backfill (offline):**

Generate historical ledger entries from existing transaction records (payment history, refund history, transfer history). The goal is a ledger that, when summed, produces balances matching the current `balance_paise` column for every user.

```sql
-- Verify backfill completeness
SELECT
  u.user_id,
  u.balance_paise AS old_balance,
  COALESCE(SUM(CASE WHEN cwe.entry_type = 'DEBIT' THEN cwe.amount_paise ELSE -cwe.amount_paise END), 0)
    AS new_ledger_balance,
  u.balance_paise - COALESCE(SUM(...), 0) AS diff
FROM users u
LEFT JOIN customer_wallet_entries cwe USING (user_id)
GROUP BY u.user_id, u.balance_paise
HAVING u.balance_paise != COALESCE(SUM(...), 0);
-- Zero rows = backfill complete and consistent
```

**Phase 3 — Shadow read (validation, no user impact):**

Switch read paths to fetch balance from the ledger but compare against the old column before returning. Log discrepancies; do not surface them to users yet.

**Phase 4 — Ledger as source of truth:**

Once discrepancies have been zero for N consecutive days:
- Switch all read paths to use the ledger balance.
- Make balance column writes best-effort (or drop them).
- Deprecate the balance column.

**Phase 5 — Cleanup:**

After a defined retention period, remove the balance column. Keep the migration code in a git branch for reference.

**Key principle:** Never migrate by copying data in a one-time batch and switching over. Always dual-write, verify, then switch reads. This gives you a rollback path at every stage.

## Quick Reference Table

| Concept | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Double-entry** | Every transaction = one debit + one equal credit | Self-verifying; no money created or destroyed |
| **Debit** | Left side of T-account; increases assets/expenses | Direction, not value judgment |
| **Credit** | Right side of T-account; increases liabilities/equity/revenue | Opposite of colloquial "credit" for asset accounts |
| **Journal entry** | Atomic group of ≥2 ledger lines that balance | Unit of audit; must be immutable once posted |
| **Ledger entry** | Single debit or credit line within a journal entry | Append-only; never updated or deleted |
| **Trial balance** | Sum of all debits vs. sum of all credits | Must equal zero; proves arithmetic integrity |
| **Chart of accounts** | Named list of all accounts by type | Schema of the financial model |
| **Subledger** | Per-entity detail behind a GL account | Enables per-customer/merchant balance without bloating GL |
| **Suspense account** | Temporary holding for unclassified transactions | Keeps ledger balanced during classification lag |
| **Immutable ledger** | No UPDATE or DELETE on posted entries | Audit trail; corrections via reversal entries only |
| **Reconciliation** | GL balance = SUM(subledger entries) | Detects partial writes, bugs, missing entries |
| **Running balance** | `balance_after` stored on each entry | O(1) balance lookup; verified against sum periodically |

---

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level in fintech, double-entry questions surface in three forms: (1) "design a wallet system" — the interviewer expects you to immediately propose a ledger table, not a balance column, and to mention that every debit needs a credit; (2) "how do you handle a refund without modifying historical records" — the answer is a reversal journal entry, never a DELETE or UPDATE; (3) "how do you detect if money has been lost in your system" — the answer is a trial balance query that must always sum to zero. Know the five account types and their normal balances cold (asset/debit, liability/credit, equity/credit, revenue/credit, expense/debit). Know the suspense account pattern — it comes up whenever a payment webhook arrives before the order record exists. And always anchor the design decision to its consequence: a balance column is not wrong because it's inelegant; it's wrong because it cannot prove it's correct, cannot answer "where did this ₹50,000 go," and cannot recover from a partial write.
