# Payment Lifecycle: India Payment Rails — IMPS, NEFT, RTGS & UPI — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

> **Domain:** Fintech / Payment Systems Architecture (India)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals & Rail Overview

**Q1. What are payment rails, and why does India have four of them instead of one?**

A: A **payment rail** is the underlying infrastructure and protocol that moves money between two bank accounts. It defines the settlement mechanism (real-time vs. batch), the settlement finality (when the recipient can spend the money), the transaction limits, the operating hours, and the fee structure.

India has four major electronic payment rails — NEFT, RTGS, IMPS, and UPI — because they were built at different times for different use cases, and each solved a specific gap at the time of its introduction:

- **NEFT (2005):** Replaced paper cheques and demand drafts for retail inter-bank transfers. Batch-based, deferred settlement. Originally operated in 11 hourly batches on weekdays; became 24×7 in December 2019.
- **RTGS (2004):** Built for high-value, time-critical transfers (corporate treasury, real estate, large B2B payments). Real-time gross settlement — each transaction settles individually and immediately in RBI's books.
- **IMPS (2010):** Filled the gap that NEFT left — instant, 24×7 inter-bank transfers for retail use cases. Operated by NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India). The first truly always-on retail rail.
- **UPI (2016):** Built on top of IMPS settlement but adds a virtual address layer (VPA / UPI ID), a standardized API, and a mobile-first flow. Eliminated the need to know the beneficiary's bank, IFSC, and account number — a phone number or UPI ID is sufficient.

The rails are complementary, not competing (except at the margin between IMPS and UPI). A fintech engineer must know which rail to use for which use case, what happens when a rail is unavailable, and how each rail's settlement mechanics affect reconciliation.

---

**Q2. Who operates each rail, and what is NPCI's role?**

A:

| Rail | Operated by | Launched | Settlement type |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NEFT** | Reserve Bank of India (RBI) | November 2005 | Net settlement (deferred) |
| **RTGS** | Reserve Bank of India (RBI) | March 2004 | Gross settlement (real-time) |
| **IMPS** | NPCI (National Payments Corporation of India) | November 2010 | Real-time, 24×7 |
| **UPI** | NPCI | April 2016 | Real-time, 24×7 (settles via IMPS infrastructure) |

**NPCI's role:** NPCI is a not-for-profit organization promoted by RBI and the Indian Banks' Association (IBA). It owns and operates IMPS, UPI, RuPay (card network), NACH (National Automated Clearing House for mandates), FASTag (toll payments), BBPS (bill payments), and AePS (Aadhaar-enabled payment system). NPCI is the infrastructure layer for the retail payments ecosystem in India — equivalent in scope to what Visa/Mastercard are for cards, but for bank-account-based rails.

RBI operates NEFT and RTGS directly because they involve settlement in central bank money (RBI's own books) — the highest form of settlement finality. NPCI rails settle through commercial bank accounts at RBI, which is one step removed but still considered final for practical purposes.

---

**Q3. What is the difference between real-time settlement and net settlement, and why does it matter for a fintech?**

A: **Real-time gross settlement (RTGS):** Each transaction is settled individually and immediately in the central bank's books the moment it is processed. There is no netting — if ₹1 crore flows from Bank A to Bank B and ₹80 lakh flows from Bank B to Bank A in the same minute, both transactions settle in full independently. RTGS requires the sending bank to have sufficient balance in its RBI current account at the moment of transaction.

**Net settlement (NEFT, batch):** Transactions are accumulated over a period (originally 1-hour batches; now continuous with near-real-time multilateral netting). At the settlement moment, all debits and credits across all banks are netted — Bank A's net position against all other banks is calculated, and only the net amount moves. This is capital-efficient (banks don't need to pre-fund the gross amount) but introduces settlement risk: if a bank's net position is a large debit and it cannot fund it, the entire batch may be delayed.

**Why it matters for fintech:**

- **Credit risk:** On NEFT, the beneficiary's bank shows the credit before final settlement (they show it to the customer as "available"). If the sending bank defaults before the batch settles, the beneficiary's bank absorbs the loss. This is called **settlement risk** and is why large-value transfers use RTGS.
- **Reconciliation timing:** On NEFT/IMPS, the credit appears in the beneficiary's account almost immediately, but your ledger must reflect the settlement status accurately. A payment that shows "credited" to the user before your bank confirms final settlement should be flagged as `PENDING_SETTLEMENT` in your ledger, not `COMPLETED`.
- **Float:** In batch settlement, there is a window between when money leaves the sender's account and when it arrives in the receiver's account. Some fintech business models (lending, float-based revenue) depend on this window.

## Section 2: NEFT — National Electronic Funds Transfer

**Q4. How does NEFT work technically? Walk through a transaction end-to-end.**

A: NEFT uses a **multilateral net settlement** model operated by RBI. Since December 2019, it operates on a continuous basis — settlement happens in 48 half-hourly batches across 24 hours, every day of the year.

**End-to-end flow:**

```
Step 1 — Origination
  Customer initiates transfer via bank's netbanking/mobile app
  OR your platform initiates a payout via bank's NEFT API
  Originating bank validates:
    - IFSC code (11 characters: bank code + branch code)
    - Account number format
    - Transaction limit (no upper limit on NEFT; no lower limit either)
    - Sender's available balance

Step 2 — Submission to RBI
  Originating bank formats the transaction as an NEFT message
  (based on ISO 8583 or bank-specific proprietary format)
  Bank submits to RBI's NEFT processing center before the next batch cutoff

Step 3 — Netting at RBI
  RBI aggregates all inbound NEFT messages from all member banks
  Computes each bank's net position:
    Net position = SUM(all credits to be received) - SUM(all debits to be paid)
  Banks with a net debit position must have sufficient funds in their RBI current account

Step 4 — Settlement
  RBI debits/credits each bank's RBI current account by their net position
  This is final and irrevocable — settlement in central bank money

Step 5 — Credit to Beneficiary
  Beneficiary's bank receives the credit advice from RBI
  Bank credits the beneficiary's account
  Bank sends credit notification (SMS/email) to beneficiary
  Timeline: same batch if submitted before cutoff; next batch otherwise
  Practical time: 30 minutes to 2 hours from initiation to credit
```

**IFSC Code structure:**

```
HDFC0001234
│   │   │
│   │   └── Branch code (last 6 characters; branch-specific)
│   └─────── Literal "0" (reserved; always 0)
└─────────── Bank code (first 4 characters; HDFC = HDFC Bank)
```

IFSC is used to route the payment to the correct bank and branch in the NEFT system. Your platform must validate IFSC codes before submitting NEFT payouts — an invalid IFSC results in a transaction rejection and a delay of up to one settlement cycle.

---

**Q5. What are NEFT's transaction limits, operating hours, and failure modes?**

A:

**Limits:**
- **Minimum:** No lower limit.
- **Maximum:** No upper limit per transaction for individuals; banks may impose their own per-transaction and per-day limits.
- **Batch frequency:** 48 batches per day (every 30 minutes), 24×7×365 since December 2019.

**Failure modes and return codes:**

| Return reason | Code (example) | Your action |
|---|---|---|
| Invalid IFSC | `R01` | Reject at submission; prompt user to verify IFSC |
| Invalid account number | `R02` | Reject; prompt user |
| Account closed | `R03` | Mark recipient inactive; notify sender; refund |
| Account frozen / inoperative | `R05` | Return funds to sender; notify |
| Beneficiary bank not reachable | `R10` | Retry in next batch; escalate if persistent |
| Beneficiary deceased | `R14` | Return funds; flag for manual review |
| Credit refused by beneficiary bank | `R16` | Return funds; contact beneficiary bank |

**NEFT return handling in your system:**

```typescript
interface NEFTReturnEvent {
  originalTxnRef:  string;  // your internal transaction ID
  returnReason:    string;  // R01, R02, R03, etc.
  returnAmount:    bigint;  // paise — should equal original amount
  returnTimestamp: Date;
}

async function handleNEFTReturn(event: NEFTReturnEvent): Promise<void> {
  await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
    // 1. Find the original payout
    const payout = await trx('payouts')
      .where({ bank_ref: event.originalTxnRef })
      .first();

    if (!payout) throw new Error(`Unknown NEFT return: ${event.originalTxnRef}`);

    // 2. Update payout status
    await trx('payouts').where({ payout_id: payout.payout_id })
      .update({ status: 'RETURNED', return_reason: event.returnReason, returned_at: event.returnTimestamp });

    // 3. Reverse the ledger entry (double-entry: credit back the sender's account)
    await appendReversalJournalEntry(trx, payout, event.returnReason);

    // 4. Notify the sender
    await notificationQueue.enqueue('PAYOUT_RETURNED', {
      userId:      payout.user_id,
      amount:      event.returnAmount,
      reason:      NEFT_RETURN_REASONS[event.returnReason] ?? 'Bank returned the transfer',
    });
  });
}
```

**Q6. When should you use NEFT over IMPS or UPI for payouts?**

A: NEFT is preferred in specific scenarios where its characteristics are advantageous:

- **Cost:** NEFT is free for customers (RBI removed charges in 2019). For high-volume payout platforms (salary disbursement, insurance claims), NEFT's lower bank-pass-through cost (compared to IMPS) makes it attractive.
- **No upper limit:** NEFT has no maximum transaction limit, making it suitable for large-value retail payouts (₹10L+) that exceed IMPS limits.
- **Broad bank coverage:** Every scheduled commercial bank in India is on NEFT. Some smaller cooperative banks may not be on IMPS. If your payout destination is an obscure bank, NEFT is the fallback.
- **Batch payouts:** If your use case is overnight salary disbursement (where 30-minute delay is acceptable), NEFT's batch model matches the use case and is often simpler to integrate with payroll systems.

**Avoid NEFT when:**
- The user expects instant confirmation (e-commerce refund, wallet load).
- The amount is time-critical (intraday trading margin, real-time lending disbursement).
- The use case is consumer-facing — users expect UPI/IMPS speed.

## Section 3: RTGS — Real Time Gross Settlement

**Q7. How does RTGS differ from NEFT at the settlement mechanics level?**

A: RTGS settles each transaction individually and immediately in RBI's central bank accounts — there is no batching, no netting, and no wait for other transactions. The moment RBI processes an RTGS instruction, the funds move from the originating bank's RBI current account to the beneficiary bank's RBI current account. This is irrevocable.

**Comparison of settlement mechanics:**

| Property | NEFT | RTGS |
|---|---|---|
| Settlement timing | Every 30 minutes (net) | Immediately (gross) |
| Netting | Yes — banks' net positions settle | No — each transaction settles in full |
| Capital requirement | Net position only | Full gross amount must be pre-funded |
| Intraday liquidity | Banks can temporarily go net debit | Banks must maintain positive balance at all times |
| Finality | At settlement batch completion | Immediate — moment RBI processes it |
| Systemic risk | Higher (settlement risk in batch) | Lower (no accumulation of unsettled transactions) |

**RTGS operating hours:**

Since December 2020, RTGS operates 24×7 — making it the only central-bank-operated real-time gross settlement system that runs around the clock (most countries' RTGS systems have cutoff windows). This enables large-value transactions at any hour, including weekends and holidays.

**Transaction limits:**
- **Minimum:** ₹2,00,000 (₹2 lakh). Transactions below ₹2L are rejected — use NEFT or IMPS instead.
- **Maximum:** No regulatory upper limit. Banks may impose limits.

**Q8. When should a fintech engineer route a payment over RTGS vs. NEFT vs. IMPS?**

A: Rail selection logic is a decision tree based on amount, urgency, and recipient bank capability:

```typescript
type Rail = 'RTGS' | 'NEFT' | 'IMPS' | 'UPI';

function selectPaymentRail(params: {
  amountPaise:          bigint;
  isUrgent:             boolean;
  hasUPIHandle:         boolean;
  beneficiaryBankIMPS:  boolean;
  beneficiaryBankUPI:   boolean;
}): Rail {
  const { amountPaise, isUrgent, hasUPIHandle, beneficiaryBankIMPS, beneficiaryBankUPI } = params;

  const IMPS_LIMIT_PAISE  = 500_000_00n;  // ₹5,00,000 (NPCI limit; banks may set lower)
  const RTGS_MIN_PAISE    = 200_000_00n;  // ₹2,00,000

  // UPI: fastest for consumer use cases, widely supported
  if (hasUPIHandle && beneficiaryBankUPI && amountPaise <= 100_000_00n) {
    // UPI limit: ₹1,00,000 per transaction (some banks allow ₹2L for verified accounts)
    return 'UPI';
  }

  // RTGS: large value, always urgent
  if (amountPaise >= RTGS_MIN_PAISE && isUrgent) {
    return 'RTGS';
  }

  // IMPS: instant, mid-range amounts
  if (amountPaise <= IMPS_LIMIT_PAISE && beneficiaryBankIMPS) {
    return 'IMPS';
  }

  // NEFT: fallback — broad coverage, no upper limit, slight delay acceptable
  return 'NEFT';
}
```

**Decision table in plain terms:**

| Scenario | Rail |
|---|---|
| Consumer payment, amount < ₹1L, recipient has UPI | UPI |
| Intraday corporate treasury, amount > ₹2L | RTGS |
| Payout to small bank not on IMPS/UPI, any amount | NEFT |
| Instant payout, ₹1L–₹5L, recipient bank on IMPS | IMPS |
| Bulk salary disbursement, overnight, any amount | NEFT |
| Real estate transaction, ₹50L | RTGS |
| Refund to customer wallet, any amount | UPI or IMPS |

## Section 4: IMPS — Immediate Payment Service

**Q9. How does IMPS work technically, and what makes it always-on?**

A: IMPS is operated by NPCI and uses a **push payment** model with real-time processing and 24×7 availability. Unlike NEFT/RTGS (which are bank-to-bank), IMPS routes through NPCI's central switch, which sits between member banks.

**End-to-end IMPS flow:**

```
Step 1 — Initiation
  Sender's bank receives transfer instruction
  Validates: account number, IFSC, amount, sender balance
  Assigns a unique IMPS transaction reference number

Step 2 — Routing via NPCI switch
  Sending bank sends IMPS message to NPCI's central switch
  Message format: ISO 8583 (financial transaction messages)
  Fields: sending bank code, receiving bank IFSC, amount, sender/receiver account

Step 3 — NPCI processing
  NPCI authenticates the message, validates the beneficiary bank
  Routes the credit instruction to the beneficiary bank
  Response time target: < 30 seconds (typically < 5 seconds in practice)

Step 4 — Credit at beneficiary bank
  Beneficiary bank receives credit instruction from NPCI
  Credits the beneficiary's account in real-time
  Sends acknowledgment to NPCI

Step 5 — Settlement (deferred)
  Actual inter-bank settlement happens later via NPCI's multilateral net settlement
  Timing: typically settled the same day or next business day
  Settlement is in RBI current accounts (same as NEFT)
```

**The "real-time" vs. "real-time gross" distinction:** IMPS credits the beneficiary's account in real-time, but the inter-bank settlement is deferred (net). This means there is a settlement risk window — if the sending bank fails between the IMPS credit and the net settlement, NPCI and member banks absorb the risk. This is why IMPS has a ₹5L per-transaction limit (banks manage their intraday exposure) while RTGS (which settles gross in real-time) has no limit.

**Q10. What are IMPS transaction identifiers, and how do you use them for reconciliation?**

A: IMPS uses two primary identifiers that you must store and reconcile against:

**RRN (Retrieval Reference Number):**
- 12-digit numeric string.
- Assigned by the **originating bank** at the time of transaction initiation.
- Used to query transaction status from your bank's API.
- Unique within the originating bank; not globally unique across all banks.

**Transaction ID / IMPS Transaction Reference:**
- Assigned by **NPCI** at its central switch.
- Globally unique within NPCI's system.
- The authoritative reference for dispute resolution with NPCI.
- Often called `NPCI Ref No` in bank statements and settlement reports.

**Your payout record schema (additions for IMPS):**

```sql
CREATE TABLE payouts (
    payout_id         UUID        PRIMARY KEY,
    user_id           UUID        NOT NULL,
    amount_paise      BIGINT      NOT NULL,
    rail              TEXT        NOT NULL,    -- 'IMPS' | 'NEFT' | 'RTGS' | 'UPI'
    status            TEXT        NOT NULL,    -- 'INITIATED' | 'PENDING' | 'SUCCESS' | 'FAILED' | 'RETURNED'
    -- IMPS-specific identifiers
    bank_rrn          TEXT,                    -- RRN from originating bank
    npci_txn_ref      TEXT,                    -- NPCI transaction reference
    -- Common identifiers
    beneficiary_account TEXT      NOT NULL,
    beneficiary_ifsc    TEXT      NOT NULL,
    beneficiary_name    TEXT      NOT NULL,
    initiated_at      TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    settled_at        TIMESTAMPTZ,
    failure_reason    TEXT,
    UNIQUE (bank_rrn),
    UNIQUE (npci_txn_ref)
);
```

**Status polling with RRN:**

```typescript
async function pollIMPSStatus(payoutId: string): Promise<void> {
  const payout = await db('payouts').where({ payout_id: payoutId }).first();
  if (!payout.bank_rrn) throw new Error('No RRN — transaction not yet submitted');

  const statusResponse = await bankClient.queryIMPSStatus({
    rrn:    payout.bank_rrn,
    amount: payout.amount_paise,
    date:   payout.initiated_at,
  });

  if (statusResponse.status === 'SUCCESS') {
    await db('payouts').where({ payout_id: payoutId }).update({
      status:       'SUCCESS',
      npci_txn_ref: statusResponse.npciRef,
      settled_at:   statusResponse.settledAt,
    });
    await settlePayoutInLedger(payoutId);
  } else if (statusResponse.status === 'FAILED') {
    await handlePayoutFailure(payoutId, statusResponse.failureReason);
  }
  // If PENDING, schedule another poll after 30 seconds
}
```

**Q11. What are the IMPS transaction limits and failure modes?**

A: **Transaction limits (as of 2024):**
- **Minimum:** ₹1 (no practical lower limit).
- **Maximum:** ₹5,00,000 (₹5 lakh) per transaction per NPCI guidelines. Some banks extend to ₹10L for verified customers via specific product agreements.
- **Daily limit:** Bank-imposed; typically ₹10L–₹25L per customer per day.

**Failure modes:**

| Error | Meaning | Your action |
|---|---|---|
| `00` | Success | Mark SUCCESS; settle in ledger |
| `01` | Refer to card issuer | Retry; escalate if persistent |
| `05` | Do not honor | Hard failure; return funds to sender |
| `14` | Invalid card number / account | Invalid beneficiary details; return funds; notify sender |
| `51` | Insufficient funds | Sender balance issue (shouldn't occur if you checked before initiating) |
| `54` | Expired card / closed account | Return funds; mark beneficiary inactive |
| `91` | Issuer unavailable (beneficiary bank down) | Retry with exponential backoff; IMPS has 24×7 but banks have maintenance windows |
| `96` | System malfunction | Retry; query status via RRN before retrying to avoid duplicate |
| `BT` | Beneficiary account type mismatch | Return funds; verify account type |

**Critical rule before retrying:** Always query the status via RRN before initiating a retry. An `IMPS 96 / System malfunction` error may mean the transaction succeeded on NPCI's side but the response was lost in transit. Retrying without checking first risks a duplicate payout.

## Section 5: UPI — Unified Payments Interface

**Q12. What is UPI architecturally, and how does it differ from IMPS?**

A: UPI is best understood as a **protocol layer on top of IMPS**. The actual money movement in UPI transactions settles through IMPS infrastructure (NPCI's switch, inter-bank settlement). What UPI adds is:

1. **Virtual Payment Address (VPA):** A human-readable identifier (`yashika@okicici`, `merchant@razorpay`) that replaces the account number + IFSC combination. VPAs are resolved to account numbers by NPCI's UPI mapper before the IMPS transfer is initiated.

2. **Standardized API (UPI API spec):** NPCI defines a common API that all PSPs (Payment Service Providers — banks and fintechs with PSP license) must implement. This means Razorpay Pay, PhonePe, Google Pay, and BHIM all interoperate on the same rail.

3. **Push and Pull payment models:** IMPS is push-only (sender initiates). UPI supports both push (pay) and pull (collect request — the merchant requests payment from the customer, customer approves).

4. **Deep link / intent flow:** UPI supports a redirect flow where a web or mobile app can deep-link into any UPI app on the user's device, passing the payment details (VPA, amount, reference) and receiving a callback on completion.

5. **QR code payments:** UPI QR encodes the VPA and optional amount; the customer scans and pays from any UPI app — no merchant app required.

**UPI vs. IMPS in a table:**

| Property | IMPS | UPI |
|---|---|---|
| Identifier | Account number + IFSC | VPA (`name@bank`) or phone number or QR |
| Initiation model | Push only | Push and Pull (collect) |
| API standardization | Bank-specific | NPCI-standard across all PSPs |
| Consumer experience | Form-fill (account + IFSC) | UPI ID or QR scan |
| Settlement | Real-time credit, deferred net settlement | Same (via IMPS) |
| Limits | ₹5L per transaction | ₹1L–₹2L per transaction (category-dependent) |
| Merchant integration | Bank API | UPI SDK / payment gateway |
| P2P enabled | Yes (via netbanking) | Yes (native, widely used) |

**Q13. Walk through the technical flow of a UPI payment (collect flow and pay flow).**

A: UPI has two primary initiation models:

**Pay flow (Push — customer initiates):**

```
Step 1 — Customer opens UPI app (PhonePe, Google Pay, BHIM, bank app)
Step 2 — Enters merchant's VPA or scans QR code
Step 3 — UPI app sends PAY request to customer's PSP (e.g., PhonePe's bank tie-up)
Step 4 — Customer's PSP resolves VPA → account + IFSC via NPCI's VPA mapper
Step 5 — Customer authenticates with UPI PIN (stored hashed in the PSP's secure storage)
Step 6 — Customer's PSP sends IMPS credit instruction to NPCI
Step 7 — NPCI routes to merchant's PSP
Step 8 — Merchant's PSP credits the merchant's account
Step 9 — NPCI sends transaction response (success / failure) back up the chain
Step 10 — Both customer and merchant receive notifications
Total time: typically 3–10 seconds end-to-end
```

**Collect flow (Pull — merchant initiates, customer approves):**

```
Step 1 — Merchant's backend calls PSP API: POST /upi/collect
          { payerVPA: "customer@bank", amount: 50000, note: "Order #1234", expiry: 10min }
Step 2 — PSP sends collect request to NPCI → routes to customer's PSP
Step 3 — Customer's UPI app shows a notification: "XYZ Merchant requests ₹500"
Step 4 — Customer reviews and approves with UPI PIN
Step 5 — Transaction completes as in Steps 6–10 of pay flow
```

**Collect flow timing:** The customer must approve within the expiry window (typically 10 minutes). If not approved, the collect request expires — no money moves, no failure record. Your system must handle expiry as a distinct state.

**UPI states your system must track:**

```typescript
type UPITransactionStatus =
  | 'INITIATED'      // collect request sent to NPCI
  | 'PENDING'        // customer hasn't responded yet
  | 'SUCCESS'        // payment completed
  | 'FAILED'         // customer declined or bank declined
  | 'EXPIRED'        // collect request expired without response
  | 'REFUNDED'       // reversed after success
  | 'DEEMED'         // terminal ambiguous state — treat as failed after investigation;

// DEEMED: NPCI returned an ambiguous response;
// must query NPCI's transaction status API before deciding
```

**Q14. What is a UPI VPA (Virtual Payment Address), and how is it resolved to a bank account?**

A: A **VPA** (also called UPI ID or UPI handle) is a unique identifier in the format `username@pspalias`. The `@pspalias` part identifies the PSP that manages the VPA:

| VPA suffix | PSP |
|---|---|
| `@okicici` | ICICI Bank (via Google Pay) |
| `@oksbi` | SBI (via Google Pay) |
| `@ybl` | Yes Bank (via PhonePe) |
| `@ibl` | IndusInd Bank (via PhonePe) |
| `@razorpay` | Razorpay (merchant VPAs) |
| `@paytm` | Paytm Payments Bank |
| `@upi` | BHIM (NPCI's own app) |

**VPA resolution process:**

```
Sender's PSP → NPCI VPA mapper
  Request: { vpa: "merchant@razorpay" }
  NPCI looks up: which PSP owns "@razorpay"? → Razorpay
  NPCI forwards resolution request to Razorpay's PSP system
  Razorpay returns: { accountNumber: "XXXXXXXXXX", ifsc: "RATN0000001", name: "Merchant XYZ" }
  NPCI returns resolved details to sender's PSP
  IMPS transaction initiated with resolved account + IFSC
```

**VPA validation before initiating collect:**

Always validate the VPA before initiating a collect request. An invalid VPA means the collect will fail immediately, wasting a transaction attempt and potentially confusing the customer.

```typescript
async function validateUPIVPA(vpa: string): Promise<{
  valid:   boolean;
  name?:   string;   // account holder's name — show to customer for confirmation
}> {
  // Basic format check: username@pspalias
  if (!/^[\w.\-]+@[\w]+$/.test(vpa)) {
    return { valid: false };
  }

  // Call PSP API to resolve VPA
  const response = await upiClient.validateVPA({ vpa });

  return {
    valid: response.status === 'VALID',
    name:  response.payeeName,   // show: "Paying to: Yashika S" before customer confirms
  };
}
```

**Q15. What are UPI transaction limits, and how do they differ by category?**

A: UPI transaction limits are set by NPCI and can be further restricted by individual PSPs and banks:

| Category | Per transaction limit | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standard P2P / P2M | ₹1,00,000 | Default for most consumers |
| Enhanced (verified accounts) | ₹2,00,000 | Bank-discretion for KYC-verified customers |
| Capital markets | ₹2,00,000 | SEBI-regulated: IPO, mutual funds, stock broking |
| Insurance premiums | ₹2,00,000 | IRDAI-governed transactions |
| Medical / healthcare | ₹2,00,000 | Hospital payments, medical insurance |
| Foreign inward remittance | ₹2,00,000 | Inbound international transfers via UPI |

**Q16. What is UPI AutoPay (recurring mandates), and how does it differ from a standard UPI collect?**

A: **UPI AutoPay** (also called UPI Mandates or UPI Recurring) is NPCI's mechanism for recurring debits via UPI — it replaces the need for NACH mandates for use cases where the recurring amount is within UPI limits and the customer has a UPI-enabled account.

**How it works:**

```
Setup phase (one-time, customer-authenticated):
  1. Merchant initiates a mandate: POST /upi/mandate
     { payerVPA, amount, frequency: 'MONTHLY', startDate, endDate, merchantRef }
  2. Customer receives notification in their UPI app
  3. Customer authenticates with UPI PIN — this authorizes ALL future debits
     within the mandate parameters (amount, frequency, validity)
  4. NPCI registers the mandate; merchant receives mandate_id

Execution phase (per billing cycle, no customer action needed):
  1. Merchant calls: POST /upi/mandate/execute { mandate_id, amount, executionDate }
     Must be called 24 hours before the debit date (pre-notification requirement)
  2. NPCI notifies customer: "₹999 will be debited tomorrow for XYZ subscription"
     Customer can pause/cancel from UPI app
  3. On execution date: debit happens automatically if not cancelled
  4. Merchant receives SUCCESS/FAILED webhook
```

**Key differences from standard collect:**

| Property | Standard UPI Collect | UPI AutoPay Mandate |
|---|---|---|
| Customer action per payment | Required (approve each time) | One-time setup; no per-payment action |
| Pre-notification | Not required | 24-hour advance notice mandatory |
| Cancellation | N/A (single transaction) | Customer can cancel from any UPI app |
| Amount flexibility | Fixed per collect request | Fixed or variable (within mandate limit) |
| Use case | One-time payments | Subscriptions, EMIs, insurance premiums |
| Customer drop-off risk | High (must approve each time) | Low after setup |
| RBI regulation | Standard UPI guidelines | RBI circular on recurring payments (2021) |

**Q17. How do you implement a payout service that supports multiple rails with automatic fallback?**

A: A production payout service must handle rail unavailability gracefully. The strategy is: select the optimal rail, attempt the payout, and fall back to an alternative rail if the primary fails.

**Architecture:**

```typescript
interface PayoutRequest {
  payoutId:           string;
  userId:             string;
  amountPaise:        bigint;
  beneficiaryAccount: string;
  beneficiaryIFSC:    string;
  beneficiaryName:    string;
  beneficiaryVPA?:    string;   // optional — if available, enables UPI
  urgency:            'INSTANT' | 'STANDARD';
}

class PayoutOrchestrator {
  private readonly railPriority: Rail[] = ['UPI', 'IMPS', 'NEFT', 'RTGS'];

  async executePayout(request: PayoutRequest): Promise<PayoutResult> {
    const eligibleRails = this.getEligibleRails(request);

    for (const rail of eligibleRails) {
      try {
        const result = await this.attemptPayout(rail, request);
        if (result.status === 'SUCCESS' || result.status === 'PENDING') {
          await this.recordPayoutAttempt(request.payoutId, rail, result);
          return result;
        }
      } catch (err) {
        if (isRailUnavailable(err)) {
          logger.warn({ rail, payoutId: request.payoutId }, 'Rail unavailable — trying next');
          await this.recordFailedAttempt(request.payoutId, rail, err);
          continue;   // try next rail
        }
        if (isHardFailure(err)) {
          // e.g., invalid account — no point retrying on another rail
          throw err;
        }
      }
    }

    throw new AllRailsFailedError(request.payoutId);
  }

  private getEligibleRails(request: PayoutRequest): Rail[] {
    const eligible: Rail[] = [];

    if (request.beneficiaryVPA && request.amountPaise <= 100_000_00n) {
      eligible.push('UPI');
    }
    if (request.amountPaise <= 500_000_00n) {
      eligible.push('IMPS');
    }
    eligible.push('NEFT');   // always available as fallback
    if (request.amountPaise >= 200_000_00n) {
      eligible.push('RTGS');
    }

    // Sort by urgency preference
    return request.urgency === 'INSTANT'
      ? eligible                           // prefer faster rails first
      : [...eligible].reverse();           // prefer NEFT first for cost savings
  }
}
```

**Q18. How do you reconcile payouts across NEFT, IMPS, RTGS, and UPI against your bank's statement?**

A: Bank statements arrive in different formats and at different frequencies across rails. Reconciliation must normalize across all of them.

**Statement format by rail:**

| Rail | Statement delivery | Key identifier in statement | Timing |
|---|---|---|---|
| NEFT | End-of-day file (MT940 / CSV) | UTR (Unique Transaction Reference) | Next morning |
| RTGS | Near-real-time (notification per transaction) | UTR | Within minutes |
| IMPS | Intraday (webhooks or file) | RRN + NPCI Ref | Same day |
| UPI | Intraday file + webhook | UPI Transaction ID | Same day |

**UTR (Unique Transaction Reference):** A 22-character reference generated by RBI for every NEFT and RTGS transaction. Format: `BANKYYYYMMDD<sequence>`. Example: `HDFC240115000123456`. Store the UTR in your payout record — it is the primary reconciliation key against the bank statement.

**Reconciliation logic:**

```typescript
interface BankStatementEntry {
  valueDate:   Date;
  amount:      bigint;    // paise
  type:        'DEBIT' | 'CREDIT';
  description: string;
  utr?:        string;    // NEFT/RTGS
  rrn?:        string;    // IMPS
  upiTxnId?:  string;    // UPI
}

async function reconcilePayouts(
  statementEntries: BankStatementEntry[],
  date: Date,
): Promise<ReconciliationReport> {
  const unmatched: BankStatementEntry[] = [];
  const matched:   Array<{ entry: BankStatementEntry; payout: Payout }> = [];
  const missing:   Payout[] = [];   // payouts in DB not in statement

  for (const entry of statementEntries.filter(e => e.type === 'DEBIT')) {
    // Try to match by UTR, RRN, or UPI transaction ID
    const payout = await db('payouts').where(function () {
      if (entry.utr)      this.orWhere({ bank_utr:     entry.utr });
      if (entry.rrn)      this.orWhere({ bank_rrn:     entry.rrn });
      if (entry.upiTxnId) this.orWhere({ upi_txn_id:  entry.upiTxnId });
    }).first();

    if (!payout) {
      unmatched.push(entry);  // in bank statement but not in our DB
      continue;
    }

    if (payout.amount_paise !== entry.amount) {
      await alertingService.critical('RECONCILIATION_AMOUNT_MISMATCH', {
        payoutId:       payout.payout_id,
        dbAmount:       payout.amount_paise,
        statementAmount: entry.amount,
      });
    }

    matched.push({ entry, payout });
    await db('payouts').where({ payout_id: payout.payout_id })
      .update({ status: 'SETTLED', settled_at: entry.valueDate });
  }

  // Find payouts in SUCCESS state not in today's statement
  const settledPayoutIds = new Set(matched.map(m => m.payout.payout_id));
  const expectedInStatement = await db('payouts')
    .where({ status: 'SUCCESS' })
    .whereBetween('initiated_at', [startOfDay(date), endOfDay(date)]);

  for (const payout of expectedInStatement) {
    if (!settledPayoutIds.has(payout.payout_id)) {
      missing.push(payout);
    }
  }

  return { matched, unmatched, missing };
}
```

**Q19. What is a "deemed" transaction in UPI, and how should your system handle it?**

A: A **deemed transaction** in UPI (also called a transaction with ambiguous final status) occurs when:
- The IMPS/UPI network processed the transaction successfully.
- But the response was lost in transit (network timeout between your system and the PSP, or between PSP and NPCI).
- Your system sees a timeout or error; NPCI's records show a success.

Without careful handling, your system might:
- **Not credit the merchant** (treating the ambiguous response as a failure) — merchant loses money.
- **Retry the transaction** — customer is double-charged.

Both are catastrophic. UPI's handling:

```typescript
async function initiateUPICollect(collectRequest: UPICollectRequest): Promise<string> {
  let transactionId: string | null = null;

  try {
    const response = await upiClient.sendCollect(collectRequest, {
      timeout: 30_000,  // 30-second timeout
    });
    transactionId = response.transactionId;
    return transactionId;

  } catch (err) {
    if (err instanceof TimeoutError || err instanceof NetworkError) {
      // DO NOT retry blindly — this may have succeeded at NPCI
      // Store as PENDING and query status
      await db('upi_transactions').insert({
        reference_id:   collectRequest.merchantRef,
        status:         'PENDING',
        initiated_at:   new Date(),
        last_queried_at: null,
      });

      // Schedule a status check job
      await statusCheckQueue.enqueue({
        merchantRef: collectRequest.merchantRef,
        checkAfter:  new Date(Date.now() + 30_000),  // check after 30s
        maxAttempts: 10,
      });

      return 'PENDING';
    }
    throw err;
  }
}

async function checkUPIStatus(merchantRef: string): Promise<void> {
  const response = await upiClient.queryTransactionStatus({ merchantRef });

  switch (response.status) {
    case 'SUCCESS':
      await settleUPITransaction(merchantRef, response);
      break;
    case 'FAILED':
    case 'EXPIRED':
      await failUPITransaction(merchantRef, response.failureCode);
      break;
    case 'PENDING':
      // Still processing — requeue for another check
      await statusCheckQueue.enqueue({ merchantRef, checkAfter: new Date(Date.now() + 60_000) });
      break;
    case 'DEEMED':
      // NPCI's terminal ambiguous state — treat as failed after 24h investigation window
      await flagAsDeemedForManualReview(merchantRef);
      break;
  }
}
```

**Timeout handling rule:** Never mark a UPI or IMPS transaction as failed on a timeout. Mark it as `PENDING` and always check status before either settling or retrying. This is the same principle as idempotency for API retries — applied to rail-level transaction outcomes.

**Q20. "Design the payment receipt flow for an EMI lending platform that supports UPI, IMPS, NEFT, and NACH mandates for monthly repayments."**

A: A lending platform collects EMI repayments on a fixed schedule. The collection strategy must handle different customer preferences, amount tiers, and failure scenarios.

**Collection decision tree per EMI:**

```
For each due EMI:
  If customer has active NACH mandate:
    → Primary: NACH debit (no customer action; most reliable for recurring)
    → If NACH fails: fall back to UPI collect → IMPS collect → manual reminder
  Else if EMI amount ≤ ₹1L AND customer has UPI handle:
    → Send UPI AutoPay mandate setup request (first time)
    → If mandate active: auto-debit via UPI AutoPay
    → If mandate not set up: send UPI collect (customer must approve each time)
  Else if EMI amount > ₹1L:
    → IMPS/RTGS payout link (customer-initiated from their bank)
    → NEFT for EMIs where 1-day delay is acceptable
```

**System design for the collection scheduler:**

```typescript
interface EMICollection {
  loanId:         string;
  emiNumber:      number;
  dueDatePaise:   bigint;
  dueDate:        Date;
  customerId:     string;
}

class EMICollectionService {
  async scheduleCollection(emi: EMICollection): Promise<void> {
    const customer = await this.getCustomerPaymentProfile(emi.customerId);

    // T-1 day: pre-debit notification (mandatory for all rails)
    await this.sendPreDebitNotification(emi, customer);

    // T day: attempt collection in priority order
    if (customer.nachMandateId && customer.nachMandateStatus === 'ACTIVE') {
      await this.collectViaNACH(emi, customer.nachMandateId);
      return;
    }

    if (customer.upiMandateId && emi.dueDatePaise <= 100_000_00n) {
      await this.collectViaUPIAutoPayMandate(emi, customer.upiMandateId);
      return;
    }

    // UPI collect (customer approval required)
    if (customer.upiHandle && emi.dueDatePaise <= 100_000_00n) {
      await this.sendUPICollectRequest(emi, customer.upiHandle);
      return;
    }

    // Fallback: send payment link (customer initiates via any method)
    await this.sendPaymentLink(emi, customer);
  }
}
```

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level in a fintech company in India, payment rails questions appear in three forms: (1) "which rail would you use for X?" — know the decision tree cold: amount thresholds (RTGS ≥ ₹2L, UPI ≤ ₹1L, IMPS ≤ ₹5L), urgency, and whether the use case is push or pull; (2) "how do you handle a payment that timed out?" — the answer is always: mark as `PENDING`, query status by RRN/UTR before retrying, never blindly retry, never mark as failed on timeout alone; (3) "design an EMI collection system" — they expect you to know NACH for recurring, UPI AutoPay for smaller mandates, and the pre-debit notification requirement. Know what UTR and RRN are and that they are different identifiers on different rails — confusing them in an interview is a red flag. Know that UPI settles via IMPS infrastructure — it is a protocol layer, not a separate settlement network. And always connect rail selection to the business consequence: choosing NEFT for an instant refund use case is not a technical mistake but a product mistake that loses customer trust.
