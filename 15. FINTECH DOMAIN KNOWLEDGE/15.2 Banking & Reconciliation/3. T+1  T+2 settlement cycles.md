## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What does "T+1" and "T+2" mean, and why does settlement not happen instantly?**
A: T is the **transaction date** — the day the payment is initiated or authorized. T+1 and T+2 refer to the number of *business days* after T on which funds are actually transferred between financial institutions and deposited into the merchant's or recipient's account.

- **T+0 (same-day)** — funds settle the same business day. Rare for card transactions; common for certain wire transfers and real-time payment rails (RTP, FedNow).
- **T+1** — funds settle the next business day. Current standard for US equities (as of May 2024), some ACH same-day credit entries, and many modern card processors under favorable conditions.
- **T+2** — funds settle two business days after the transaction. Traditional card settlement standard; still the default for most card networks globally.
- **T+3 and beyond** — legacy norms now mostly phased out for cards; still common for certain securities (international equities, some bonds) and slower ACH batches.

**Why not instant?**
Settlement involves multiple independent institutions — the merchant's acquiring bank, the card network (Visa/Mastercard), and the cardholder's issuing bank — each running batch processes on their own systems, often on different technology stacks with different cut-off times. The delay exists because:

1. **Batch processing architecture** — card networks historically (and still largely) process transactions in discrete batches rather than real-time streams. Batches close at specific cut-off times (often 10 PM–midnight local time); transactions after the cut-off go into the next day's batch.
2. **Interbank clearing** — funds do not move directly from issuer to acquirer. They flow through a central clearinghouse (Visa/MC's own settlement systems, or FedACH for ACH), which nets out obligations and moves the net difference.
3. **Risk and float management** — the delay gives the network and banks time to identify fraudulent transactions, process chargebacks, and withhold funds from high-risk merchants before releasing them.
4. **Reserve requirements** — processors and acquirers may hold a rolling reserve (a percentage of settled funds) for a defined period as fraud/chargeback insurance, effectively extending the practical settlement window beyond T+2.

**Q2. What is the difference between authorization, capture, clearing, and settlement? Why do engineers conflate them?**
A: These four terms describe distinct stages in the payment flow, each with different timing and different parties involved. Conflation is common because most payment SDKs abstract them into "charge succeeded," but the underlying mechanics differ critically for reconciliation, cash flow, and dispute handling.

**Authorization**
- Timing: real-time (200–800ms)
- What happens: the merchant's processor sends an authorization request to the card network, which forwards it to the issuing bank. The issuer checks available credit/funds, fraud rules, and velocity limits. If approved, the issuer places a **hold** on the cardholder's funds equal to the authorized amount.
- Money moves: **no**. A hold is a reservation, not a transfer.
- Who's involved: merchant system → acquirer → card network → issuer.

**Capture**
- Timing: immediate (auto-capture) or deferred (up to 7 days, varies by network; authorization expires if not captured)
- What happens: the merchant instructs the processor to finalize the authorized amount and submit it for clearing. For e-commerce, auto-capture (simultaneous authorization + capture) is standard. For hotel/rental/marketplace, deferred capture allows the final amount to differ from the authorization (e.g., adding charges for room service).
- Money moves: **no** — capture is still within the merchant's processor. It moves the transaction from "authorized" to "captured" status in the processor's system.
- Who's involved: merchant system → processor.

**Clearing**
- Timing: batch process, typically once daily
- What happens: captured transactions are batched by the acquirer and submitted to the card network. The network routes each transaction to the appropriate issuer and computes the net settlement obligations (how much each bank owes or is owed across all transactions in the batch).
- Money moves: **not yet** — clearing produces a net position (a debit or credit) for each bank.
- Who's involved: acquirer → card network → issuer.

**Settlement**
- Timing: T+1 or T+2 after capture
- What happens: the card network (or a designated settlement bank) executes the actual fund transfers between banks based on the cleared net positions. The acquirer receives funds from the network and credits the merchant's account (minus fees).
- Money moves: **yes** — actual interbank fund transfer occurs here.
- Who's involved: card network settlement bank → acquirer → merchant bank account.

**The engineering trap:** a `payment_status = 'succeeded'` in Stripe means capture succeeded. It does *not* mean funds are in the merchant's bank account. An engineer building a payout system who equates "succeeded" with "available for payout" will create incorrect cash flow projections and potentially overdraft the merchant's operating account.

**Q3. What is the difference between gross settlement and net settlement, and why does it matter?**
A: This distinction determines how much money actually moves between financial institutions at settlement time.

- **Gross settlement (RTGS — Real-Time Gross Settlement)** — each transaction settles individually, in full, in real time. $1,000 transaction → $1,000 moves from issuer to acquirer immediately. Used for high-value wire transfers (Fedwire, TARGET2 in Europe). Safe, final, and expensive (per-transaction cost). Not practical for consumer card transactions (millions per day).

- **Net settlement** — all transactions between a pair of institutions are accumulated over a period (typically one day), and only the *net difference* moves. If Bank A owes Bank B $10M across thousands of transactions, and Bank B owes Bank A $8M across different transactions, only $2M moves (Bank A → Bank B). Used by Visa, Mastercard, ACH. Dramatically reduces the volume and cost of interbank transfers.

**Why it matters for engineers:**
- In a net settlement system, the settlement amount your processor receives is not the sum of individual transaction amounts — it's the net after interchange, processor fees, chargebacks, and refunds are deducted. Your reconciliation system must account for this.
- Net settlement creates **intraday risk**: if a large issuer fails before settlement completes, the network may not be able to pay acquirers for transactions that have already been authorized. Card networks mitigate this with settlement guarantees and collateral requirements on member banks.
- **Settlement finality** is different from authorization. An authorization can be reversed; a settled fund transfer has legal finality in most jurisdictions (subject to chargeback rules, which operate on top of settlement).

## Section 2: Card Settlement in Depth

**Q4. Walk through the exact mechanics of how a Visa transaction settles from authorization to merchant bank deposit.**
A: Step by step, with timing:

**Day 0 (T) — Authorization through Capture**
```
09:00 AM  Customer taps card at merchant terminal
09:00:00  Terminal → Acquirer: authorization request (ISO 8583 message)
09:00:00  Acquirer → Visa Network: authorization forwarded
09:00:01  Visa → Issuing Bank: authorization request
09:00:01  Issuing Bank: checks balance, fraud rules, approves
09:00:01  Issuing Bank → Visa: authorization response (code 00 Approved)
09:00:01  Visa → Acquirer: approved
09:00:01  Acquirer → Terminal: approved
          Issuing bank places HOLD on cardholder funds

10:00 PM  Merchant terminal batch close / capture
          Merchant's POS or processor auto-submits all captured
          transactions to the acquirer for clearing
```

**Day 1 (T+1) — Clearing**
```
12:00 AM  Acquirer batches all captured transactions and submits
          clearing file to Visa VisaNet clearing system

          Visa processes clearing file:
          - Routes each transaction to the correct issuer
          - Computes interchange for each transaction
          - Nets obligations: [Issuer owes Acquirer] - [Chargebacks, Refunds]
          - Produces settlement position for each member bank

02:00 AM  Visa settlement file distributed to member banks
          Issuers: net debit position (they owe Visa the settlement amount)
          Acquirers: net credit position (Visa owes them the settlement amount)
```

**Day 1 or 2 (T+1 or T+2) — Settlement**
```
06:00 AM  Visa Settlement Bank (US Bank for Visa) executes:
          - Debits each issuer's Federal Reserve account (Fedwire)
            for their net settlement obligation
          - Credits each acquirer's Federal Reserve account
            for their net settlement receipt

08:00 AM  Acquirer receives credit in their Federal Reserve / bank account

          Acquirer computes merchant payout:
          Settlement received
          - Interchange (paid to issuers, already netted by Visa)
          - Acquirer processing fees
          - Chargebacks and refunds
          - Rolling reserve (if applicable)
          = Net merchant payout

10:00 AM  Acquirer initiates ACH transfer to merchant's bank account
          OR credits merchant's account if they hold funds at the acquirer

D+1 to D+2  Funds available in merchant's bank account
             (ACH delivery adds another 1–2 business days for some acquirers)
```

**Total elapsed time:** card swipe at 9 AM on Monday → funds in merchant account Thursday morning is a common real-world timeline, even though "T+2" implies Wednesday.

**Q5. What is interchange, who pays it, and how does it flow through the settlement process?**
A: Interchange is the fee paid by the merchant's acquirer to the cardholder's issuer on every card transaction. It is set by the card network (Visa, Mastercard) but flows between the acquirer and issuer — the network itself is not the recipient.

**Flow:**
```
Transaction: $100 consumer credit card (interchange rate: 1.65% + $0.10 = $1.75)

Cardholder pays merchant:   $100.00
Issuer holds cardholder:    $100.00

Settlement flow:
  Issuer → Card Network:    $100.00  (gross)
  Card Network → Acquirer:   $98.25  (net of $1.75 interchange)
  Network pays Issuer:        $1.75  (interchange, as net settlement adjustment)

  Acquirer receives:         $98.25
  Acquirer deducts its fee:  -$0.30  (processor markup)
  Merchant receives:         $97.95
```

**Key engineering implications:**
- The merchant never sees the interchange line item in most integrations — it's already netted by the time the acquirer pays out. But it appears in the acquirer's settlement report, which your reconciliation system must parse.
- Interchange varies per transaction — the same merchant can see 10+ different interchange rates in a single day's settlement file depending on card type, entry mode, and MCC. Reconciliation cannot assume a flat fee rate.
- For interchange-plus pricing (the most transparent model), your settlement report shows: gross transaction amount, interchange amount, processor markup, and net payout — four distinct fields. For flat-rate pricing (Stripe standard), interchange is bundled into the processor fee and not separately visible.

**Q6. What are settlement cut-off times, and how do they interact with business days and holidays?**
A: Settlement cut-off times are the deadlines by which captured transactions must be submitted to the processor to be included in that day's clearing batch. Missing the cut-off means the transaction rolls into the next business day's batch.

**Typical cut-off times:**
| Processor / Network | Cut-off Time (ET) | Settlement Timing |
|--|--|--|
| Visa (US) | 10:00 PM | T+1 (next business day) |
| Mastercard (US) | 10:00 PM | T+1 |
| Stripe (US merchants) | No cut-off (continuous) | 2 business days after capture |
| Adyen | Multiple daily batches | T+1 or T+2 depending on currency |
| Braintree | 12:00 AM | T+2 |
| American Express | Varies by market | T+1 to T+3 |

**Business day complications:**
- "Business day" means banking days — weekdays excluding federal holidays (US: New Year's, MLK Day, Presidents Day, Memorial Day, July 4th, Labor Day, Columbus Day, Veterans Day, Thanksgiving, Christmas).
- A Friday night capture (after cut-off) enters Saturday's batch — but Saturday is not a business day for Fedwire. The clearing and settlement happens on the following Monday, making effective settlement T+3 or T+4.
- Holiday weekends compound: a Thursday capture before a three-day weekend (Memorial Day) that misses Thursday's cut-off effectively settles on Tuesday — T+5 elapsed calendar days.

**Engineering implication:** your settlement estimation system cannot use simple `transaction_date + 2 days` arithmetic. It requires a **business calendar** that accounts for cut-off times, weekends, and holiday schedules per country and per processor. Build or license a business calendar library; do not implement holiday logic from scratch (holiday schedules change).

## Section 3: ACH Settlement in Depth

**Q7. How does ACH settlement differ from card settlement, and what are the timing implications?**
A: ACH (Automated Clearing House) is a batch electronic fund transfer network operated by Nacha (National Automated Clearing House Association) in the US. It handles direct deposits, bill payments, and B2B transfers. Its settlement mechanics are fundamentally different from card networks.

**Structural differences:**

| Property | Card (Visa/MC) | ACH |
|--|--|--|
| Authorization | Real-time (< 1s) | None — funds deducted on settlement |
| Settlement timing | T+1 to T+2 | Standard: T+1; Same-Day ACH: T+0 |
| Reversibility | Chargeback (up to 120 days) | Return window: 2–60 business days |
| Settlement guarantee | Near-certain (network guarantees) | Not guaranteed — returns are common |
| Failure notification | Synchronous (decline code) | Asynchronous (return file, days later) |
| Typical cost | 1.5–3% (card) | $0.20–$1.50 flat |
| Infrastructure | ISO 8583 messages | NACHA file format (fixed-width text) |

**ACH timing mechanics:**
```
Standard ACH (next-day):
  Day 0 (T):      Originator submits NACHA file to ODFI (bank) by 2:45 PM ET
  Day 0 (T):      ODFI submits to Federal Reserve ACH (FedACH) by cut-off
  Day 1 (T+1):    FedACH processes overnight, distributes to RDFI (recipient bank)
  Day 1 (T+1):    RDFI credits recipient's account
  Day 1–60:       RDFI return window (can reverse for various reasons)

Same-Day ACH (three processing windows daily):
  Window 1:   Submission by 10:30 AM ET → settlement by 1:00 PM ET (T+0)
  Window 2:   Submission by 2:45 PM ET  → settlement by 5:00 PM ET (T+0)
  Window 3:   Submission by 4:45 PM ET  → settlement by 6:00 PM ET (T+0)
  Same-Day ACH cap: $1,000,000 per transaction (raised from $100K in 2020)
```

**The key engineering difference:** card authorization gives you near-real-time certainty about payment outcome. ACH gives you *no* synchronous response — the funds are presumed to transfer, and you learn about failures (returns) *days later* via an asynchronous return file. Your application must handle the "funds in flight" state gracefully: provisionally credit the customer while the ACH settles, and reconcile returns when they arrive.

**Q8. What are ACH return codes and how do they affect settlement accounting?**
A: ACH return codes are generated by the recipient's bank (RDFI) and sent back to the originator's bank (ODFI) when a debit or credit cannot be processed. They arrive asynchronously — often 2–5 business days after the original transaction.

**Return timing by code type:**
| Return Type | Return Window | Example Codes |
|--|--|--|
| Administrative | 2 business days | R02 (Account Closed), R03 (No Account), R04 (Invalid Account Number) |
| Insufficient funds | 2 business days | R01 (NSF), R09 (Uncollected Funds) |
| Authorization-related | 60 calendar days | R07 (Authorization Revoked), R08 (Payment Stopped), R10 (Not Authorized) |
| Late returns | Up to 60 calendar days | R06 (ODFI Return Request), R31 |

**Settlement accounting impact:**
When an ACH debit is returned, the funds flow in reverse:
```
Original debit (Day 0):    ODFI debits customer account, credits merchant   +$500
Return received (Day 3):   ODFI reverses — debits merchant account          -$500
Net to merchant:            $0 (plus any return fee from processor)
```

Your accounting system must:
1. Record the original ACH debit as a *pending* receivable, not confirmed revenue.
2. On return receipt, reverse the receivable and book a return fee expense.
3. If goods/services were already delivered, open a collections workflow.
4. Track return codes for risk scoring — a customer with multiple R01 or R10 returns is a collections risk.

**NACHA retry rules for returns:**
- R01 (Insufficient Funds): maximum 2 retries within 180 days of original authorization.
- R09 (Uncollected Funds): same 2-retry limit.
- R10 (Not Authorized): **zero retries** — this is a dispute; retrying is a NACHA violation and potential fraud.

## Section 4: Real-Time Settlement Rails

**Q9. What are RTP and FedNow, and how do they change the settlement model?**
A: RTP (Real-Time Payments, operated by The Clearing House) and FedNow (operated by the Federal Reserve, launched July 2023) are instant payment rails that settle transactions in **seconds**, 24/7/365, including weekends and holidays.

**Architecture difference from ACH/Card:**
```
ACH / Card (batch, deferred):
  Originator → Batch file → Clearinghouse → Batch file → Recipient
  Settlement: T+0 to T+2, business days only, batch processing

RTP / FedNow (real-time, continuous):
  Sender Bank → Payment message → RTP/FedNow → Recipient Bank → Credit
  Settlement: < 5 seconds, any time of day, every day
  Settlement finality: immediate and irrevocable
```

**Key properties of RTP / FedNow:**

| Property | RTP (TCH) | FedNow (Federal Reserve) |
|--|--|--|
| Launch | 2017 | July 2023 |
| Availability | 24/7/365 | 24/7/365 |
| Settlement | Immediate (< 5s) | Immediate (< 5s) |
| Max transaction | $10M (raised from $1M in 2022) | $500,000 (default; up to $25M for institutions) |
| Settlement finality | Irrevocable | Irrevocable |
| Credit/debit push | Push only (credit transfers) | Push only |
| Reach | ~65% of US DDA accounts | Growing (Fed member banks) |
| Operator | The Clearing House (private) | Federal Reserve (public) |

**Settlement finality is the critical engineering difference:**
- ACH returns can reverse a settled transaction up to 60 days later. You must hold a reserve.
- RTP and FedNow settlements are **irrevocable** — once the recipient's bank confirms, the funds cannot be recalled by the payment rail. Disputes are handled out-of-band (bank-to-bank negotiation or legal), not through the payment system.
- For merchants: receiving an RTP payment is as good as cash. No chargeback risk, no settlement delay. For payers: sending an RTP payment is permanent — there is no "undo" button within the payment system.

**When engineers encounter RTP/FedNow:**
- Instant payouts to gig workers (Uber, DoorDash settling driver earnings in real time).
- Insurance claim disbursements.
- B2B same-day supplier payments.
- Merchant payouts from payment platforms.
- Increasingly, as a checkout payment option (bank-to-bank, bypassing card networks).

**Q10. How does the SWIFT network handle international settlement, and what is the correspondent banking model?**
A: SWIFT (Society for Worldwide Interbank Financial Telecommunication) is a messaging network — it does not move money. It transmits payment instructions (MT103, MT202 messages) between banks. The actual movement of funds uses correspondent banking.

**Correspondent banking:**
Most banks do not have direct accounts at every other bank in the world. Instead, they maintain accounts (called **nostro accounts** — "our money at your bank") at large correspondent banks that have global reach (JP Morgan, Citi, Deutsche Bank, HSBC). When Bank A in Brazil wants to pay Bank B in South Korea:

```
Bank A (Brazil) → [SWIFT MT103 message] → Correspondent X (US, USD)
Correspondent X → [SWIFT MT103 message] → Correspondent Y (Singapore, USD/KRW)
Correspondent Y → [local clearing] → Bank B (South Korea)

Settlement:
  Bank A's nostro account at Correspondent X: debited
  Correspondent X's account at Correspondent Y: debited
  Bank B receives KRW via local clearing
```

**Settlement timing for SWIFT:**
- Standard SWIFT wire: T+1 to T+3 depending on the currency corridor and number of correspondent hops.
- SWIFT GPI (Global Payments Innovation): guarantees same-day delivery for participating banks in many corridors, with end-to-end tracking.
- Actual credit to beneficiary: depends on recipient bank's processing schedule — a SWIFT message arriving Friday afternoon may not be processed until Monday.

**FX and settlement risk:**
- Multi-currency payments introduce **FX settlement risk** — the exchange rate at instruction time vs. settlement time may differ.
- **Herstatt risk** (named after a 1974 bank failure): a bank pays in one currency but the counterparty fails before delivering the other currency leg. CLS Bank (Continuous Linked Settlement) mitigates this by settling both legs of FX transactions simultaneously.

**Engineering implication:**
For cross-border payment products, your settlement model must account for T+1 to T+5 variability, FX rate lock-in timing, correspondent bank cut-off times (which differ from domestic cut-offs), and the possibility of settlement failure mid-chain. SWIFT GPI tracking tokens allow you to trace a payment through the correspondent chain — build this visibility into your reconciliation system.

## Section 5: Reconciliation Engineering

**Q11. What is payment reconciliation, and why is it an engineering problem, not just an accounting problem?**
A: Reconciliation is the process of matching your internal records of what *should* have happened (orders, authorizations, captures) against what the payment processor and bank report *did* happen (settlement files, bank statements). It is an engineering problem because:

- **Volume**: a platform processing 1M transactions/day cannot reconcile manually. Automated matching pipelines are required.
- **Data heterogeneity**: settlement files from Stripe, Adyen, and your bank all have different formats (CSV, JSON, NACHA flat file, MT940 SWIFT statement) with different field names, timestamp formats, and fee structures.
- **Timing mismatches**: an order placed at 11:58 PM on Day 0 may appear in the Day 1 settlement file — your system must handle cross-day matching without false discrepancies.
- **Partial matches**: a single settlement payout may correspond to hundreds of transactions minus fees, chargebacks, and refunds — not a 1:1 match.
- **Currency conversion**: multi-currency platforms must reconcile in both transaction currency (what the customer paid) and settlement currency (what you received), with FX rates applied.

**Types of reconciliation:**

| Type | Frequency | What it matches |
|--|--|--|
| **Transaction reconciliation** | Daily | Internal order records vs. processor transaction-level data |
| **Settlement reconciliation** | Daily | Processor settlement file vs. bank statement deposit |
| **Fee reconciliation** | Monthly | Expected fees (interchange + markup) vs. charged fees |
| **Chargeback reconciliation** | Daily | Chargeback notifications vs. internal records |
| **Payout reconciliation** | Per payout | Marketplace payout ledger vs. actual bank transfers |

**Q12. What is a settlement file, and what does it contain?**
A: A settlement file (also called a remittance report or funding report) is the document your payment processor sends daily, listing every transaction that settled on that day and the net amount transferred to your bank account.

**Typical fields in a processor settlement file:**

```
settlement_date        DATE         -- date funds transferred to your bank
merchant_id            VARCHAR      -- your merchant account identifier
transaction_id         VARCHAR      -- processor's transaction reference
order_id               VARCHAR      -- your internal order reference (if submitted)
authorization_code     VARCHAR      -- issuer authorization code
card_type              VARCHAR      -- visa, mastercard, amex, discover
card_last_four         VARCHAR      -- last 4 digits of card (PCI-compliant)
transaction_date       DATE         -- date of original capture
transaction_amount     DECIMAL      -- gross transaction amount in presentment currency
currency               CHAR(3)      -- ISO 4217 currency code
interchange_amount     DECIMAL      -- interchange fee (interchange-plus pricing only)
processor_fee          DECIMAL      -- processor markup
network_fee            DECIMAL      -- card network assessment fee
chargeback_amount      DECIMAL      -- chargebacks deducted (if any)
refund_amount          DECIMAL      -- refunds deducted
reserve_amount         DECIMAL      -- rolling reserve held back
net_payout_amount      DECIMAL      -- actual amount included in bank transfer
payout_id              VARCHAR      -- batch ID for this bank transfer
```

**Engineering challenges with settlement files:**
- **Format inconsistency**: Stripe delivers JSON via API; Adyen delivers CSV via SFTP; many legacy processors deliver fixed-width flat files from the 1980s. Build a normalization layer — a `settlement_event` internal schema that all processor-specific parsers write to.
- **Missing order IDs**: if your system didn't pass a stable order reference when submitting the transaction, matching the processor's `transaction_id` to your internal order requires fuzzy matching on `(amount, timestamp, last_four)` — brittle and error-prone. Always pass a stable `metadata.order_id` in every authorization request.
- **Multi-currency**: settlement may be in a different currency than the original transaction. A EUR transaction may settle in USD at today's FX rate. Your reconciliation must store both and handle the conversion gain/loss as an accounting entry.

**Q13. How do you build an automated reconciliation pipeline?**
A: A production reconciliation pipeline has five stages:

**Stage 1 — Ingestion**
```python
# Pull settlement data from multiple sources
def ingest_settlement_files(date):
    stripe_data    = stripe_client.fetch_settlement(date)        # REST API
    adyen_data     = sftp_client.pull('/adyen/settlement/', date) # SFTP CSV
    bank_statement = mt940_parser.parse(bank_sftp_pull(date))    # MT940
    
    for record in normalize([stripe_data, adyen_data]):
        db.insert('settlement_events', record)
    
    for entry in bank_statement:
        db.insert('bank_statement_lines', entry)
```

**Stage 2 — Internal record preparation**
```sql
-- Build expected settlement view from internal order data
CREATE MATERIALIZED VIEW expected_settlements AS
SELECT
    o.id              AS order_id,
    o.created_at      AS transaction_date,
    pa.processor      AS processor,
    pa.processor_txn_id,
    o.total_amount,
    o.currency,
    -- Expected settlement date accounting for cut-offs and business days
    next_business_day(o.captured_at, 2) AS expected_settlement_date
FROM orders o
JOIN payment_attempts pa ON pa.order_id = o.id
WHERE pa.status = 'captured';
```

**Stage 3 — Matching**
```python
def reconcile(settlement_date):
    settlement_events = fetch_settlement_events(settlement_date)
    expected          = fetch_expected_settlements(settlement_date)
    
    matched, unmatched_actual, unmatched_expected = [], [], []
    
    for event in settlement_events:
        match = find_match(event, expected, tolerance_cents=1)
        if match:
            matched.append((event, match))
        else:
            unmatched_actual.append(event)
    
    for exp in expected:
        if not already_matched(exp, matched):
            unmatched_expected.append(exp)
    
    return ReconciliationResult(matched, unmatched_actual, unmatched_expected)

def find_match(event, expected_set, tolerance_cents):
    # Primary match: processor_txn_id (exact)
    exact = [e for e in expected_set if e.processor_txn_id == event.transaction_id]
    if exact: return exact[0]
    
    # Fallback: (order_id, amount ± tolerance, date within ±1 day)
    fuzzy = [e for e in expected_set
             if e.order_id == event.order_id
             and abs(e.total_amount - event.transaction_amount) <= tolerance_cents / 100
             and abs((e.expected_settlement_date - event.settlement_date).days) <= 1]
    return fuzzy[0] if len(fuzzy) == 1 else None
```

**Stage 4 — Discrepancy classification**
| Discrepancy Type | Cause | Resolution |
|--|--|--|
| **In processor, not in DB** | DB write failed after capture; race condition | Investigate; create order record; alert engineering |
| **In DB, not in processor** | Order not captured; processor dropped transaction | Re-capture if within window; mark void if expired |
| **Amount mismatch** | Partial refund, chargeback, or FX rounding | Match against refund/chargeback records |
| **Date mismatch** | Cut-off timing; holiday; late batch | Allow ±1 business day tolerance; roll to next day |
| **Duplicate in processor** | Idempotency key collision; processor bug | Alert immediately; initiate refund for duplicate |

**Stage 5 — Alerting and reporting**
- Unmatched rate > 0.5% of daily volume → PagerDuty alert (indicates systemic issue).
- Amount discrepancy > $100 per day → finance team review.
- Duplicate transactions detected → immediate engineering escalation.
- Daily reconciliation summary → finance dashboard with match rate, total discrepancy amount, and open items by age.

## Section 6: Float, Reserves, and Cash Flow Engineering

**Q14. What is payment float, and how does it affect platform cash flow management?**
A: Float is the time between when a customer's funds leave their account and when the merchant's funds arrive in their account. During this window, *someone* is holding the money — and in financial systems, holding money has economic value (interest, investment returns).

**Who holds float and when:**
```
Day 0 (T):     Customer's account debited (hold placed)
               → Issuing bank holds float for 1–2 days

Day 1–2 (T+1/T+2): Issuer pays network settlement
               → Card network holds float briefly (intraday)

Day 2–3:       Acquirer receives settlement funds
               → Acquirer holds float until merchant payout

Day 3–5:       Merchant receives funds in bank account
               Total float period: 3–5 calendar days
```

**Float at platform scale:**
A marketplace processing $10M/day with T+2 settlement holds $20M in float permanently — $20M that is "in transit" at any given time. This has material implications:
- **Working capital requirement**: the platform must have enough capital to pay sellers before settlement arrives from buyers. Some platforms solve this with a credit facility (revolving line of credit) backed by settlement receivables.
- **Float income**: the acquirer earns interest on the float during the settlement window. This is a meaningful revenue source for large processors — one reason acquirers have historically been slow to reduce settlement times.
- **Float as a moat**: Stripe, Square, and PayPal have used fast settlement (T+1, same-day, instant) as a competitive differentiator. The cost of accelerating settlement (credit risk, borrowing costs) is justified by merchant retention value.

**Q15. What is a rolling reserve, and how does it affect settlement accounting?**
A: A rolling reserve is a percentage of settlement funds that the processor withholds from the merchant for a defined period (typically 90–180 days), then releases on a rolling basis. It is the processor's insurance against chargebacks that arrive after settlement.

**Mechanics:**
```
Example: 5% rolling reserve, 90-day hold

Day 1:   $10,000 settles
         Processor withholds $500 (5%)
         Merchant receives $9,500

Day 91:  The $500 from Day 1 is released to merchant
         (assuming no chargebacks in that period)

Steady state: merchant always has [5% × 90 days of GMV] locked in reserve
At $10,000/day: $10,000 × 0.05 × 90 = $45,000 permanently in reserve
```

**When reserves are applied:**
- New merchants (first 3–6 months, until chargeback history is established).
- High-risk merchant categories (travel, subscription, digital goods, CBD).
- Merchants with elevated chargeback rates (> 0.5–1% of transactions).
- Merchants with sudden GMV spikes (processor suspects fraud).

**Engineering and accounting implications:**
- Settlement reconciliation must track the reserve holdback separately from the net payout. The `reserve_amount` field in the settlement file must be booked to a deferred asset account (money you own but can't spend yet), not written off as a fee.
- Your cash flow projection model must include reserve release schedule as future cash inflows.
- For marketplace platforms paying out to sellers: if your processor withholds a reserve from *you*, you must decide whether to pass the reserve logic to sellers or absorb it from platform margins. Most platforms absorb it and model it as a working capital cost.

**Q17. "How do you model settlement timing in a marketplace where sellers expect next-day payouts?"**
A: A marketplace that promises next-day seller payouts while receiving T+2 card settlement is using its own balance sheet as a float bridge. This is a credit function, not just an engineering one — you need both systems and financial modeling.

**The timing gap:**
```
Day 0 (T):    Buyer pays via card         → $100 captured
Day 1 (T+1):  Marketplace pays seller     → $90 payout (before card funds arrive)
Day 2 (T+2):  Card settlement arrives     → $97 net (after interchange + fees)
              Marketplace net float cost: funded seller for 1 day from own balance
```

**Engineering components:**

1. **Settlement receivables ledger** — treat unsettled captures as a receivable asset, not revenue. Each captured transaction creates a `settlement_receivable` entry with expected settlement date.

```sql
settlement_receivables (
  id                  UUID PRIMARY KEY,
  order_id            UUID NOT NULL,
  processor           VARCHAR(32),
  capture_date        DATE NOT NULL,
  expected_settlement DATE NOT NULL,      -- computed from cut-off + business calendar
  gross_amount        NUMERIC(12,2),
  expected_net        NUMERIC(12,2),      -- gross minus estimated fees
  status              VARCHAR(16),        -- pending, settled, disputed
  settled_at          DATE,
  actual_net          NUMERIC(12,2)
)
```

2. **Seller payout eligibility engine** — before initiating a payout, check:
   - Is the corresponding order captured? (if not: hold payout)
   - Is the order within chargeback window? (if platform holds reserve: deduct reserve)
   - Is the seller's payout account verified? (KYC/KYB gate)
   - Does the payout amount exceed the platform's available float balance? (credit limit gate)

3. **Float balance monitoring** — real-time ledger of:
   - Cash in platform bank accounts (available to fund payouts)
   - Settlement receivables (incoming in 1–2 days)
   - Seller payouts in flight (outgoing, initiated but not cleared)
   - Net float position = cash + receivables - payouts_in_flight
   - Alert when float position drops below a minimum buffer (e.g., 2 days of average payout volume)

4. **Business calendar for settlement estimation** — `expected_settlement_date` is critical for cash flow projection. Errors here cause the platform to over-commit payouts and undershoot available cash.

```python
def expected_settlement_date(capture_datetime, processor, currency):
    settlement_days = PROCESSOR_SETTLEMENT_DAYS[(processor, currency)]  # e.g., 2
    cut_off = PROCESSOR_CUT_OFF_TIMES[(processor, currency)]           # e.g., 22:00 ET
    
    if capture_datetime.time() > cut_off:
        # Missed today's batch — starts counting from tomorrow
        base_date = capture_datetime.date() + timedelta(days=1)
    else:
        base_date = capture_datetime.date()
    
    return add_business_days(base_date, settlement_days, holiday_calendar='US_FEDERAL')
```

**Q18. "Your daily reconciliation shows a $50,000 discrepancy between processor settlement and your internal records. Walk through your investigation."**
A: A $50K single-day discrepancy is material — it requires immediate, systematic investigation. The root cause is almost always one of five things.

**Step 1 — Characterize the discrepancy (first 30 minutes)**
```sql
-- Is the $50K a single transaction or spread across many?
SELECT COUNT(*), SUM(discrepancy_amount), MAX(ABS(discrepancy_amount))
FROM reconciliation_discrepancies
WHERE reconciliation_id = [today's run id];

-- What types of discrepancies?
SELECT discrepancy_type, COUNT(*), SUM(discrepancy_amount)
FROM reconciliation_discrepancies
WHERE reconciliation_id = [today's run id]
GROUP BY discrepancy_type;
```

**Step 2 — Check for known systematic causes:**

*Is the $50K in processor, not in your DB?*
→ Check for orders created in the last 48h where `payment_attempts.status = 'captured'` but no `orders` record exists. This indicates a write that succeeded at the processor but your DB write failed (e.g., DB timeout after the processor call). Immediate action: create the order record; audit the failed write path; ensure idempotency on the order creation flow.

*Is the $50K in your DB, not in processor?*
→ Check `expected_settlements` for orders captured 2 days ago that appear in no settlement file. Possible causes: (a) capture submitted after processor cut-off — will appear in tomorrow's file (check date + 1); (b) processor dropped the transaction — query processor API directly for the transaction; (c) authorization expired before capture — order should be voided and customer re-charged.

*Is it an amount mismatch spread across many transactions?*
→ A systematic $0.01 difference per transaction across 50,000 transactions = $500 (not $50K). A $50K spread evenly suggests a fee rate change (interchange tier change, processor repricing) that your cost model hasn't been updated with. Request updated fee schedules from processor.

*Is it a date alignment issue?*
→ Check if today is Monday (Friday night transactions rolling in), or the day after a holiday. A $50K "missing" could be yesterday's late batch arriving in today's settlement file. Expand the matching window to ±1 business day.

*Is it a currency conversion rounding?*
→ For multi-currency platforms: check if $50K corresponds to a large batch of foreign currency transactions where FX rounding accumulated. Verify the FX rate used in settlement vs. your booking rate.

**Step 3 — Escalation thresholds:**
- Identified cause, no financial loss risk: finance team notified, resolved within 3 days.
- Suspected DB write failure (money taken from customer, no order record): **immediate engineering escalation** — potential double-charge or lost revenue.
- Suspected duplicate charge (processor charged twice): **immediate processor support contact** — initiate refund within 24 hours before chargeback window opens.
- Unresolved > $10K after 24 hours: CFO notification required.
