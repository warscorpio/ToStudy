# Compliance: AML — Transaction Monitoring Rules, Suspicious Transaction Reports & System Design — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

> **Domain:** Fintech / Compliance Systems Architecture (India)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is AML, and why is it a system design problem, not just a compliance checkbox?**

A: **Anti-Money Laundering (AML)** is the set of laws, regulations, and procedures designed to prevent criminals from disguising illegally obtained money as legitimate income. The three stages of money laundering that AML systems are designed to detect are:

- **Placement:** Introducing illicit cash into the financial system — depositing it into bank accounts, purchasing monetary instruments, or blending it with legitimate business revenue.
- **Layering:** Creating distance between the money and its criminal origin through a series of complex transactions — wire transfers across jurisdictions, shell company payments, currency conversions.
- **Integration:** Reintroducing the now-"cleaned" money into the legitimate economy through real estate purchases, luxury goods, investments, or business income.

AML is a system design problem because:

- **Volume:** A mid-sized fintech processes millions of transactions per day. AML monitoring cannot be manual — every transaction must be evaluated against a rule set in near-real-time or in batch, and anomalies must be surfaced for human review.
- **Latency:** Real-time monitoring must evaluate a transaction within milliseconds if it is to block or hold the transaction before it clears. Batch monitoring (post-transaction) is simpler but only detects patterns, not individual suspicious transactions, in time.
- **False positive rate:** An AML system that flags 30% of transactions as suspicious is useless — compliance analysts cannot review that volume. The system must be calibrated to a false positive rate that humans can actually handle (typically < 1% of transactions, ideally < 0.1%).
- **Regulatory obligation:** In India, the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA) 2002 and rules thereunder, enforced by the Financial Intelligence Unit — India (FIU-IND), require all Reporting Entities (banks, NBFCs, payment aggregators, wallets) to implement transaction monitoring and file Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs) and Cash Transaction Reports (CTRs) within defined timelines. Failure attracts regulatory penalties, licence cancellation, and personal liability for the Principal Officer.

**Q2. Who are the key regulatory bodies and what are their roles in India's AML framework?**

A:

| Body | Full name | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **FIU-IND** | Financial Intelligence Unit — India | Central agency that receives, analyses, and disseminates STRs, CTRs, and CCTRs. Reports to the Finance Ministry. Maintains the goAML portal for electronic filing. |
| **RBI** | Reserve Bank of India | Regulates banks, NBFCs, payment aggregators, PPI issuers. Issues AML/KYC Master Directions. Can impose penalties for AML failures. |
| **SEBI** | Securities and Exchange Board of India | Regulates brokers, mutual funds, portfolio managers. Enforces AML compliance in capital markets. |
| **IRDAI** | Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority | Regulates insurers. AML obligations apply to premium payments and claim settlements. |
| **ED** | Enforcement Directorate | Investigates PMLA offences. Has powers of search, seizure, attachment of property, and arrest. A referral from FIU-IND can trigger an ED investigation. |
| **FATF** | Financial Action Task Force | International standard-setter. India is a FATF member. FATF's 40 Recommendations are the global AML standard that Indian regulations align with. |

**PMLA obligations for a fintech (Reporting Entity):**

```
Under PMLA 2002 and Prevention of Money Laundering (Maintenance of Records) Rules 2005:

1. Maintain records of all transactions (identity of clients + records of transactions)
   for 5 years from the date of the transaction or cessation of business.

2. Report to FIU-IND:
   a. CTR (Cash Transaction Report): Cash transactions > ₹10 lakh (or equivalent foreign currency)
      in a calendar month, per customer. Due: by 15th of the following month.
   b. STR (Suspicious Transaction Report): Any transaction suspected to involve proceeds
      of crime or financing of terrorism. Due: within 7 working days of becoming satisfied
      that the transaction is suspicious.
   c. CCTR (Counterfeit Currency Transaction Report): On receipt of counterfeit notes.
   d. NTR (Non-Profit Organisation Transaction Report): Transactions by NPOs > ₹10 lakh.

3. Designate a Principal Officer responsible for AML compliance.
   The Principal Officer is personally liable for filing failures.

4. Conduct Customer Due Diligence (CDD) — KYC at onboarding and ongoing monitoring.

5. Not "tip off" the customer when an STR is filed —
   the existence of an STR is confidential.
```

**Q3. What is the difference between rule-based monitoring and risk-based monitoring, and which should you implement?**

A: **Rule-based monitoring** applies deterministic thresholds and pattern checks to every transaction. A rule fires if a transaction or a set of transactions meets the defined criteria. Examples:

- Cash deposit > ₹10 lakh → flag for CTR.
- Wire transfer to a FATF grey-listed country > ₹5 lakh → alert.
- More than 10 transactions in 24 hours from the same account → alert.
- Transaction amount exactly at ₹9,99,999 (just below the ₹10 lakh reporting threshold) → "structuring" alert.

Advantages: transparent, explainable, deterministic, easy to audit. The compliance team and regulators can understand exactly why a flag was raised.

Disadvantages: static — criminals learn the rules and structure their behaviour to evade them. High false positive rates for busy legitimate accounts. Cannot detect novel patterns.

**Risk-based monitoring** assigns a risk score to each customer and transaction based on multiple factors — customer profile, transaction history, peer comparison, network analysis — and calibrates alert thresholds based on the risk score. High-risk customers have lower alert thresholds; low-risk customers have higher thresholds.

Advantages: reduces false positives dramatically. Adapts to customer behaviour baselines. Can incorporate machine learning for pattern detection.

Disadvantages: harder to explain to regulators ("the model said so" is not an acceptable audit trail). Requires significant data and model maintenance. Introduces model risk.

**Which to implement:**

In practice, mature AML systems use both in layers:

```
Layer 1 — Hard rules (synchronous, pre-transaction or real-time):
  Block transactions that violate absolute regulatory limits.
  Example: OFAC/UN sanctions screening; PEP (Politically Exposed Person) flag.

Layer 2 — Rule-based monitoring (batch or near-real-time):
  Flag transactions matching defined patterns.
  Generates high-volume alerts that analysts triage.
  Example: structuring, velocity rules, round-number transactions.

Layer 3 — Risk-based / ML scoring (batch):
  Score customers and transactions on risk dimensions.
  Use scores to prioritize analyst queues and tune rule thresholds.
  Example: peer group analysis, graph-based money mule detection.

Layer 4 — Human review (analyst workbench):
  Analysts review flagged alerts, clear false positives, escalate true positives.
  True positives → STR filed with FIU-IND.
```

**Q4. What are the key AML risk dimensions, and how do they map to your customer and transaction data?**

A: AML risk is assessed across four dimensions, often called the "4 Cs" or the FATF risk-based approach framework:

**Customer risk:**
- PEP (Politically Exposed Person): current or former senior government official, their family members, and close associates. Higher corruption and bribery risk. Requires enhanced due diligence.
- Adverse media: negative news linking the customer to crime, fraud, terrorism.
- High-risk occupation: cash-intensive businesses (pawn shops, jewellers, money exchangers), lawyers, accountants (professional money launderers).
- Country of origin / nationality: citizens of FATF grey-listed or black-listed countries.

**Geographic risk:**
- Transactions to/from FATF grey-listed countries (currently include Syria, Yemen, Pakistan, among others — list changes).
- Transactions to/from high-secrecy jurisdictions (Cayman Islands, BVI).
- Internal: transactions originating from high-crime geographies within India.

**Product/channel risk:**
- Cash transactions: highest risk (anonymous, no paper trail).
- Cryptocurrency: high risk (pseudonymous, cross-border).
- Wire transfers: high risk for large amounts.
- PPI wallets: medium risk (limited KYC required for small wallets).
- Lending: risk in disbursement and repayment sources.

**Transaction risk:**
- Size: large transactions, especially near reporting thresholds.
- Velocity: many transactions in short periods.
- Pattern: round numbers, just-below-threshold amounts, dormant accounts suddenly active.
- Counterparty: transactions with high-risk entities, known money mule accounts.

## Section 2: Transaction Monitoring Rules

**Q5. What are the core categories of AML transaction monitoring rules, with concrete examples for each?**

A: AML monitoring rules fall into six categories. Every rule has a definition, a trigger condition, a lookback window, and a disposition (alert type and priority).

**Category 1 — Threshold rules:**

Single-transaction rules based on absolute amounts.

```typescript
const THRESHOLD_RULES = [
  {
    ruleId:       'THR_001',
    name:         'Large Cash Transaction — CTR Trigger',
    description:  'Single cash transaction >= ₹10 lakh triggers mandatory CTR filing',
    trigger: (txn: Transaction) =>
      txn.channel === 'CASH' && txn.amountPaise >= 1_000_000_00n,
    alertType:    'CTR_REQUIRED',
    priority:     'CRITICAL',  // regulatory filing required
  },
  {
    ruleId:       'THR_002',
    name:         'High-Value Wire Transfer',
    description:  'Outward wire transfer >= ₹25 lakh to any account',
    trigger: (txn: Transaction) =>
      txn.type === 'WIRE_OUT' && txn.amountPaise >= 2_500_000_00n,
    alertType:    'HIGH_VALUE_WIRE',
    priority:     'HIGH',
  },
];
```

**Category 2 — Structuring rules:**

Patterns designed to detect amounts deliberately kept just below reporting thresholds — a classic money-laundering technique called "smurfing."

```typescript
const STRUCTURING_RULES = [
  {
    ruleId:   'STR_001',
    name:     'Just-Below-Threshold Single Transaction',
    description: 'Single transaction amount within 5% below ₹10L threshold',
    trigger: (txn: Transaction) => {
      const threshold = 1_000_000_00n;
      const margin    = threshold / 20n;  // 5%
      return txn.amountPaise >= threshold - margin
          && txn.amountPaise <  threshold
          && txn.channel === 'CASH';
    },
    alertType: 'POSSIBLE_STRUCTURING',
    priority:  'HIGH',
  },
  {
    ruleId:   'STR_002',
    name:     'Aggregate Just-Below-Threshold (7-day)',
    description: 'Sum of cash transactions in 7 days is >= ₹8L but each individual transaction < ₹10L',
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const sevenDayTotal = await db.raw(`
        SELECT COALESCE(SUM(amount_paise), 0) AS total
        FROM transactions
        WHERE customer_id = $1
          AND channel = 'CASH'
          AND created_at >= now() - interval '7 days'
      `, [customerId]);
      const total = BigInt(sevenDayTotal.rows[0].total);

      const maxSingle = await db.raw(`
        SELECT COALESCE(MAX(amount_paise), 0) AS max_single
        FROM transactions
        WHERE customer_id = $1
          AND channel = 'CASH'
          AND created_at >= now() - interval '7 days'
      `, [customerId]);
      const maxSingleTxn = BigInt(maxSingle.rows[0].max_single);

      return total >= 800_000_00n      // aggregate >= ₹8L
          && maxSingleTxn < 1_000_000_00n;  // no single txn crossed ₹10L
    },
    alertType: 'AGGREGATE_STRUCTURING',
    priority:  'HIGH',
  },
];
```

**Category 3 — Velocity rules:**

Unusual transaction frequency or volume in a short period.

```typescript
const VELOCITY_RULES = [
  {
    ruleId:   'VEL_001',
    name:     'High Transaction Frequency (24h)',
    description: 'More than 20 transactions in any 24-hour rolling window',
    lookbackHours: 24,
    threshold:     20,
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const count = await db('transactions')
        .where({ customer_id: customerId })
        .where('created_at', '>=', new Date(Date.now() - 86_400_000))
        .count('* as count')
        .first();
      return Number(count.count) > 20;
    },
    alertType: 'HIGH_VELOCITY',
    priority:  'MEDIUM',
  },
  {
    ruleId:   'VEL_002',
    name:     'Sudden Volume Spike',
    description: 'Monthly transaction volume 3x or more above the customer\'s 6-month average',
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const sixMonthAvg = await getMonthlyAvgVolume(db, customerId, 6);
      const thisMonth   = await getCurrentMonthVolume(db, customerId);
      return sixMonthAvg > 0n && thisMonth >= sixMonthAvg * 3n;
    },
    alertType: 'VOLUME_SPIKE',
    priority:  'MEDIUM',
  },
];
```

**Category 4 — Pattern rules:**

Behavioural patterns associated with specific money laundering typologies.

```typescript
const PATTERN_RULES = [
  {
    ruleId:   'PAT_001',
    name:     'Round Dollar Transactions',
    description: 'Three or more transactions in 30 days with amounts that are exact multiples of ₹1L',
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const count = await db('transactions')
        .where({ customer_id: customerId })
        .whereRaw('amount_paise % 10000000 = 0')  // multiple of ₹1L (1_000_000_00 paise)
        .where('created_at', '>=', new Date(Date.now() - 30 * 86_400_000))
        .count('* as count')
        .first();
      return Number(count.count) >= 3;
    },
    alertType: 'ROUND_NUMBER_PATTERN',
    priority:  'MEDIUM',
  },
  {
    ruleId:   'PAT_002',
    name:     'Dormant Account Sudden Activity',
    description: 'Account inactive for 6+ months suddenly processes transactions > ₹1L in 7 days',
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const lastTxnBefore6Months = await db('transactions')
        .where({ customer_id: customerId })
        .where('created_at', '<', new Date(Date.now() - 180 * 86_400_000))
        .orderBy('created_at', 'desc')
        .first();
      if (!lastTxnBefore6Months) return false;  // never had transactions before

      const recentTotal = await db('transactions')
        .where({ customer_id: customerId })
        .where('created_at', '>=', new Date(Date.now() - 7 * 86_400_000))
        .sum('amount_paise as total')
        .first();
      return BigInt(recentTotal.total ?? 0) >= 100_000_00n;
    },
    alertType: 'DORMANT_ACCOUNT_ACTIVITY',
    priority:  'HIGH',
  },
  {
    ruleId:   'PAT_003',
    name:     'Pass-Through Activity',
    description: 'Funds received and sent out within 48 hours with < 10% retained',
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const fortyEightHoursAgo = new Date(Date.now() - 48 * 3_600_000);
      const inflow  = await sumTransactions(db, customerId, 'CREDIT', fortyEightHoursAgo);
      const outflow = await sumTransactions(db, customerId, 'DEBIT',  fortyEightHoursAgo);
      if (inflow === 0n) return false;
      const retentionRate = Number(inflow - outflow) / Number(inflow);
      return inflow >= 100_000_00n && retentionRate < 0.10;
    },
    alertType: 'PASS_THROUGH',
    priority:  'HIGH',
  },
];
```

**Category 5 — Sanctions and watchlist rules:**

Real-time screening against government-published lists. These are synchronous and must run before a transaction is approved.

```typescript
const SANCTIONS_RULES = [
  {
    ruleId:   'SAN_001',
    name:     'UN Sanctions List Match',
    description: 'Transaction counterparty name or account matches UN consolidated sanctions list',
    // Screening runs synchronously via sanctions screening service
    // Fuzzy name matching with configurable match threshold
    matchThreshold: 0.85,  // 85% name similarity triggers a hold
    alertType: 'SANCTIONS_MATCH',
    priority:  'CRITICAL',
    action:    'BLOCK',   // transaction held pending compliance review
  },
  {
    ruleId:   'SAN_002',
    name:     'PEP Transaction — Enhanced Due Diligence',
    description: 'Any transaction where customer is classified as a PEP',
    trigger: (customerId: string, pepRegistry: PEPRegistry) =>
      pepRegistry.isPEP(customerId),
    alertType: 'PEP_TRANSACTION',
    priority:  'HIGH',
    action:    'ALERT',   // do not block; flag for enhanced review
  },
  {
    ruleId:   'SAN_003',
    name:     'FATF High-Risk Country Transfer',
    description: 'Cross-border transfer to/from FATF grey or black-listed country',
    trigger: (txn: Transaction, fatfList: FATFCountryList) =>
      fatfList.isHighRisk(txn.destinationCountryCode),
    alertType: 'HIGH_RISK_COUNTRY',
    priority:  'HIGH',
    action:    'ALERT',
  },
];
```

**Category 6 — Network / relationship rules:**

Graph-based rules that look at relationships between accounts, not just individual account behaviour.

```typescript
const NETWORK_RULES = [
  {
    ruleId:   'NET_001',
    name:     'Money Mule Network Detection',
    description: 'Account receives funds from 10+ distinct sources and sends to 1–3 destinations',
    trigger: async (customerId: string, db: DB) => {
      const inboundSources = await db('transactions')
        .where({ receiver_id: customerId })
        .where('created_at', '>=', new Date(Date.now() - 30 * 86_400_000))
        .countDistinct('sender_id as sources')
        .first();

      const outboundDestinations = await db('transactions')
        .where({ sender_id: customerId })
        .where('created_at', '>=', new Date(Date.now() - 30 * 86_400_000))
        .countDistinct('receiver_id as dests')
        .first();

      return Number(inboundSources.sources)      >= 10
          && Number(outboundDestinations.dests)  <= 3;
    },
    alertType: 'MONEY_MULE_PATTERN',
    priority:  'CRITICAL',
  },
];
```

**Q6. How do you implement a rules engine that is maintainable, testable, and auditable?**

A: Hardcoding rules in application code is maintainable only for small rule sets. At scale, rules must be configurable without deployments, individually enable/disable-able, and versionable (so you know which rule version was active when an alert was generated).

**Architecture — rule registry with database-backed configuration:**

```sql
CREATE TABLE aml_rules (
    rule_id          TEXT        PRIMARY KEY,   -- 'VEL_001', 'STR_002', etc.
    rule_name        TEXT        NOT NULL,
    category         TEXT        NOT NULL,      -- 'THRESHOLD' | 'STRUCTURING' | 'VELOCITY' | 'PATTERN' | 'SANCTIONS' | 'NETWORK'
    description      TEXT        NOT NULL,
    rule_config      JSONB       NOT NULL,      -- thresholds, lookback windows, etc.
    alert_type       TEXT        NOT NULL,
    priority         TEXT        NOT NULL,      -- 'LOW' | 'MEDIUM' | 'HIGH' | 'CRITICAL'
    action_on_trigger TEXT       NOT NULL,      -- 'ALERT' | 'BLOCK' | 'CTR_REQUIRED'
    is_enabled       BOOLEAN     NOT NULL DEFAULT true,
    effective_from   TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    effective_to     TIMESTAMPTZ,               -- null = currently active
    version          INT         NOT NULL DEFAULT 1,
    created_by       TEXT        NOT NULL,
    created_at       TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);

-- Example rule config entries
INSERT INTO aml_rules VALUES (
  'VEL_001', 'High Transaction Frequency (24h)', 'VELOCITY',
  'More than N transactions in 24h rolling window',
  '{"lookbackHours": 24, "transactionCountThreshold": 20}',
  'HIGH_VELOCITY', 'MEDIUM', 'ALERT', true, now(), null, 1, 'compliance_team', now()
);
```

**Rule engine execution:**

```typescript
class AMLRulesEngine {
  private ruleRegistry: Map<string, AMLRule> = new Map();

  async loadRules(): Promise<void> {
    const activeRules = await db('aml_rules')
      .where({ is_enabled: true })
      .whereNull('effective_to')
      .orWhere('effective_to', '>', new Date());

    for (const rule of activeRules) {
      this.ruleRegistry.set(rule.rule_id, {
        ...rule,
        evaluator: this.buildEvaluator(rule),
      });
    }
  }

  async evaluate(
    transaction: Transaction,
    customer:    Customer,
  ): Promise<AMLAlert[]> {
    const alerts: AMLAlert[] = [];

    for (const [ruleId, rule] of this.ruleRegistry) {
      try {
        const triggered = await rule.evaluator(transaction, customer);

        if (triggered) {
          const alert = await this.createAlert(rule, transaction, customer);
          alerts.push(alert);

          // If action is BLOCK — throw to halt transaction processing
          if (rule.action_on_trigger === 'BLOCK') {
            throw new TransactionBlockedError(
              `Transaction blocked by AML rule ${ruleId}`,
              alerts,
            );
          }
        }
      } catch (err) {
        if (err instanceof TransactionBlockedError) throw err;
        // Rule evaluation failure must not block the transaction
        // Log and continue — fail-open for monitoring rules, fail-closed for sanctions
        logger.error({ ruleId, err }, 'AML rule evaluation failed');
        if (rule.category === 'SANCTIONS') throw err;  // sanctions must never fail silently
      }
    }

    return alerts;
  }

  private async createAlert(
    rule:        AMLRule,
    transaction: Transaction,
    customer:    Customer,
  ): Promise<AMLAlert> {
    const alert = {
      alert_id:       generateUUID(),
      rule_id:        rule.rule_id,
      rule_version:   rule.version,
      transaction_id: transaction.transactionId,
      customer_id:    customer.customerId,
      alert_type:     rule.alert_type,
      priority:       rule.priority,
      status:         'OPEN',
      triggered_at:   new Date(),
      context_data:   this.buildAlertContext(rule, transaction, customer),
    };

    await db('aml_alerts').insert(alert);
    return alert;
  }
}
```

**Auditability requirement:** Every alert must store `rule_id` AND `rule_version` — so that if the rule changes in the future, you can still explain exactly what the rule said when it fired. This is a regulatory requirement: compliance teams must be able to reconstruct the rationale for any past alert during an audit.

**Q7. How do you handle the false positive problem in AML rule-based monitoring?**

A: The false positive problem is the defining operational challenge of AML monitoring. A rule that flags too aggressively buries analysts in noise; one that is too permissive misses real threats.

**Measuring false positive rate:**

```typescript
interface AlertDispositionMetrics {
  ruleId:           string;
  period:           string;    // e.g., '2024-01'
  totalAlerts:      number;
  truePositives:    number;    // STR filed
  falsePositives:   number;    // cleared as not suspicious
  pendingReview:    number;
  falsePositiveRate: number;   // falsePositives / (truePositives + falsePositives)
  avgReviewTimeHours: number;
}

async function computeAlertMetrics(ruleId: string, month: string): Promise<AlertDispositionMetrics> {
  const result = await db.raw(`
    SELECT
      rule_id,
      COUNT(*)                                                      AS total_alerts,
      COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE disposition = 'STR_FILED')            AS true_positives,
      COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE disposition = 'FALSE_POSITIVE')       AS false_positives,
      COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE disposition IS NULL)                   AS pending_review,
      AVG(EXTRACT(EPOCH FROM (reviewed_at - triggered_at))/3600)   AS avg_review_hours
    FROM aml_alerts
    WHERE rule_id = $1
      AND DATE_TRUNC('month', triggered_at) = $2::DATE
    GROUP BY rule_id
  `, [ruleId, month + '-01']);

  const row = result.rows[0];
  const total = Number(row.true_positives) + Number(row.false_positives);
  return {
    ...row,
    falsePositiveRate: total > 0 ? Number(row.false_positives) / total : 0,
  };
}
```

**Tuning strategies:**

- **Whitelist / suppress for known-good:** Regular salary credits, utility bill payments, government disbursements — these match velocity rules but are low-risk. Add a `transaction_category` field and suppress alerts for whitelisted categories.
- **Risk-tier the thresholds:** Apply different thresholds per customer risk tier. A HIGH-risk customer triggers VEL_001 at 5 transactions/day; a LOW-risk customer triggers it at 50.
- **Time-based context:** A transaction at 3 AM on a weekend may be more suspicious than the same transaction at 2 PM on a weekday. Add time-of-day as a signal modifier.
- **Alert suppression window:** If the same customer triggered the same rule 3 days ago and it was cleared as a false positive, suppress the same rule for 7 days (configurable) — don't re-alert on the same pattern immediately.

```sql
-- Alert suppression: don't re-alert if same rule was cleared as FP in last N days
CREATE TABLE alert_suppressions (
    customer_id      TEXT    NOT NULL,
    rule_id          TEXT    NOT NULL,
    suppressed_until TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL,
    created_at       TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
    PRIMARY KEY (customer_id, rule_id)
);
```

## Section 3: Suspicious Transaction Reports (STRs)

**Q8. What is an STR, what must it contain, and what triggers the obligation to file one?**

A: A **Suspicious Transaction Report (STR)** is a mandatory filing with FIU-IND when a Reporting Entity has reason to believe, or has reason to suspect, that a transaction involves the proceeds of crime or is related to terrorist financing.

**The filing threshold is subjective — there is no minimum amount.** A ₹500 transaction that shows clear signs of terrorist financing must be filed as an STR. A ₹50 lakh transaction that has a clear legitimate explanation need not be. This subjectivity is intentional — AML law requires the Reporting Entity to exercise judgement, not just apply mechanical rules.

**STR content requirements (under PMLA Rules, Rule 7):**

```typescript
interface STRFiling {
  // Reporting entity details
  reportingEntityName:    string;
  reportingEntityCode:    string;    // FIU-IND assigned code
  principalOfficerName:   string;
  filingDate:             Date;
  reportReferenceNumber:  string;    // your internal reference

  // Suspicious activity details
  suspicionCategory:      SuspicionCategory;   // see below
  suspicionDescription:   string;    // narrative: why is this suspicious?
  suspicionFirstDetected: Date;      // when did you become aware?
  activityPeriodFrom:     Date;
  activityPeriodTo:       Date;

  // Transactions involved
  transactions: Array<{
    transactionId:          string;
    transactionDate:        Date;
    transactionType:        string;
    amountPaise:            bigint;
    currency:               string;
    channel:                string;
    senderAccountNumber:    string;
    senderBankIFSC:         string;
    receiverAccountNumber:  string;
    receiverBankIFSC:       string;
  }>;

  // Subject (customer / entity being reported)
  subjects: Array<{
```

**Q9. What is the STR workflow — from alert to filing? Who does what, and what does the system enforce?**

A: The STR workflow has four stages: alert generation, analyst review, escalation decision, and filing.

```
Stage 1 — Alert Generation (automated)
  Transaction monitoring engine fires a rule
  System creates an AML alert record (status: OPEN)
  Alert routed to analyst queue based on priority and customer risk tier
  SLA clock starts: CRITICAL alerts must be reviewed within 4 hours,
                    HIGH within 24 hours, MEDIUM within 72 hours

Stage 2 — Analyst Review (human)
  Analyst claims the alert from the queue
  System locks the alert to that analyst (prevents double-review)
  Analyst reviews:
    - Transaction details and history
    - Customer KYC profile, PEP status, adverse media
    - Related transactions (same customer, same counterparty)
    - Customer's explanation (if they were contacted — "enquiry" stage)
  Analyst documents their findings in the alert record

Stage 3 — Disposition Decision (human + system)
  Analyst makes one of three decisions:
    a. FALSE_POSITIVE: not suspicious; close the alert; note reasoning
    b. SAR_PENDING: suspicious but needs more information; escalate for 30 days
    c. STR_FILE: suspicious; proceed to STR preparation

Stage 4 — STR Preparation and Filing (human + system)
  If STR_FILE:
    - System pre-populates the STR form from the alert + customer + transaction data
    - Analyst reviews, amends, and adds the suspicion narrative
    - Compliance Manager / Principal Officer approves
    - System submits via FIU-IND's goAML portal API
    - System records the FIU-IND acknowledgement number
    - Alert status → STR_FILED; STR record created
  CRITICAL: No "tip-off" — the customer must NOT be informed that an STR has been filed.
            System must not send any customer-facing communication after STR_FILE decision.
```

**Q10. What is a CTR (Cash Transaction Report), and how does it differ from an STR?**

A: A **CTR (Cash Transaction Report)** is a mandatory, mechanical filing — unlike an STR, which requires human judgement. Any cash transaction (or series of cash transactions by the same customer in a calendar month) exceeding ₹10 lakh must be reported to FIU-IND, regardless of whether the transaction is suspicious.

| Property | STR | CTR |
|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Suspicion of money laundering or terrorist financing | Cash transaction(s) >= ₹10 lakh per customer per month |
| **Threshold** | No minimum amount | ₹10 lakh (mandatory) |
| **Human judgement required** | Yes — analyst must decide to file | No — system files automatically when threshold crossed |
| **Filing deadline** | 7 working days after decision to file | By 15th of the following calendar month |
| **Frequency** | Per suspicious event | Monthly aggregate per customer |
| **Content** | Narrative + evidence | Structured data: customer + transactions + amounts |
| **Confidentiality** | Yes — cannot tip off customer | Yes — same tipping-off prohibition applies |

**CTR automation:**

```typescript
async function generateMonthlyCTRs(month: Date): Promise<void> {

  const endOfMonth   = endOfMonth(month);

  const eligibleCustomers = await db.raw(`
    SELECT
        customer_id,

      AND created_at <  $2
```

```typescript
// Processes each transaction event from Kafka
// Maintains per-customer rolling windows in Redis
class VelocityRuleConsumer {
  async processTransaction(txn: Transaction): Promise<void> {
    const windowKey = `velocity:${txn.customerId}`;

    // Add transaction to rolling window in Redis
    await redis.zadd(
      windowKey,
      Date.now(),         // score = timestamp
      JSON.stringify({ txnId: txn.transactionId, amountPaise: txn.amountPaise }),
    );

    // Expire old entries outside the 24-hour window
    await redis.zremrangebyscore(windowKey, 0, Date.now() - 86_400_000);
    await redis.expire(windowKey, 86_400 * 2);  // TTL: 2 days

    // Count transactions in last 24h
    const count24h = await redis.zcard(windowKey);

    if (count24h > VELOCITY_THRESHOLD_24H) {
      await this.raiseAlert('VEL_001', txn, { count24h });
    }

    // Sum amount in last 24h
    const entries = await redis.zrangebyscore(windowKey, Date.now() - 86_400_000, '+inf');
    const total24h = entries.reduce((sum, e) => {
      const parsed = JSON.parse(e);
      return sum + BigInt(parsed.amountPaise);
    }, 0n);

    if (total24h >= AMOUNT_THRESHOLD_24H) {
      await this.raiseAlert('VEL_003', txn, { total24h: total24h.toString() });
    }
  }
}
```

**Q12. How do you implement sanctions screening with sub-200ms latency?**

A: Sanctions screening must run synchronously before a transaction is approved, which means it must complete in well under 200ms. Querying a remote API per transaction is too slow and creates a single point of failure. The solution is a locally cached, in-memory screening service.

**Architecture:**

```typescript
class SanctionsScreeningService {
  private sanctionsIndex: FuzzySearchIndex;
  private lastSyncAt:     Date;
  private syncIntervalMs = 3_600_000;  // re-sync from source every hour

  async initialize(): Promise<void> {
    await this.syncSanctionsList();
    setInterval(() => this.syncSanctionsList(), this.syncIntervalMs);
  }

  private async syncSanctionsList(): Promise<void> {
    // Download from UN, OFAC, MHA consolidated lists
    const lists = await Promise.all([
      this.downloadUNList(),
      this.downloadOFACList(),
      this.downloadMHAList(),
    ]);

    const allEntries = lists.flat();
    this.sanctionsIndex = buildFuzzyIndex(allEntries);
    this.lastSyncAt     = new Date();

    logger.info({ entriesLoaded: allEntries.length }, 'Sanctions list synced');
  }

  async screen(params: {
    name:           string;
    accountNumber?: string;
    panNumber?:     string;
    country?:       string;
  }): Promise<SanctionsScreeningResult> {
    const start = Date.now();

    // 1. Exact match on PAN / account number (fastest path)
    if (params.panNumber) {
      const exactMatch = this.sanctionsIndex.lookupByPAN(params.panNumber);
      if (exactMatch) return { matched: true, matchType: 'EXACT_PAN', entry: exactMatch };
    }

    // 2. Fuzzy name match (Jaro-Winkler similarity, threshold = 0.85)
    const nameMatches = this.sanctionsIndex.fuzzyMatchName(params.name, 0.85);
    if (nameMatches.length > 0) {
      return {
        matched:   true,
        matchType: 'FUZZY_NAME',
        score:     nameMatches[0].score,
        entry:     nameMatches[0].entry,
      };
    }

    const latencyMs = Date.now() - start;
    metrics.histogram('sanctions_screening_latency_ms', latencyMs);

    return { matched: false };
  }
}
```

**Handling false positives in sanctions screening:**

Name-matching produces false positives — "Mohammed Ali" may match many innocent customers. The workflow:

1. Fuzzy match above 0.85 threshold → alert type: `POSSIBLE_SANCTIONS_MATCH`, transaction held.
2. Analyst reviews within 4 hours.
3. If not a true match → whitelist the customer-against-list-entry pair; release the held transaction.
4. If true match → freeze account, file STR, notify FIU-IND.

**Q13. How do you handle data retention for AML records, and what are the regulatory requirements?**

A: PMLA's record retention requirements are strict and have legal consequences:

**PMLA retention requirements:**

```
Rule 10 of PMLA (Maintenance of Records) Rules 2005:

1. Records of transactions (identity + transaction details):
   Retain for 5 years from the date of the transaction.

2. Records of account files and business correspondence:
   Retain for 5 years after the cessation of the business relationship.

3. STR and CTR records:
   FIU-IND recommends retaining for 10 years (beyond the 5-year legal minimum).

4. KYC records used for CDD:
   Retain for 5 years after the business relationship ends.

Records must be retrievable within 48 hours of a regulatory request.
```

## Section 5: Scenario Questions

**Q14. "Design a transaction monitoring system for a UPI payment platform processing 10 million transactions per day."**

A: At 10M transactions/day (~116 transactions/second average, peaking higher), every architectural decision has a performance implication.

**Scale constraints:**

```
10M transactions/day
  = ~116 TPS average
  = ~500–1000 TPS peak (payment peaks in morning + evening)
  
Synchronous screening SLA: < 200ms per transaction
Near-real-time velocity check SLA: < 5 seconds after transaction
Batch rules SLA: complete within 2 hours of day-end

Alert volume estimate:
  If 0.5% of transactions trigger an alert = 50,000 alerts/day
  If analysts can review 50 alerts/analyst/day → 1,000 analysts needed (unrealistic)
  Target: 0.05% trigger rate = 5,000 alerts/day → 100 analysts
  Target FP rate: < 90% (i.e., > 10% of alerts are true positives or SAR_PENDING)
```

**Architecture decisions:**

```
1. Synchronous screening (in-process, per transaction):
   - In-memory sanctions index updated hourly from list source
   - Redis for PEP flag lookups (< 1ms)
   - Single-transaction threshold check (pure computation, < 1ms)
   - Total synchronous latency target: < 50ms
   - Scale: stateless service, horizontally scalable

2. Near-real-time velocity (Kafka Streams, per event):
   - Transaction published to Kafka on approval
   - Velocity consumer: Redis sorted sets for rolling window counts
   - One Kafka partition per customer hash bucket (ensures order per customer)
   - Consumer group scaled to 50 instances for 10M/day throughput
   - Alert latency: < 30 seconds from transaction

3. Batch pattern rules (nightly Postgres batch jobs):
   - Structuring detection: SQL window functions over 7-day/30-day windows
   - Dormant account detection: JOIN on last_transaction_date
   - Pass-through detection: aggregate inflow/outflow per customer per 48h window
   - Run time: target < 2 hours on 10M records (requires index optimization)

4. Network analysis (weekly batch, Apache Spark):
   - Build transaction graph: nodes = accounts, edges = transactions
   - Detect money mule clusters: high in-degree + low out-degree nodes
   - Community detection algorithms (Louvain, Leiden)
   - Output: risk scores written back to customer risk profiles

5. Alert management:
   - Priority queue: CRITICAL alerts go to dedicated queue, reviewed < 4h
   - Auto-triage: ML model pre-scores each alert (FP probability)
   - Alerts with FP probability > 0.95 auto-closed after supervisor review
   - Dashboard: real-time alert counts, SLA breach metrics, analyst workload
```

**Q15. "A compliance analyst raises a concern: the same customer has triggered the same AML rule 15 times in the past 3 months, been cleared as a false positive every time, but the rule keeps firing. What's wrong and how do you fix it?"**

A: This is the false positive feedback loop — the rule is misconfigured for this customer's legitimate behaviour pattern.

**Root cause analysis:**

```sql
-- Step 1: understand why this customer keeps triggering the rule
SELECT
    a.alert_id,
    a.triggered_at,
    a.context_data,
    a.disposition,
    a.disposition_notes
FROM aml_alerts a
WHERE a.customer_id = $1
  AND a.rule_id     = $2
ORDER BY a.triggered_at DESC
LIMIT 20;

-- Step 2: understand what the customer's behaviour actually looks like
SELECT
    DATE_TRUNC('day', created_at) AS day,
    COUNT(*)                       AS txn_count,
    SUM(amount_paise)              AS daily_volume
FROM transactions
WHERE customer_id = $1
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1 DESC
LIMIT 90;
```

**Common findings and fixes:**

| Finding | Root cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Customer is a business with legitimately high transaction volume | Rule threshold designed for individuals | Add customer segment to rule config; apply different thresholds for `BUSINESS` vs `INDIVIDUAL` accounts |
| Customer's salary arrives on the same date every month, triggering a pattern rule | Rule doesn't exclude regular salary credits | Add transaction category exclusion: `WHERE NOT (type = 'SALARY_CREDIT')` |
| Customer is a PEP (politician), which elevates risk score, which lowers alert threshold below their normal volume | Correct classification but threshold is too aggressive for verified-legitimate PEPs | Create a PEP whitelist for known-legitimate behaviour; raise their individual threshold after EDD (Enhanced Due Diligence) |
| Rule STR_002 fires because the customer makes 8 cash deposits of ₹99,999 per month | Potential structuring — but analyst cleared it 15 times | This should NOT be auto-suppressed. Escalate to Principal Officer. 15 false positive clearances for a structuring pattern is itself suspicious (analyst may be colluding). |

**The last case is critical:** A pattern that has been cleared as a false positive repeatedly but keeps recurring is a red flag — either the analyst is wrong (structuring is actually happening) or the analyst is compromised. The system should flag when the same analyst clears the same pattern more than N times for the same customer without supervisor review.

| **Fail-closed (sanctions)** | Block transaction if sanctions service is unavailable | Sanctions must never fail silently; regulatory and reputational risk |
| **Fail-open (monitoring rules)** | Allow transaction if monitoring rule evaluation fails | Monitoring failures should not halt transactions; alert ops team |
| **Record retention** | 5 years (PMLA); 10 years (STR/CTR best practice) | Retrievable within 48h of regulatory request |
| **False positive rate** | FP / (TP + FP) per rule | Key metric; target < 90% FP rate; drives analyst workload |
| **Alert SLA** | CRITICAL: 4h; HIGH: 24h; MEDIUM: 72h | Missed SLAs reported to Principal Officer; drives escalation |
| **Repeated clearance flag** | Alert supervisor when same analyst clears same rule for same customer 5+ times | Internal control against analyst collusion or error |

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level in a fintech with AML obligations, these questions appear in three forms: (1) "how does your platform detect money laundering?" — the answer must cover both real-time (sanctions screening, threshold rules) and batch (pattern rules, network analysis), and explicitly distinguish between synchronous blocking (sanctions) and asynchronous alerting (velocity/pattern); (2) "what happens when an AML rule fires?" — the answer is: create an alert, route to analyst queue, analyst reviews, disposes as FP or escalates to STR, STR filed via goAML within 7 working days, no tip-off to customer; (3) "design an AML system for X TPS" — the key insight is the two-path architecture: synchronous in-memory screening pre-transaction (< 200ms), and asynchronous Kafka/batch monitoring post-transaction. Know the PMLA filing deadlines cold — 7 working days for STR, 15th of the following month for CTR. Know that structuring is itself a crime, not just suspicious behaviour — the STR obligation is triggered by the pattern, not just the amounts. And always anchor system design decisions to the false positive rate: an AML system that generates 50,000 alerts/day at a 99% FP rate is not an AML system — it is analyst-burnout as a service.
