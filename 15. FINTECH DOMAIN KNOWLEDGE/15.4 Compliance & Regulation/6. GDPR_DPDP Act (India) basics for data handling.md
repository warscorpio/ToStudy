# Compliance: GDPR & DPDP Act — Data Privacy, Consent, Rights & Engineering Obligations — Interview Q&A (5 YOE Level)

> **Domain:** Fintech / Compliance & Data Engineering (India + Global)

---

## Section 1: Fundamentals

**Q1. What is data privacy law, and why does a backend engineer need to understand it?**

A: Data privacy law governs how organisations collect, store, process, share, and delete personal information belonging to individuals. It creates enforceable rights for individuals and corresponding obligations for organisations that handle their data.

A backend engineer needs to understand it for three concrete reasons:

- **Privacy requirements are system requirements.** "Delete all data for this user within 30 days of request" is not a policy document — it is a feature that must be designed into the data model, the deletion pipeline, the backup strategy, and the audit log from day one. Retrofitting it into a system that was built without it is one of the most expensive engineering tasks in fintech.
- **Violations are detected in code review and architecture review, not in legal review.** A lawyer reviewing a privacy policy cannot tell you that your analytics pipeline copies raw PAN numbers into a data warehouse that has no deletion mechanism. An engineer who understands the law can.
- **Personal liability is real.** Under India's DPDP Act 2023, penalties up to ₹250 crore per instance of non-compliance can be imposed on the Data Fiduciary (the company). Under GDPR, fines up to 4% of global annual turnover apply. Engineering decisions — like logging user PII in plaintext, or retaining data beyond the stated purpose — directly contribute to these violations.

The practical knowledge a 5 YOE engineer needs is not the full text of the law, but the engineering obligations it creates: consent collection and storage, data minimisation, purpose limitation, right-to-erasure implementation, data localisation, breach notification timelines, and cross-border transfer controls.

---

**Q2. What are GDPR and the DPDP Act, and how do they relate to each other?**

A: **GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation)** is a European Union regulation that came into force on 25 May 2018. It applies to any organisation — anywhere in the world — that processes the personal data of individuals located in the EU/EEA, regardless of where the organisation is based. A fintech company in India that has EU users, or that processes data of EU residents, is subject to GDPR.

**DPDP Act (Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023)** is India's national data protection law, enacted in August 2023 and progressively coming into force as rules are notified by the central government. It applies to processing of digital personal data of individuals (Data Principals) in India, and to processing outside India if it involves offering goods or services to individuals in India.

**How they relate:**

| Dimension | GDPR | DPDP Act 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| **Jurisdiction** | EU/EEA data subjects; global extra-territorial reach | India; extra-territorial for offering services to India residents |
| **Regulator** | Data Protection Authority of each EU member state; coordinated by EDPB | Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) — yet to be constituted as of 2024 |
| **Max penalty** | €20M or 4% of global annual turnover (whichever higher) | ₹250 crore per instance; ₹200 crore for breach notification failure |
| **Legal basis for processing** | 6 lawful bases (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interest, public task, legitimate interest) | Consent + certain legitimate uses (narrower than GDPR) |
| **Data subject rights** | 8 rights (access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, objection, automated decision-making, etc.) | 5 rights (access, correction, erasure, grievance redressal, nominee) |
| **Data localisation** | No blanket localisation; adequacy decisions and SCCs for transfers | Significant data fiduciaries: central government may restrict certain data from leaving India |
| **Breach notification** | 72 hours to supervisory authority; without undue delay to data subjects | DPBI and Data Principal "without delay" — specific timelines pending rules |
| **DPO requirement** | Mandatory for certain controllers/processors | Data Protection Officer not explicitly mandated; Consent Manager introduced |
| **Children's data** | Parental consent for under-16 (member state variation: 13–16) | Verifiable parental consent for under-18 |

**For a fintech engineer:** If your platform serves both Indian and EU users, you must comply with both simultaneously. In practice, building to GDPR's higher standard typically satisfies DPDP Act requirements as well — though DPDP Act has some India-specific obligations (localisation for significant data fiduciaries, government exemptions) that GDPR does not.

**Q3. What is "personal data" under each law, and what counts as sensitive personal data?**

A: **Personal data** is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person. Both laws use similar definitions but with differences:

**GDPR — Personal Data (Article 4):**
Any information that directly or indirectly identifies a natural person. Examples: name, email, IP address, cookie identifier, device ID, location data, online identifiers. The key test: can this data, alone or in combination with other data, identify a specific person?

**DPDP Act 2023 — Personal Data (Section 2(t)):**
Any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data. Applies specifically to **digital** personal data — data that is collected in digital form, or that is in non-digital form and subsequently digitised.

**Special categories (higher protection):**

Under GDPR — "Special categories of personal data" (Article 9) require explicit consent or specific legal basis:
- Racial or ethnic origin
- Political opinions
- Religious or philosophical beliefs
- Trade union membership
- Genetic data
- Biometric data (when used for unique identification)
- Health data
- Sex life or sexual orientation
- Criminal convictions and offences

Under DPDP Act — the Act does not explicitly define a "sensitive personal data" category as a separate tier (unlike the earlier PDPB 2019 draft). However, the rules (yet to be notified) may create such categories. Currently, India's IT (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures) Rules 2011 under the IT Act define sensitive personal data to include: passwords, financial data, health data, sexual orientation, biometric data, and similar.

**Q4. What is a "Data Fiduciary" under DPDP and a "Data Controller" under GDPR? What obligations do they carry?**

A: These are the central obligated entities in each law — the organisations that determine the purpose and means of data processing.

**GDPR — Data Controller (Article 4(7)):**
A natural or legal person, public authority, agency, or other body which, alone or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of personal data.

**DPDP Act — Data Fiduciary (Section 2(i)):**
Any person who, alone or in conjunction with other persons, determines the purpose and means of processing of personal data.

Both definitions are functionally identical. A fintech company that collects customer data to provide lending services is the Data Controller (GDPR) / Data Fiduciary (DPDP Act) for that data.

**Processor / Data Processor:**
An entity that processes data on behalf of the Controller/Fiduciary — following their instructions, not making independent decisions about the data. Examples: a cloud hosting provider (AWS), a KYC service (CKYC Registry), a payment gateway (Razorpay). The Controller/Fiduciary remains responsible; the processor must operate under a data processing agreement.

**Significant Data Fiduciary (DPDP Act):**
A category of Data Fiduciary designated by the central government based on volume of data, sensitivity, risk to Data Principal, national security implications, or impact on sovereignty. Significant Data Fiduciaries face additional obligations:
- Appoint a Data Protection Officer (India-based)
- Conduct periodic Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)
- Conduct periodic audits by independent auditor
- Restrictions on cross-border data transfer (to be specified in rules)

Large fintechs, payment aggregators, and credit bureaus are likely to be designated as Significant Data Fiduciaries.

**Core obligations of a Data Fiduciary / Controller:**

```
1. Lawful basis: collect data only with valid consent or legitimate use
2. Purpose limitation: use data only for the purpose it was collected for
3. Data minimisation: collect only what is necessary for the stated purpose
4. Storage limitation: retain data only as long as necessary
5. Accuracy: keep personal data accurate and up to date
6. Security: implement appropriate technical and organisational measures
7. Accountability: demonstrate compliance (maintain records, conduct DPIAs)
8. Rights fulfilment: honour Data Principal rights (access, correction, erasure, portability)
9. Breach notification: notify regulator and individuals of data breaches
10. Children's data: verifiable parental consent for minors
```

## Section 2: Consent & Legal Basis

**Q5. What is valid consent under GDPR and DPDP Act, and how do you implement it technically?**

A: Consent is the most commonly relied-upon lawful basis for processing personal data, but both laws impose strict requirements on what makes consent valid.

**GDPR requirements for valid consent (Article 7 + Recital 32):**
- **Freely given:** No bundling consent with a service ("accept all cookies to use our app"). No imbalance of power that makes refusal impossible.
- **Specific:** Separate consent for each purpose — one checkbox for "everything" is invalid.
- **Informed:** The Data Subject must know who is collecting, what is collected, why, and for how long.
- **Unambiguous:** Requires a clear affirmative act — pre-ticked boxes are invalid. Silence is not consent.
- **Withdrawable:** Must be as easy to withdraw as to give. Withdrawal must be honoured immediately.
- **Documented:** The Controller must be able to demonstrate that consent was given.

**DPDP Act requirements for valid consent (Section 6):**
- Given through a **Consent Notice** that is clear, plain-language, and available in all scheduled Indian languages upon request.
- Specific — for a specific purpose.
- Must be accompanied by a notice describing: what personal data is to be collected, the purpose, the right to withdraw consent, and the grievance redressal mechanism.
- Withdrawable at any time; withdrawal must not be made more difficult than giving consent.
- A **Consent Manager** (a new concept in DPDP) may be used — a registered entity through which a Data Principal can give, manage, and withdraw consents across multiple Data Fiduciaries.

**Technical implementation:**


**Consent check in application code:**

```typescript
class ConsentService {
  async hasValidConsent(
    userId:      string,
    purposeCode: string,
  ): Promise<boolean> {
    const consent = await db('consent_records')
      .where({
        user_id:       userId,
        purpose_code:  purposeCode,
        consent_status: 'GRANTED',
      })
      .where(function () {
        this.whereNull('expires_at').orWhere('expires_at', '>', new Date());
      })
      .orderBy('granted_at', 'desc')
      .first();

    return !!consent;
  }

  async withdrawConsent(
    userId:      string,
    purposeCode: string,
    reason?:     string,
  ): Promise<void> {
    await db.transaction(async (trx) => {
      // Mark consent as withdrawn
      await trx('consent_records')
        .where({ user_id: userId, purpose_code: purposeCode, consent_status: 'GRANTED' })
        .update({ consent_status: 'WITHDRAWN', withdrawn_at: new Date() });

      // Audit trail
      await trx('consent_audit_log').insert({
        user_id:      userId,
        purpose_code: purposeCode,
        action:       'WITHDRAWN',
        reason,
        actioned_at:  new Date(),
      });

      // Trigger downstream: stop processing for this purpose
      await purposeProcessingQueue.enqueue({
        action:      'STOP_PROCESSING',
        userId,
        purposeCode,
        effectiveAt: new Date(),
      });
    });
  }
}
```

**Critical: what happens when consent is withdrawn?**

Withdrawal must stop the processing that relied on that consent. This is not just a database update — it must propagate to:
- Marketing email pipelines (remove from mailing lists)
- Analytics pipelines (stop ingesting this user's events)
- Third-party data sharing (notify third parties of withdrawal)
- Scheduled jobs that use this data (dequeue or skip)

Designing this propagation into the system from day one is far cheaper than retrofitting it.

**Q6. What are the other legal bases for processing under GDPR, and when does each apply in fintech?**

A: GDPR provides six lawful bases. DPDP Act provides consent plus a narrower set of "legitimate uses." Understanding which basis applies to each processing activity is critical — over-relying on consent creates withdrawal risk; under-relying on it creates compliance gaps.

**GDPR's six lawful bases and their fintech applications:**

**1. Consent (Article 6(1)(a)):**
- Use for: marketing communications, sending promotional offers, non-essential analytics, sharing data with partners for cross-sell.
- Key risk: withdrawable at any time. If your core product relies on consent, withdrawal breaks the product.

**2. Contract performance (Article 6(1)(b)):**
- Use for: processing necessary to perform the loan agreement — credit assessment, KYC verification, EMI collection, sending repayment schedules.
- This is the most important basis for fintech core processing. You do not need consent to process a borrower's PAN for KYC — you need it to comply with the loan contract and legal obligations.
- Key risk: scope-limited. You can only process what is actually necessary for the contract — not everything that might be useful.

**3. Legal obligation (Article 6(1)(c)):**
- Use for: AML/KYC (PMLA, RBI KYC Master Directions), tax reporting (Form 26AS, TDS filing), CIBIL reporting (RBI mandate), CTR/STR filing (PMLA).
- Regulatory obligations create a lawful basis — you must process the data to comply with the law. You do not need the customer's consent.
- Key advantage: cannot be blocked by a customer withdrawal request.

**4. Vital interests (Article 6(1)(d)):**
- Use for: detecting and preventing immediate physical harm. Extremely rare in fintech.

**5. Public task (Article 6(1)(e)):**
- Use for: processing by public authorities (government banks, RBI) in exercise of official authority.

**6. Legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)):**
- Use for: fraud detection and prevention, security monitoring, network integrity.
- Requires a balancing test: the legitimate interest must outweigh the Data Subject's privacy interest. Must be documented.
- Key risk in fintech: regulators scrutinise this basis carefully. "It's good for business" is not a legitimate interest. Fraud prevention and security are.

**DPDP Act — Legitimate uses (Section 7) beyond consent:**
- Performance of state functions by central/state government
- Compliance with legal obligations / court orders
- Medical emergencies or public health
- Employment-related processing
- Publicly available personal data (with caveats)

The narrower scope means that for most fintech processing beyond the contract and legal obligations, DPDP Act requires consent rather than relying on a broad "legitimate interests" catch-all.

**Q7. What is purpose limitation, and how do you enforce it technically?**

A: **Purpose limitation** means that personal data collected for one specific purpose must not be used for a different purpose without either a new legal basis or (if relying on consent) fresh consent for the new purpose.

**Classic violations in fintech:**
- Collecting a customer's phone number for OTP authentication, then using it for marketing SMS without separate consent.
- Collecting transaction data for fraud detection, then selling aggregated (but identifiable) behaviour profiles to advertisers.
- Using income data collected for loan underwriting to make algorithmic decisions about insurance pricing (a different product, different purpose).
- Importing KYC data from a partner for identity verification, then enriching your own user profile with it for credit scoring.

**Technical enforcement — purpose tagging:**

Every data access path must be tagged with the purpose it serves, and every data access must be validated against the user's active consents for that purpose.

```typescript
// Every data access goes through the purpose-aware data access layer
class PurposeAwareDataAccess {

  async getCustomerData(
    userId:      string,
    purpose:     ConsentPurpose,
    requestedFields: string[],
  ): Promise<Partial<CustomerData>> {
    // 1. Check that the caller has a valid lawful basis for this purpose
    const hasAccess = await this.checkLawfulBasis(userId, purpose);
    if (!hasAccess) {
      throw new PurposeLimitationError(
        `Access to customer data for purpose '${purpose}' denied: no valid lawful basis`
      );
    }

    // 2. Filter to only the fields allowed for this purpose
    const allowedFields = PURPOSE_FIELD_ALLOWLIST[purpose];
    const permittedFields = requestedFields.filter(f => allowedFields.includes(f));

    if (permittedFields.length < requestedFields.length) {
      const blocked = requestedFields.filter(f => !allowedFields.includes(f));
      logger.warn({ purpose, userId, blocked }, 'Fields blocked by purpose limitation');
    }

    // 3. Fetch only permitted fields
    const data = await db('customers')
      .where({ user_id: userId })
      .select(permittedFields);

    // 4. Audit log every access
    await this.logDataAccess(userId, purpose, permittedFields);

    return data[0];
  }

  private async checkLawfulBasis(userId: string, purpose: ConsentPurpose): Promise<boolean> {
    // Legal obligation and contract: always permitted
    if (LEGAL_OBLIGATION_PURPOSES.includes(purpose)) return true;
    if (CONTRACT_PURPOSES.includes(purpose)) return true;

    // Consent-based: check active consent record
    return this.consentService.hasValidConsent(userId, purpose);
  }
}

// Field allowlist per purpose: what data fields may be accessed for each purpose
const PURPOSE_FIELD_ALLOWLIST: Record<ConsentPurpose, string[]> = {
  'CREDIT_ASSESSMENT': [
    'pan_number', 'income_paise', 'employment_type',
    'existing_loans', 'credit_score', 'address',
  ],
  'KYC_VERIFICATION': [
    'full_name', 'date_of_birth', 'address', 'pan_number',
    'aadhaar_hash', 'photo_url',
  ],
  'MARKETING': [
    'full_name', 'email', 'phone_number', 'product_preferences',
    // NOT: income_paise, pan_number, credit_score, aadhaar_hash
  ],
  'EMI_COLLECTION': [
    'bank_account_number', 'ifsc_code', 'mandate_id',
  ],
};
```

## Section 3: Data Subject / Principal Rights

**Q8. What are the data subject rights, and what does each require from your system?**

A: Both GDPR and DPDP Act grant individuals rights over their personal data. Each right requires a specific engineering capability.

**Right 1 — Right of Access (GDPR Art. 15 / DPDP Act Sec. 11):**

The individual can request a copy of all personal data held about them, along with information about how it is being processed.

```typescript
async function generateSubjectAccessReport(userId: string): Promise<SARReport> {
  // Collect data from every system that holds personal data
  const [
    profile,
    transactions,
    loanApplications,
    consentRecords,
    auditLogs,
    communicationHistory,
    creditAssessmentData,
    kycDocuments,
  ] = await Promise.all([
    db('customers').where({ user_id: userId }).select('*'),
    db('transactions').where({ customer_id: userId }).select('*'),
    db('loan_applications').where({ applicant_id: userId }).select('*'),
    db('consent_records').where({ user_id: userId }).select('*'),
    db('audit_logs').where({ user_id: userId }).select('*'),
    db('communications').where({ user_id: userId }).select('*'),
    db('credit_assessments').where({ user_id: userId }).select('*'),
    listKYCDocuments(userId),
  ]);

  // Mask or exclude fields that would reveal third-party information
  // (e.g., other users' data that appears in transactions)
  const sanitisedTransactions = transactions.map(txn => ({
    ...txn,
    counterparty_name: maskCounterparty(txn.counterparty_id, userId),
  }));

  return {
    generatedAt:          new Date(),
    userId,
    profile:              maskSystemFields(profile[0]),
    transactions:         sanitisedTransactions,
    loanApplications,
    consentHistory:       consentRecords,
    auditTrail:           auditLogs,
    communicationHistory,
    creditAssessmentData: maskThirdPartyScores(creditAssessmentData),
    kycDocuments:         kycDocuments.map(d => ({ type: d.type, uploadedAt: d.created_at })),
    // Third-party sources: list the sources, not the data (to protect third-party rights)
    thirdPartySources: ['CIBIL', 'EXPERIAN', 'CKYC_REGISTRY'],
    processingPurposes: await getPurposesForUser(userId),
    retentionPolicies:  await getRetentionPoliciesForUser(userId),
  };
}
```

**Response timeline:** GDPR requires response within 30 calendar days (extendable to 90 days for complex requests with notice). DPDP Act timelines are pending rules — align to GDPR's 30 days as a safe baseline.

**Right 2 — Right to Rectification (GDPR Art. 16 / DPDP Act Sec. 12):**

The individual can request correction of inaccurate or incomplete data.

Engineering requirement: a correction workflow where the user submits a correction request, supporting evidence is optionally collected, the correction is applied across all systems holding that data field, and downstream systems (CIBIL reports, third-party integrations) are notified of the correction.

**Right 3 — Right to Erasure / Right to be Forgotten (GDPR Art. 17 / DPDP Act Sec. 12):**

The most technically complex right. The individual can request deletion of their personal data. This right is not absolute — legal obligations and contractual requirements can override it. See Q9 for full implementation.

**Right 4 — Right to Data Portability (GDPR Art. 20 — no direct equivalent in DPDP Act):**

The individual can request their personal data in a structured, machine-readable format to transfer to another service. Applies only to data processed by consent or contract, by automated means.

```typescript
async function generatePortabilityExport(userId: string): Promise<PortabilityPackage> {
  // Only data provided by the user or generated through their use of the service
  // NOT: derived data (credit scores, fraud scores), third-party data (CIBIL reports)
  const exportableData = {
    profile: await db('customers').where({ user_id: userId })
      .select(['full_name', 'email', 'phone_number', 'address', 'date_of_birth']),
    transactions: await db('transactions').where({ customer_id: userId })
      .select(['transaction_id', 'amount_paise', 'currency', 'created_at', 'description']),
    paymentMethods: await db('payment_methods').where({ user_id: userId })
      .select(['card_last4', 'card_brand', 'expiry_month', 'expiry_year']),
  };

  return {
    format:       'JSON',
    schema:       'https://schema.yourplatform.com/portability/v1',
    exportedAt:   new Date(),
    data:         exportableData,
  };
}
```

**Right 5 — Right to Object (GDPR Art. 21):**

The individual can object to processing based on legitimate interests or for direct marketing. For direct marketing, the objection is absolute and must be honoured immediately.

**Right 6 — Rights related to automated decision-making (GDPR Art. 22):**

The individual has the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that significantly affects them — including credit scoring, loan approval/rejection. They have the right to:
- Request human review of an automated decision.
- Obtain an explanation of the decision.
- Contest the decision.

**Q9. How do you implement the Right to Erasure (Right to be Forgotten)?**

A: The Right to Erasure is the most architecturally challenging right because personal data tends to proliferate across systems — primary databases, replicas, backups, data warehouses, analytics systems, audit logs, third-party integrations, and caches — and because some data cannot be deleted even when the right is invoked.

**When erasure can be refused (legitimate grounds to retain):**

```typescript
const ERASURE_OVERRIDE_REASONS = [
  'LEGAL_OBLIGATION',     // PMLA: 5-year transaction record retention
  'LEGAL_CLAIM',          // Active loan dispute; regulatory investigation
  'PUBLIC_INTEREST',      // AML/STR-related records
  'CONTRACT_ACTIVE',      // Active loan; cannot erase while debt outstanding
  'REGULATORY_AUDIT',     // Pending RBI/FIU-IND audit
];

async function assessErasureEligibility(userId: string): Promise<ErasureAssessment> {
  const [
    activeLoans,
    pendingDisputes,
    amlFlags,
    regulatoryHolds,
    activeConsents,
  ] = await Promise.all([
    db('loans').where({ customer_id: userId, status: 'ACTIVE' }).count(),
    db('disputes').where({ customer_id: userId, status: 'OPEN' }).count(),
    db('aml_alerts').where({ customer_id: userId, status: 'OPEN' }).count(),
    db('regulatory_holds').where({ customer_id: userId, active: true }).count(),
    db('consent_records').where({ user_id: userId, consent_status: 'GRANTED' }).count(),
  ]);

  const blocks: ErasureBlock[] = [];

  if (Number(activeLoans) > 0) {
    blocks.push({
      reason:        'LEGAL_OBLIGATION',
      description:   'Active loan exists; data required for contract performance and regulatory compliance',
      retentionUntil: 'LOAN_CLOSURE + 5 years (PMLA)',
    });
  }
  if (Number(amlFlags) > 0) {
    blocks.push({
      reason:        'LEGAL_OBLIGATION',
      description:   'Open AML alert; data required for PMLA compliance',
      retentionUntil: 'ALERT_RESOLUTION + 5 years',
    });
  }

  return {
    userId,
    isEligibleForErasure: blocks.length === 0,
    blocks,
    partialErasurePossible: blocks.length > 0,
    // Partial erasure: delete marketing data even if financial data must be retained
    partialErasureScope: blocks.length > 0 ? PARTIAL_ERASURE_SCOPE : FULL_ERASURE_SCOPE,
  };
}
```

**Erasure implementation — what actually happens:**

Deletion is rarely simple deletion. The correct approach depends on the data category:

```typescript
const ERASURE_STRATEGIES: Record<DataCategory, ErasureStrategy> = {
  // Truly delete — no retention obligation
  MARKETING_DATA:      'HARD_DELETE',
  SESSION_DATA:        'HARD_DELETE',
  DEVICE_TOKENS:       'HARD_DELETE',
  PREFERENCES:         'HARD_DELETE',
```

## Section 4: Data Localisation, Transfers & Security

**Q10. What is data localisation, and how does it affect fintech system architecture?**

A: **Data localisation** is a requirement to store or process personal data (or specific categories of it) within the borders of a specific country — prohibiting or restricting its transfer to servers or processors in other jurisdictions.

**India — DPDP Act 2023:**
The Act itself does not impose a blanket localisation requirement. However, Section 16 empowers the central government to restrict the transfer of personal data to specified countries or territories outside India. This means localisation requirements may be imposed by subordinate rules or government orders.

Currently (under the IT Act framework), RBI has imposed strict data localisation for payment data:

- **RBI Payment Data Localisation Circular (2018, reinforced 2019):** All data related to payment systems (full end-to-end transaction details, including customer data collected/carried as part of payment messages) must be stored only in India. A copy may be stored abroad for cross-border transactions, but the master copy must be in India.
- **Applies to:** Payment aggregators, payment gateways, card networks (Visa, Mastercard were required to comply), PPI issuers, banks.
- **Scope of "payment data":** Customer data, payment sensitive data (account/card numbers), payment credentials, transaction/payment data, end-to-end transaction details.

**Architectural implications:**

```
Without localisation constraints:
  User → Your API (AWS us-east-1) → Database (AWS us-east-1)
  Analytics events → BigQuery (US data centre)
  Logs → Datadog (US data centres)

With RBI payment data localisation:
  User → Your API (AWS ap-south-1 Mumbai) → Database (AWS ap-south-1)
  Payment transaction data must NEVER leave India's borders

  Cross-border transaction (e.g., international wire):
    Master copy in India (ap-south-1)
    Foreign copy allowed for processing the cross-border leg
    Foreign copy must be deleted after processing; master stays in India

  Analytics: if analytics events contain payment data → must use India region
  Logs: if logs contain payment amounts / account numbers → must use India region

  Allowed to be outside India:
    - Aggregated, anonymised analytics (no individual payment data)
    - Non-payment user data (preferences, device tokens) — no current RBI restriction
    - Employee data (DPDP Act rules pending)
```

**GDPR cross-border transfers:**

For EU user data, GDPR restricts transfers to non-EU countries unless:
- The destination country has an **adequacy decision** (EU Commission has approved its data protection level) — India does not currently have one.
- **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)** are in place between the exporter and importer.
- **Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs)** cover intra-group transfers.
- The transfer is covered by a **derogation** (explicit consent, necessary for contract performance, etc.).

For an Indian fintech serving EU users:
- EU user data stored in India requires SCCs between your EU operations and your Indian entity.
- Using AWS/GCP with EU regions (Frankfurt, Ireland) for EU user data avoids the transfer issue entirely — a common practical approach.

## Section 5: Engineering Obligations & System Design

**Q12. What is a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA), and when must you conduct one?**

A: A **DPIA** (called a Data Protection Impact Assessment under GDPR Article 35; a similar concept is introduced for Significant Data Fiduciaries under DPDP Act) is a structured risk assessment of a new processing activity or system change that is likely to result in a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms.

**When a DPIA is mandatory under GDPR:**
- Systematic and extensive profiling with significant effects (credit scoring, insurance pricing by algorithm)
- Large-scale processing of special category data (health data, biometric data)
- Systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas (CCTV — less relevant for fintech)
- New technologies with uncertain privacy implications
- Automated decision-making that produces legal or similarly significant effects

**When a DPIA is good practice in fintech (mandatory or not):**
- Launching a new credit product with ML-based scoring
- Integrating a new third-party data source (bureau data, alternative data)
- Changing data retention periods
- Adding a new analytics use case that processes customer transaction data
- Building a new internal analytics tool that accesses raw customer data
- Onboarding a new data processor (new cloud provider, new KYC vendor)

```typescript
// Runs nightly — identifies and processes expired data
class DataRetentionPipeline {
  async run(): Promise<RetentionRunReport> {
    const report: RetentionRunReport = { processed: 0, deleted: 0, errors: [] };

    for (const policy of DATA_RETENTION_POLICIES) {
      try {
        await this.enforcePolicy(policy, report);
      } catch (err) {
        report.errors.push({ policy: policy.dataCategory, error: err.message });
        // Log but continue — do not let one policy failure block others
      }
    }

    // Alert if any policy enforcement failed
    if (report.errors.length > 0) {
      await alertingService.warn('RETENTION_PIPELINE_ERRORS', report);
    }

    return report;
  }

  private async enforcePolicy(
    policy: RetentionPolicy,
    report: RetentionRunReport,
  ): Promise<void> {
    const expiredRecords = await this.findExpiredRecords(policy);

    for (const record of expiredRecords) {
      // Check for exceptions: regulatory hold, active dispute, AML flag
      const hasException = await this.checkExceptions(record, policy.exceptionFor);
      if (hasException.blocked) {
        await this.extendRetention(record, hasException.reason);
        continue;
      }

      switch (policy.actionOnExpiry) {
        case 'HARD_DELETE':
          await this.hardDelete(record, policy);
          break;
        case 'PSEUDONYMISE':
          await this.pseudonymise(record, policy);
          break;
        case 'ANONYMISE':
          await this.anonymise(record, policy);
          break;
        case 'SECURE_DELETE':
          await this.secureDelete(record, policy);  // overwrite + delete
          break;
      }

      report.processed++;
      report.deleted++;
    }
  }
}
```

**Q14. How do you handle a data subject access request (DSAR) or erasure request operationally — from receipt to fulfilment?**

A: DSARs are an operational process as much as a technical one. The law requires a response within 30 days (GDPR); DPDP Act timelines are pending rules. The process must be documented and auditable.

**End-to-end DSAR workflow:**

```
Stage 1 — Request Receipt (Day 0)
  Channel: in-app request form, email, written request
  System: create a DSAR ticket; send acknowledgement to user within 3 business days
  Identity verification: confirm the requester is the data subject
    (GDPR: right to ask for additional info to confirm identity before processing)
    (DPDP Act: reasonable identity verification)
  System assigns a DSAR reference number; logs the request

Stage 2 — Identity Verification (Days 1–5)
  For in-app requests: user is already authenticated — no additional verification
  For email/written requests: send identity verification challenge
  (e.g., confirm OTP to phone number on file, or submit a government ID)
  Log the verification outcome

Stage 3 — Eligibility Assessment (Days 5–10)
  For erasure: run assessErasureEligibility() — check for legal holds
  For access: no eligibility check needed (right is near-absolute)
  For portability: check that request covers consent/contract data only
  If third-party data is involved: assess what can be shared without revealing others

Stage 4 — Fulfilment (Days 10–25)
  Access request: run generateSubjectAccessReport(); compile PDF or JSON package
  Erasure: run the erasure pipeline; propagate to all downstream systems
  Portability: run generatePortabilityExport(); package as JSON/CSV
  Rectification: apply correction; notify downstream systems

Stage 5 — Response (by Day 30)
  Send fulfilment to the requester via secure channel
  For erasure: confirm what was deleted and what was retained (with legal basis)
  Log the completed DSAR including: request date, fulfilment date, actions taken

Stage 6 — Exception Handling
  If request denied (e.g., erasure refused due to active loan):
    Send written refusal with legal basis within 30 days
    Inform the user of their right to complain to the regulator
    Offer partial fulfilment where possible (delete marketing data; retain financial data)
```

```typescript
class ConsentManager {
  async checkProcessingPermission(
    userId:      string,
    purpose:     ConsentPurpose,
    jurisdiction: 'IN' | 'EU' | 'OTHER',
  ): Promise<ProcessingPermission> {

    const legalBasis = this.getLegalBasis(purpose, jurisdiction);

    // Legal obligation and contract: always permitted regardless of consent
    if (legalBasis === 'LEGAL_OBLIGATION' || legalBasis === 'CONTRACT') {
      return { permitted: true, basis: legalBasis, requiresConsent: false };
    }

    // Legitimate interest (EU fraud detection): requires balancing test on file
    if (legalBasis === 'LEGITIMATE_INTEREST') {
      const balancingTestOnFile = await this.hasDocumentedBalancingTest(purpose, jurisdiction);
      return { permitted: balancingTestOnFile, basis: legalBasis, requiresConsent: false };
    }

    // Consent-based: check active consent
    const consent = await this.consentCache.get(userId, purpose);
    if (!consent) {
      // Cache miss: check database
      const dbConsent = await this.consentService.hasValidConsent(userId, purpose);
      await this.consentCache.set(userId, purpose, dbConsent);
      return { permitted: dbConsent, basis: 'CONSENT', requiresConsent: true };
    }

    return { permitted: consent.status === 'GRANTED', basis: 'CONSENT', requiresConsent: true };
  }
}
```

**Q16. "Your engineering team proposes logging full API request and response bodies to Datadog (a US-based service) for debugging. What privacy concerns does this raise and how do you address them?"**

A: This proposal has multiple privacy problems and must be redesigned before implementation.

**Problem 1 — PII in logs:**

API request bodies for a fintech will contain: PAN numbers, Aadhaar numbers, bank account numbers, addresses, income data, phone numbers. Logging these to any external service violates the principle of data minimisation and creates a breach risk — if Datadog is compromised, or if a Datadog employee accesses logs, your customers' sensitive data is exposed.

**Problem 2 — Cross-border transfer (GDPR):**

Datadog is a US-based service. Logging EU user data to Datadog constitutes a cross-border transfer. For this to be lawful, SCCs must be in place between your company and Datadog. Datadog does offer SCCs — but you must execute them, configure data residency to Datadog's EU region, and verify that Datadog's subprocessors also have adequate protection.

**Problem 3 — RBI payment data localisation:**

If the API logs contain payment transaction data, RBI's localisation requirement prohibits that data from leaving India. Logging payment request/response bodies to a US-based service violates this.

**Problem 4 — Retention:**

Datadog's default log retention is 15 days to 15 months (configurable). This must align with your data retention policy for log data. You cannot retain PII in logs longer than necessary.

**How to address:**

```typescript
// Before logging: strip all sensitive fields
function sanitiseForLogging(requestBody: unknown): unknown {
  if (typeof requestBody !== 'object' || requestBody === null) return requestBody;

  const FIELDS_TO_REMOVE = [
    'pan_number', 'pan', 'aadhaar', 'aadhaar_number', 'aadhaar_hash',
    'bank_account_number', 'account_number', 'ifsc', 'cvv', 'card_number',
    'password', 'pin', 'otp', 'token', 'authorization',
    'income', 'salary', 'phone_number', 'mobile', 'email',
    'address', 'date_of_birth', 'dob',
  ];

  const FIELDS_TO_MASK = [
    { field: 'name', mask: (v: string) => v[0] + '***' },
    { field: 'user_id', mask: (v: string) => v.substring(0, 8) + '...' },
  ];

  const sanitised = { ...requestBody as Record<string, unknown> };

  for (const field of FIELDS_TO_REMOVE) {
    if (field in sanitised) sanitised[field] = '[REDACTED]';
  }

  for (const { field, mask } of FIELDS_TO_MASK) {
    if (field in sanitised && typeof sanitised[field] === 'string') {
      sanitised[field] = mask(sanitised[field] as string);
    }
  }

  return sanitised;
}
```

## Quick Reference Table

| Concept | GDPR | DPDP Act 2023 | Engineering implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data subject / principal** | Natural person in EU/EEA | Individual in India | Whose data you must protect |
| **Controller / Fiduciary** | Determines purpose and means | Same definition | Your company's role; carries full obligations |
| **Processor** | Processes on Controller's behalf | Data Processor | Must sign DPA; you remain responsible |
| **Lawful bases** | 6 (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interest, public task, legitimate interest) | Consent + specified legitimate uses (narrower) | Map each processing activity to a basis; document it |
| **Consent validity** | Freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous, withdrawable | Same + notice in Indian languages | Pre-ticked boxes invalid; withdrawal = easy |
| **Right to access** | 30 days | Rules pending; align to 30 days | SAR pipeline must query all systems holding user data |
| **Right to erasure** | Yes; overridden by legal obligation | Yes; overridden by legal obligation | Pseudonymise financial records; hard-delete marketing data |
| **Right to portability** | Yes (GDPR only) | Not explicit | Export consent/contract data in machine-readable format |
| **Automated decisions** | Right to human review; explanation required | Not explicit yet | Credit rejection must be explainable and reviewable |
| **Data localisation** | No blanket requirement; SCCs for transfers | Rules pending; RBI mandates payment data in India | India region for payment data; SCCs for EU data exports |
| **Breach notification** | 72h to authority; without delay to subjects | "Without delay" to DPBI and subjects | 72h SLA; breach records; DPO notification pipeline |
| **DPIA** | Mandatory for high-risk processing | Mandatory for Significant Data Fiduciaries | Credit scoring, health data, biometric data = DPIA required |
| **Children's data** | Parental consent; under-16 (or 13–16 per member state) | Verifiable parental consent; under-18 | Age gate + parental consent flow |
| **Max penalty** | €20M or 4% global turnover | ₹250 crore per instance | Engineering decisions directly affect liability |
| **Tipping off / confidentiality** | No specific rule for AML tip-off (separate AML law) | No specific rule | AML tip-off prohibition comes from PMLA, not DPDP |

**Tip for the interview:** At the 5 YOE level at a fintech, privacy law questions appear in three forms: (1) "how do you implement the right to be forgotten?" — the expected answer covers three layers: hard delete for marketing data, pseudonymisation for financial records (PMLA retention), and propagation to all downstream systems including backups (flag for exclusion, not retroactive removal); (2) "we want to log everything to Datadog for debugging — what are the issues?" — the expected answer covers PII in logs (sanitise before logging), cross-border transfer (SCCs or use India region), RBI localisation for payment data, and retention policy alignment; (3) "how do you ensure purpose limitation in your data architecture?" — the expected answer is a purpose-tagged data access layer where every query is validated against the user's active consent for that purpose, not a policy document. Know the RBI payment data localisation circular — it is more immediately actionable than the DPDP Act for most Indian fintechs. Know that the 72-hour GDPR breach notification clock runs from when you became aware, not from when the breach occurred — and that "became aware" in engineering terms means your monitoring detected an anomaly, not when legal finished their assessment.
