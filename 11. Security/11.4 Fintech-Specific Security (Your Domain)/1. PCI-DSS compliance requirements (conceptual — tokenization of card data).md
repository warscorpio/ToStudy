## Section 1: PCI-DSS Fundamentals

**Q1. What is PCI-DSS, who does it apply to, and what are the consequences of non-compliance for a fintech company?**
A: PCI-DSS (Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) is a contractual security framework created and enforced by the PCI Security Standards Council — a body founded by the five major card networks: Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Discover, and JCB. It is not a law — it is a condition of accepting card payments. Any organization that stores, processes, or transmits cardholder data (CHD) must comply.

**Who it applies to — the "merchant level" classification:**
```
Level 1: > 6 million card transactions per year
  → Annual onsite QSA (Qualified Security Assessor) audit
  → Quarterly network vulnerability scans by ASV (Approved Scanning Vendor)
  → Annual penetration test

Level 2: 1–6 million transactions per year
  → Annual Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ) — type depends on integration method
  → Quarterly ASV scans
  → Some acquirers require QSA audit at this level

Level 3: 20,000–1 million e-commerce transactions per year
  → Annual SAQ
  → Quarterly ASV scans

Level 4: < 20,000 e-commerce transactions or up to 1 million other transactions
  → Annual SAQ (often SAQ A or SAQ A-EP for most e-commerce)
  → Quarterly ASV scans (recommended, sometimes required by acquirer)

Service Providers (payment processors, gateways, SaaS handling CHD):
  Level 1: > 300,000 transactions per year → QSA audit
  Level 2: < 300,000 transactions per year → SAQ or QSA
```

**Consequences of non-compliance:**
```
Financial penalties (levied by the acquiring bank, not PCI SSC directly):
  Non-compliance fines: $5,000–$100,000/month from acquirer to merchant
  Post-breach fines:    $5,000–$500,000 per incident from card networks
  Card replacement cost: $3–$10 per compromised card (networks charge this to breached entity)

Operational consequences:
  Termination of card acceptance: acquirer can revoke the merchant account
  → Business cannot accept Visa/Mastercard → effectively stops operations
  Increased transaction fees: acquiring banks charge higher interchange to non-compliant merchants
  Mandatory forensic investigation: breached merchants pay for PFI (Payment Forensic Investigator)
  Reputational damage: breaches are public; customer trust loss, regulatory scrutiny

Real examples:
  Target (2013): 40M card numbers stolen → ~$202M in settlements + fines
  Heartland Payment Systems (2008): 100M+ cards → $140M+ in settlements, briefly lost card acceptance
  In India: RBI's framework for digital payments also references PCI-DSS for card-on-file storage
```

**Q2. What are the 12 PCI-DSS requirements, and which ones are most directly relevant to a backend Node.js API that handles payment data?**
A: PCI-DSS v4.0 (released March 2022) organizes its requirements into six goals and twelve requirements. As a backend engineer, you do not need to memorize all 12 exhaustively — but you must deeply understand the ones that directly govern how your code handles card data:

```
Goal 1 — Build and Maintain a Secure Network and Systems
  Req 1: Install and maintain network security controls
         → Firewalls, security groups, network segmentation (CDE isolation)
  Req 2: Apply secure configurations to all system components
         → No default passwords, no unnecessary services, hardened OS/Node.js config

Goal 2 — Protect Account Data
  Req 3: Protect stored account data                        ← MOST RELEVANT
         → No storage of CVV/CVC, encrypted PANs, key management
         → Tokenization lives here — the alternative to encrypted storage
  Req 4: Protect cardholder data with strong cryptography during transmission
         → TLS 1.2+ for all CHD in transit; no card data in URLs or logs

Goal 3 — Maintain a Vulnerability Management Program
  Req 5: Protect all systems and networks from malicious software
         → AV/EDR on systems in CDE; dependency vulnerability scanning
  Req 6: Develop and maintain secure systems and software
         → Secure SDLC; code review; dependency updates; OWASP Top 10

Goal 4 — Implement Strong Access Control Measures
  Req 7: Restrict access to system components and cardholder data by business need
         → RBAC; principle of least privilege; no shared admin accounts
  Req 8: Identify users and authenticate access to system components
         → MFA for all access to CDE; strong password policies
  Req 9: Restrict physical access to cardholder data
         → Data center controls; media destruction (less relevant for cloud-native)

Goal 5 — Regularly Monitor and Test Networks
  Req 10: Log and monitor all access to system components and cardholder data
          → Audit logs with tamper protection; SIEM alerting; log retention ≥ 12 months
  Req 11: Test security of systems and networks regularly
          → Quarterly ASV scans; annual penetration test; internal vulnerability scans

Goal 6 — Maintain an Information Security Policy
  Req 12: Support information security with organizational policies and programs
          → Security policies; incident response plan; vendor management
```

**The three requirements a backend engineer owns most directly:**
```
Req 3 — Card data storage:
  What you build: tokenization (replace PANs with tokens before they reach your DB),
  encrypted storage if you must store, key management HSM integration
  What you avoid: storing CVV at all (prohibited absolutely), storing PANs in plaintext

Req 4 — Card data in transit:
  What you build: TLS 1.2+ on all APIs handling CHD, no card data in query params,
  no card data in application logs, certificate management
  What you avoid: HTTP endpoints for card submission, logging req.body when it may contain CHD

Req 6 — Secure development:
  What you build: security in SDLC — SAST in CI pipeline, dependency scanning,
  code review checklist including OWASP API Security Top 10, DAST in staging
  What you avoid: deploying with known critical CVEs, no security review of auth changes
```

**Q3. What is the Cardholder Data Environment (CDE), and why is minimizing its scope the single most impactful architectural decision a fintech team can make?**
A: The CDE (Cardholder Data Environment) is the set of systems, people, and processes that store, process, or transmit cardholder data, plus any system that is network-connected to those systems. Everything inside the CDE is subject to all 12 PCI-DSS requirements. Everything outside the CDE is out of scope.

**What constitutes CHD and the full CDE boundary:**
```
Cardholder Data (CHD) — PCI-defined:
  PAN (Primary Account Number):     16-digit card number  ← most sensitive; governs scope
  Cardholder Name:                  name on card
  Expiration Date:                  month/year
  Service Code:                     3-digit code on mag stripe

Sensitive Authentication Data (SAD) — NEVER store, even encrypted:
  CVV2/CVC2/CID:                   3–4 digit security code
  Full magnetic stripe data:        Track 1 + Track 2
  PIN / PIN block:                  encrypted PIN for debit

CDE scope — everything in the dashed boundary:
  ┌ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ┐
  │  CDE (in scope for all 12 requirements)           │
  │                                                   │
  │  Systems that store/process/transmit CHD:         │
  │  ┌─────────────┐  ┌─────────────┐                │
  │  │ Payment API  │  │ Card DB     │                │
  │  │ (handles PAN)│  │ (stores PAN)│                │
  │  └─────────────┘  └─────────────┘                │
  │                                                   │
  │  Systems with network connectivity to above:      │
  │  ┌─────────────┐  ┌─────────────┐                │
  │  │ Auth Service │  │ Admin Panel │                │
  │  │ (same VPC)   │  │ (same VPC)  │                │
  │  └─────────────┘  └─────────────┘                │
  └ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ┘

  Out of scope (separate VPC, no connectivity to CDE):
  ┌─────────────┐  ┌─────────────┐  ┌─────────────┐
  │ Loans       │  │ Notification│  │ Analytics   │
  │ Service     │  │ Service     │  │ Service     │
  └─────────────┘  └─────────────┘  └─────────────┘
```

**Why scope minimization is the most impactful decision:**
```
Cost of compliance scales with CDE size:
  100 systems in CDE: every system needs hardening, logging, access control, scanning
  5 systems in CDE:   same requirements but 20x fewer systems to secure and audit
  
Every system added to CDE:
  → Must be hardened per Req 2
  → Must have access controls per Req 7/8
  → Must be logged per Req 10
  → Must be scanned quarterly per Req 11
  → Adds surface area for a QSA to audit → audit cost, audit time, audit risk

The architecture decision:
  WRONG: Build the entire platform and make it PCI-compliant
  RIGHT: Isolate card data handling to the smallest possible dedicated subsystem;
         connect everything else to that subsystem via tokens (not card numbers)

Concrete example:
  Loans service processes loan applications with card repayments
  WRONG: Loans service receives the PAN, stores it, passes it around
         → Loans service is in CDE → all its dependencies are in CDE
  RIGHT: Loans service receives a TOKEN (not PAN); sends token to payment service
         → Loans service never sees a PAN → never enters CDE
         → Only the payment gateway/vault is in CDE → 2 services instead of 20
```

## Section 2: Tokenization — Architecture and Mechanics

**Q4. What is payment tokenization, how does it differ from encryption, and what problem does it solve that encryption alone cannot?**
A: Tokenization replaces a sensitive value (the PAN) with a non-sensitive surrogate value (the token) that has no mathematical relationship to the original. Encryption transforms a value into a different representation that can be reversed with a key. Both protect the original value — but they solve different problems:

```
Encryption:
  Input:   4111 1111 1111 1111  (PAN)
  Key:     AES-256 key
  Output:  a8f3c2d91b7e4f60... (ciphertext)
  Reversal: possible — with the key, anyone can decrypt
  The encrypted value IS the card number in disguised form
  Risk: if key is compromised → all encrypted data is instantly decrypted
  Scope: systems storing the ciphertext remain in CDE
         (they could decrypt it if they had the key — PCI still cares)

Tokenization:
  Input:   4111 1111 1111 1111  (PAN)
  Lookup:  Vault stores { token: "tok_8x9mR2pL" ↔ PAN: "4111 1111 1111 1111" }
  Output:  tok_8x9mR2pL  (token — opaque reference)
  Reversal: only possible with access to the vault's lookup table
  The token is NOT the card number in any form — it is a database key
  Risk: even if the database with tokens is stolen → tokens are useless without vault
  Scope: systems using tokens are OUT of CDE — they cannot recover the PAN even if compromised
```

**The problem encryption cannot solve — CDE scope reduction:**
```
Scenario: Loans service needs to charge repayments to a saved card.

With encryption:
  Loans DB stores: { loanId: "L001", encryptedPAN: "a8f3c2d9..." }
  To charge: Loans service must decrypt → gets the PAN → calls payment gateway
  Decryption capability makes Loans service able to see PANs → IN SCOPE for PCI
  Even if the Loans service never actually decrypts in normal operation,
  the CAPABILITY exists → PCI assessor says: in scope

With tokenization:
  Loans DB stores: { loanId: "L001", cardToken: "tok_8x9mR2pL" }
  To charge: Loans service sends token → payment vault detokenizes → charges gateway
  Loans service CANNOT detokenize — it has no access to the vault
  Even if Loans DB is compromised → attacker has tok_8x9mR2pL → useless
  Loans service is OUT of scope for PCI

The key insight: scope reduction is about CAPABILITY, not intent.
A service that CAN decrypt encrypted PANs is in scope, even if it never does.
A service that CANNOT detokenize tokens is out of scope by construction.
```

**Q5. What are the two types of tokenization — vault-based and vaultless (format-preserving) — and what are the trade-offs between them?**
A: The distinction determines the token's properties, the infrastructure required, and the residual PCI scope:

**Vault-based tokenization (most common for payment tokens):**
```
Architecture:
  Token Vault: secure database mapping { token → PAN }
  Token:       random opaque string (e.g., tok_8x9mR2pL7Kx3...)
               No mathematical relationship to the PAN
               Token format: typically UUID or custom prefix + random bytes

Token generation:
  1. PAN arrives at tokenization service
  2. Generate cryptographically random token (128 bits)
  3. Store mapping in vault: token → encrypted PAN (vault encrypts at rest)
  4. Return token to caller; discard PAN from memory

Detokenization:
  1. Token arrives at tokenization service
  2. Look up token in vault → retrieve encrypted PAN
  3. Decrypt PAN (vault-internal operation)
  4. Return PAN to authorized caller for payment processing

Properties:
  ✓ Token is completely opaque — no attack on the token reveals the PAN
  ✓ Access control at the vault: only authorized services can detokenize
  ✓ Audit log: every detokenization is recorded (who, when, for what purpose)
  ✗ Requires vault infrastructure — database, replication, HA, backups
  ✗ Detokenization requires a network call → latency
  ✗ Vault itself is a high-value target and must be heavily protected
  ✗ Token is non-functional without vault — can't validate Luhn, can't charge without lookup
```

**Vaultless tokenization — Format-Preserving Encryption (FPE):**
```
Architecture:
  No vault database required
  Token IS a transformation of the PAN using a secret key + algorithm
  FPE algorithm (e.g., FF1 or FF3-1 from NIST SP 800-38G):
    Preserves the format: 16-digit PAN → 16-digit token
    Preserves numerical digit structure: token looks like a valid card number
    Reversible only with the key (like encryption, but format-preserving)

Token generation:
  token = FPE_encrypt(key, PAN, tweak)
  e.g., PAN = 4111 1111 1111 1111
        token = 7293 4851 0627 3841  (still 16 digits, passes Luhn check optionally)

Detokenization:
  PAN = FPE_decrypt(key, token, tweak)
  No database lookup — purely computational

Properties:
  ✓ No vault database — simpler infrastructure
  ✓ Token format matches PAN format — drop-in for legacy systems expecting 16 digits
  ✓ Stateless — deterministic (same PAN + same key → same token, unlike vault-based)
  ✓ Can be configured to pass Luhn check — legacy systems that validate card number format work
  ✗ Token IS a mathematical transformation of PAN — if key leaks, all tokens → PANs instantly
  ✗ Key management is the sole security dependency — single point of failure
  ✗ Deterministic: same PAN always produces same token → token frequency analysis
    (if attacker sees many tokens, they can learn that two tokens = same card without the key)
  ✗ PCI considers FPE tokens to be "encrypted cardholder data" → system with FPE key = in CDE
  ✗ FF3-1 has known weaknesses for certain tweak values — careful parameter selection required

When to use each:
  Vault-based:   new systems; e-commerce; maximum scope reduction; can redesign data flows
  FPE vaultless: legacy systems that expect 16-digit card numbers; cannot change data formats;
                 existing processors that validate Luhn; mainframe integrations
```

**Q6. Walk through the complete tokenization flow for a card-on-file payment in a fintech lending platform — from customer card entry to a recurring loan repayment charge.**
A: This end-to-end flow illustrates where tokenization occurs, which systems are in scope, and how the token travels through the system:

```
Phase 1 — Card Capture and Initial Tokenization (customer-facing)

Customer fills payment form in mobile app or web browser
          │
          │  IMPORTANT: The PAN must NEVER touch your application servers
          │  Use a hosted fields / JavaScript SDK from your payment gateway
          │
          ▼
Payment Gateway JS SDK (Razorpay.js / Stripe.js / JusPay SDK)
  Captures card fields in gateway-hosted iframes (your JS cannot read them)
  Sends PAN directly to payment gateway's servers over TLS
  Payment gateway stores PAN in their vault (they are PCI Level 1 certified)
  Gateway returns to your frontend: { customerId: "cust_xyz", paymentMethodId: "pm_abc" }
  → pm_abc is the gateway's token for this card

          │  pm_abc flows to your backend (NOT the PAN)
          ▼
Your Backend API (POST /api/payment-methods)
  Receives: { userId: "user-123", paymentMethodId: "pm_abc" }
  Stores in YOUR database: { userId: "user-123", gatewayToken: "pm_abc", last4: "1111", brand: "Visa" }
  Your database NEVER contains the PAN — only the gateway's token
  Your backend servers are OUT OF SCOPE for PCI

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Phase 2 — Recurring Repayment (automated, no customer present)

Loan repayment scheduler (cron / event) triggers repayment for loan L001
          │
          ▼
Loans Service (repayment-service.ts)
  Reads from DB: { loanId: "L001", userId: "user-123", amountDue: 5000, currency: "INR" }
  Reads payment method: { userId: "user-123", gatewayToken: "pm_abc" }
  Constructs charge request:
    {
      amount:        500000,          // in paise (₹5000)
      currency:      "INR",
      customer_id:   "cust_xyz",      // gateway customer ID
      token:         "pm_abc",        // gateway token for the saved card
      description:   "EMI for L001 - Month 3",
      idempotency_key: "L001-M3-2025-09", // prevents double-charge
    }
  → Loans service never sees a PAN; sends the token to the gateway

          │
          ▼
Payment Gateway API (POST https://api.razorpay.com/v1/charges)
  Receives the charge request with token pm_abc
  Detokenizes internally: token pm_abc → PAN 4111 1111 1111 1111
  Sends to card network: Visa authorization request with the actual PAN
  Returns to your backend: { chargeId: "ch_xyz", status: "captured" }

          │
          ▼
Loans Service records: { loanId: "L001", chargeId: "ch_xyz", status: "SUCCESS", timestamp: now }
Notification service sends repayment confirmation to customer

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Data flow summary:

System              Sees PAN?  Sees Token?  PCI Scope?
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Customer browser    YES (typed) NO          Out of scope (end-user)
Gateway JS SDK      YES         NO          Gateway's scope (not yours)
Payment Gateway     YES         YES         IN SCOPE (they handle it; certified)
Your Backend API    NO          YES         OUT OF SCOPE ✓
Your Loans DB       NO          YES         OUT OF SCOPE ✓
Your Loans Service  NO          YES         OUT OF SCOPE ✓
```

## Section 3: What You Must Never Store

**Q7. What data is absolutely prohibited from storage under PCI-DSS, and what are the technical controls to enforce these prohibitions in a Node.js API?**
A: PCI-DSS Req 3 draws a hard line between data that may be stored (with restrictions) and data that must never be stored under any circumstances — not encrypted, not hashed, not truncated:

```
PROHIBITED — Never store after authorization, regardless of protection method:
  Full magnetic stripe data  (Track 1 + Track 2 — everything on the mag stripe)
  CVV2 / CVC2 / CID         (3-4 digit security code — front or back of card)
  PIN / encrypted PIN block  (debit card PIN)

These are collectively called Sensitive Authentication Data (SAD).
The prohibition is absolute: "SAD must not be retained after authorization."
No encryption, no HSM, no format-preserving transformation makes storage acceptable.
Rationale: these are the values that enable fraudulent card-present transactions.
If an attacker has PAN + expiry + CVV → they can make purchases as the cardholder.

ALLOWED to store (with restrictions):
  PAN (truncated):       First 6 + last 4 digits → 411111XXXXXX1111 (shows card brand, last4)
  PAN (encrypted/tokenized): if stored, must use strong cryptography with key management
  Cardholder name:       name on card — may be stored
  Expiration date:       month/year — may be stored
  Service code:          may be stored (3-digit code on mag stripe — not the CVV)
```

**Technical controls in Node.js to enforce storage prohibitions:**

```typescript
// Control 1 — DTO-level stripping: remove prohibited fields before any processing
import { IsString, IsOptional, Length, Matches } from 'class-validator';
import { Exclude, Expose } from 'class-transformer';

// This DTO is what your API receives from the frontend (if not using hosted fields)
// In a proper PCI-compliant implementation this DTO should not exist —
// the card data should go directly to the payment gateway JS SDK
// But if you process card data on the server (higher PCI scope):

class CardInputDto {
  @IsString()
  @Matches(/^\d{13,19}$/)
  pan: string;            // Received for tokenization — immediately discarded after

  @IsString()
  @Matches(/^(0[1-9]|1[0-2])\/\d{2}$/)
  expiry: string;         // MM/YY — may be stored

  @IsString()
  @Length(3, 4)
  @Matches(/^\d{3,4}$/)
  cvv: string;            // Received for authorization — NEVER stored, even transiently
}

// Control 2 — Request handler: tokenize immediately, never store the PAN
@Post('payment-methods')
async addCard(@Body() dto: CardInputDto, @Req() req: RequestWithUser) {
  // Step 1: Tokenize the PAN immediately via payment gateway
  const gatewayResult = await this.paymentGateway.tokenize({
    pan:    dto.pan,
    expiry: dto.expiry,
    cvv:    dto.cvv,    // Sent to gateway for authorization; not stored
  });

  // Step 2: What we store — gateway token + non-sensitive display data only
  const paymentMethod = await this.paymentMethodRepo.create({
    userId:       req.user.userId,
    gatewayToken: gatewayResult.token,    // ✓ Opaque token — not a PAN
    last4:        dto.pan.slice(-4),      // ✓ Last 4 digits — allowed
    expiryMonth:  dto.expiry.split('/')[0], // ✓ Expiry — allowed
    expiryYear:   '20' + dto.expiry.split('/')[1],
    brand:        detectCardBrand(dto.pan.slice(0, 6)), // ✓ BIN lookup for brand only
    // NOT stored: dto.pan (PAN), dto.cvv (prohibited)
  });

  // Step 3: Explicit memory hygiene — overwrite sensitive fields before GC
  // Note: V8's garbage collector is non-deterministic; this is best-effort
  // The real protection is: never log it, never persist it, never pass it further
  dto.pan = '0000000000000000'; // Overwrite in object
  dto.cvv = '000';              // Overwrite in object

  return { paymentMethodId: paymentMethod.id, last4: paymentMethod.last4 };
}

// Control 3 — Global logging middleware: scrub card data from all logs
@Injectable()
export class CardDataScrubMiddleware implements NestMiddleware {
  private readonly PAN_PATTERN = /\b(?:\d[ -]?){13,19}\b/g;
  private readonly CVV_PATTERN = /\b\d{3,4}\b/g; // Context-dependent — be conservative

  use(req: Request, res: Response, next: NextFunction) {
    // Scrub from request body before any logging middleware processes it
    if (req.body) {
      req.body = this.scrubObject(req.body);
    }
    next();
  }

  private scrubObject(obj: any): any {
    if (typeof obj === 'string') {
      return obj.replace(this.PAN_PATTERN, '[PAN REDACTED]');
    }
    if (typeof obj === 'object' && obj !== null) {
      return Object.fromEntries(
        Object.entries(obj).map(([k, v]) => [
          k,
          // Key-name based scrubbing for known field names
          ['pan', 'card_number', 'cardNumber', 'cvv', 'cvc', 'cvv2',
           'card_cvv', 'security_code'].includes(k.toLowerCase())
            ? '[REDACTED]'
            : this.scrubObject(v),
        ])
      );
    }
    return obj;
  }
}

// Control 4 — Database schema: no columns that could hold a PAN
// Code review checklist: reject any migration that adds:
//   card_number VARCHAR, pan VARCHAR, full_pan VARCHAR, card_data TEXT
// These column names are a red flag in any table outside the dedicated vault

// TypeORM entity for payment methods — note the absence of PAN column
@Entity('payment_methods')
export class PaymentMethod {

  id: string;

  @Column({ name: 'user_id' })
  userId: string;

  @Column({ name: 'gateway_token' })

  @Column({ length: 4, name: 'last_four' })
  lastFour: string;       // Last 4 digits — PCI allows this for display

  @Column({ length: 6, name: 'bin', nullable: true })
  bin: string;            // First 6 digits (BIN) — allowed for card brand detection

  @Column({ length: 2, name: 'expiry_month' })
  expiryMonth: string;

  @Column({ length: 4, name: 'expiry_year' })
  expiryYear: string;

  @Column({ length: 20, name: 'brand' })
  brand: string;           // "visa", "mastercard", "rupay"

  // NO: pan, card_number, cvv, full_mag_stripe
}
```

**Q8. What is PAN truncation and PAN masking, how do they differ, and what are the PCI rules governing which digits can be displayed?**
A: Truncation and masking are two distinct techniques for limiting PAN exposure in stored data and user interfaces:

**PAN truncation — permanent, irreversible removal of digits:**
```
Full PAN:         4111 1111 1111 1111
Truncated PAN:    XXXX XXXX XXXX 1111  (last 4 only)
                  411111XXXXXX1111     (first 6 + last 4 = BIN + last 4)

PCI rule: may store the truncated PAN; this is NOT considered cardholder data storage
          if both the truncated version AND the full encrypted version are not
          stored together on the same system (storing both would enable reconstruction)

First 6 (BIN) allowed: identifies issuing bank and card brand — useful for routing
Last 4 allowed:         customer identification, display in UI ("ending in 1111")

First 6 + last 4 stored together: 12 of 16 digits known
  → Middle 6 digits unknown → 10^6 = 1,000,000 possibilities
  → Can this enable PAN reconstruction? PCI says: acceptable for storage
  → But: storing first 6 + last 4 + other CHD on same system = still in scope

What is NOT allowed to store:
  More than first 6 OR more than last 4 (i.e., first 8 + last 4 = too much)
  The full PAN in any unencrypted form
```

**PAN masking — display-time redaction, original may still be stored:**
```
Full PAN stored (encrypted): 4111 1111 1111 1111
Displayed to user (masked):  **** **** **** 1111  or  411111******1111

PAN masking is for UI/UX — showing the user enough to identify their card
without displaying the full number in the browser/app where it could be captured

PCI requirement (Req 3.3.1): PAN must be masked when displayed
  Only personnel with legitimate business need may see full PAN
  Display must show no more than first 6 / last 4 by default

Implementation:
```
```typescript
// Utility functions for PAN handling
export class PanUtils {
  // Mask for display — never log, never send to frontend in full
  static mask(pan: string): string {
    if (!pan || pan.length < 8) return '****';
    const last4  = pan.slice(-4);
    const first6 = pan.slice(0, 6);
    const masked = '*'.repeat(pan.length - 10); // mask middle digits
    return `${first6}${masked}${last4}`; // 411111******1111
  }

  // Truncate for storage — last 4 only, for UI display purposes
  static truncateLast4(pan: string): string {
    return pan.slice(-4);
  }

  // Validate basic format before tokenization
  static isValidFormat(pan: string): boolean {
    const digits = pan.replace(/\s|-/g, '');
    if (!/^\d{13,19}$/.test(digits)) return false;
    return this.luhnCheck(digits);
  }

  // Luhn algorithm — validate card number check digit
  // Use for format validation only; do NOT use Luhn to authenticate transactions
  private static luhnCheck(pan: string): boolean {
    let sum = 0;
    let isEven = false;
    for (let i = pan.length - 1; i >= 0; i--) {
      let digit = parseInt(pan[i], 10);
      if (isEven) {
        digit *= 2;
        if (digit > 9) digit -= 9;
      }
      sum += digit;
      isEven = !isEven;
    }
    return sum % 10 === 0;
  }
}
```

## Section 4: Key Management and Encryption

**Q9. When tokenization is not possible and you must encrypt stored PANs, what does PCI-DSS require for key management, and what is a KEK (Key Encryption Key)?**
A: If your architecture requires storing encrypted PANs (legacy systems, regulatory requirements for chargeback support, etc.), PCI-DSS Req 3 imposes strict key management requirements that go far beyond simply choosing AES-256:

**PCI-DSS key management requirements:**
```
Req 3.6 — Cryptographic key management procedures:
  3.6.1: Key generation using strong cryptography and random number generation
         → AES-256 keys generated via CSPRNG (crypto.randomBytes), never from passwords
         → Key generation in an HSM (Hardware Security Module) for highest assurance

  3.6.1.2: Key custodians formally acknowledge key responsibilities
           → Two-person integrity: no single person has access to the complete key
           → Key custodians named, acknowledged in writing

  3.6.1.3: Secure storage of cryptographic keys — keys must themselves be encrypted
           → DATA ENCRYPTION KEY (DEK): encrypts the actual PAN data
           → KEY ENCRYPTION KEY (KEK): encrypts the DEK — stored separately/securely
           → The KEK never coexists in the same location as the DEK in plaintext

  3.6.1.4: Cryptoperiod (key lifetime) defined and enforced
           → DEKs: 1 year maximum cryptoperiod (PCI recommendation)
           → After expiry: data encrypted with old key must be re-encrypted with new key
           → Old key retained (in encrypted form) only as long as data encrypted with it exists

  3.7: Cryptographic key management policies and procedures
       → Key rotation procedures documented and tested
       → Key compromise response procedure documented
       → Split knowledge / dual control enforced for key components
```

**The KEK pattern — two-tier key hierarchy:**
```
Tier 1 — Key Encryption Key (KEK):
  Stored in: HSM (Hardware Security Module) or AWS KMS / GCP Cloud KMS
  Never leaves the HSM/KMS in plaintext — operations (encrypt, decrypt) happen inside
  The KEK is the master key; losing it = losing access to all encrypted data
  Rotation: annual at minimum; requires re-wrapping all DEKs

Tier 2 — Data Encryption Key (DEK):
  Used to: directly encrypt PAN data at rest
  Stored in: application database or secrets manager, BUT encrypted by the KEK
  What's in the DB: { encryptedPAN: "...", wrappedDEK: "...", keyVersion: "2" }
  "wrappedDEK" = AES-wrap(KEK, DEK) — DEK encrypted by the KEK

Encryption flow:
  1. Retrieve wrapped DEK from DB
  2. Send wrappedDEK to KMS: Decrypt(KEK, wrappedDEK) → DEK (happens inside KMS)
  3. Use DEK to decrypt: AES-256-GCM decrypt(DEK, encryptedPAN) → PAN
  4. Immediately discard DEK from memory after use

Why two tiers:
  If only DEK (no KEK):
    Rotating encryption keys → must decrypt and re-encrypt all PANs → massive operation
    Key is in the application → compromised app = compromised key = all PANs decrypted

  With KEK (two-tier):
    To rotate DEK: just generate new DEK, re-wrap with KEK → one new wrappedDEK per PAN
    The PAN ciphertext itself doesn't need to change (just swap the wrapped key)
    KEK rotation: re-wrap all DEKs with new KEK (DEKs change, PAN ciphertext unchanged)
    Key compromise: only re-encrypt data touched by the compromised DEK
    Access control: KMS enforces who can unwrap DEKs (IAM policies on the KEK)
```

```typescript
// AWS KMS implementation of the KEK pattern
import { KMSClient, EncryptCommand, DecryptCommand } from '@aws-sdk/client-kms';
import crypto from 'crypto';

const kms = new KMSClient({ region: 'ap-south-1' });
const KEK_ARN = process.env.KMS_KEK_ARN!; // The KEK lives in KMS — never leaves

@Injectable()
export class CardEncryptionService {
  // Encrypt a PAN for storage
  async encryptPan(pan: string): Promise<EncryptedPanRecord> {
    // Generate a fresh DEK for this PAN (envelope encryption)
    const dek = crypto.randomBytes(32); // 256-bit AES key

    // Wrap the DEK using KMS (KEK never leaves KMS)
    const { CiphertextBlob: wrappedDek } = await kms.send(new EncryptCommand({
      KeyId:     KEK_ARN,
      Plaintext: dek,
      EncryptionContext: { purpose: 'pan-encryption', version: '1' },
    }));

    // Encrypt the PAN with the DEK (local operation — fast)
    const iv           = crypto.randomBytes(12); // 96-bit IV for GCM
    const cipher       = crypto.createCipheriv('aes-256-gcm', dek, iv);
    const encrypted    = Buffer.concat([cipher.update(pan, 'utf8'), cipher.final()]);
    const authTag      = cipher.getAuthTag();

    // Zero out DEK from memory (best-effort in JS)
    dek.fill(0);

    return {
      encryptedPan: encrypted.toString('base64'),
      iv:           iv.toString('base64'),
      authTag:      authTag.toString('base64'),
      wrappedDek:   Buffer.from(wrappedDek!).toString('base64'),
      keyVersion:   '1', // Track which KEK version was used
    };
  }

  // Decrypt a stored encrypted PAN
  async decryptPan(record: EncryptedPanRecord): Promise<string> {
    // Unwrap the DEK using KMS (audited, access-controlled)
    const { Plaintext: dek } = await kms.send(new DecryptCommand({
      KeyId:          KEK_ARN,
      CiphertextBlob: Buffer.from(record.wrappedDek, 'base64'),
      EncryptionContext: { purpose: 'pan-encryption', version: '1' },
    }));

    // Decrypt the PAN locally with the DEK
    const decipher = crypto.createDecipheriv(
      'aes-256-gcm',
      Buffer.from(dek!),
      Buffer.from(record.iv, 'base64'),
    );
    decipher.setAuthTag(Buffer.from(record.authTag, 'base64'));
    const pan = decipher.update(Buffer.from(record.encryptedPan, 'base64')) +
                decipher.final('utf8');

    return pan;
  }
}
```

**Q11. What does PCI-DSS Req 10 require for logging, and what must never appear in application logs when handling card data?**

```
  All individual user access to cardholder data

  Logs must be protected from modification (append-only, WORM storage)
```

## Section 6: SAQ Types, Scope, and Real-World Fintech Patterns

**Q12. What is a Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ), which type applies to a fintech that uses a payment gateway with hosted fields, and what does SAQ A actually require?**
A: The SAQ type determines which subset of PCI-DSS requirements apply to your organization. The correct SAQ type depends entirely on how your integration handles card data — specifically, whether your systems ever touch the PAN:

```
SAQ A — Fully outsourced card processing (the lowest-burden, most common for e-commerce):
  Eligible if:
    ✓ Card data entry is entirely handled by the payment gateway (hosted fields/iframes)
    ✓ Your e-commerce page never receives, processes, or transmits card data
    ✓ You only store cardholder name, expiry, last 4 (not PAN)
    ✓ Your site is not susceptible to attacks that could capture card data
  Coverage: 22 requirements (out of 78 in the full SAQ D)
  Requirements include:
    Req 2:  No default passwords on hosting/CMS
    Req 6:  Web application security (no XSS, CSRF that could redirect to fake page)
    Req 8:  MFA for admin access to hosting environment
    Req 9:  Secure media disposal
    Req 10: Audit logging and monitoring
    Req 12: Security policies

SAQ A-EP — JavaScript-based card capture (your JS captures card data before sending to gateway):
  Eligible if:
    ✓ Card data sent directly from browser to gateway (client-side JS handles it)
    ✗ But your JS runs on your page (not gateway's iframe)
    ✓ Your servers never receive the card data — it goes browser → gateway
  Coverage: ~191 requirements
  Higher burden than SAQ A because your web page could be compromised to inject
  malicious JS that captures card data — you must demonstrate your site security

SAQ D — Full self-assessment (everything):
  Required if:
    ✗ Your servers receive or process PANs (even to immediately tokenize)
    ✗ You store encrypted PANs
    ✗ You have your own card vault
  Coverage: all 78 requirement areas
  This is effectively a mini-QSA audit

SAQ B, B-IP, C, C-VT: For physical POS terminal scenarios — less relevant for fintech APIs
```

**The architecture implication for SAQ A eligibility:**
```
Achieving SAQ A means:
  The payment gateway (Razorpay, Stripe, JusPay) provides a JavaScript SDK
  that renders card input fields in iframes hosted on the GATEWAY'S domain
  Your JavaScript on your page cannot access iframe content (same-origin policy)
  The card number goes directly: customer keystrokes → gateway iframe → gateway server
  Your servers: never see the PAN → never in scope → SAQ A eligible

Common mistake that breaks SAQ A eligibility:
  Building your own card input form (<input type="text" id="card-number">)
  JavaScript reads the value: document.getElementById('card-number').value
  Your JS sends it anywhere (even directly to the gateway via fetch())
  → Your JavaScript HANDLED the card number → SAQ A-EP at minimum, possibly SAQ D
  → Your page must be secured against XSS, supply chain attacks on JS libraries
```

**Q13. What is a QSA (Qualified Security Assessor), what does the QSA audit process look like for a Level 1 fintech, and how should your engineering team prepare?**
A: A QSA is a company certified by PCI SSC to conduct on-site PCI-DSS assessments and produce a Report on Compliance (ROC) for Level 1 merchants and service providers. The audit is a multi-month engagement that examines people, processes, and technology:

**The QSA audit process:**
```
Phase 1 — Scoping (2–4 weeks):
  QSA interviews: what systems store/process/transmit CHD?
  Network diagram review: identify all data flows involving card data
  Scope boundary agreement: which systems are CDE vs. out of scope?
  Output: agreed CDE scope → determines what the audit covers

Phase 2 — Documentation review (2–4 weeks):
  Security policies reviewed against each PCI requirement
  Architecture diagrams reviewed for network segmentation
  Key management procedures reviewed
  Incident response plan reviewed
  Vulnerability scanning results reviewed (ASV reports)
  Penetration test results reviewed
  Change management records reviewed

Phase 3 — Technical testing (2–3 weeks on-site / remote):
  Configuration review of all in-scope systems (OS, DB, application, network)
  Interview developers: "How do you ensure card data doesn't end up in logs?"
  Interview DBAs: "Show me the schema for payment data tables"
  Interview ops: "Walk me through how you rotate encryption keys"
  Network segmentation testing: can out-of-scope systems reach CDE systems?
  Sample testing: review code changes for PCI-relevant controls
  Access control review: who has access to CDE systems? Is MFA enforced?

Phase 4 — Reporting (2–4 weeks):
  QSA produces ROC (Report on Compliance) — detailed findings per requirement
  Non-compliant findings → remediation required before ROC can attest compliance
  Final ROC → submitted to acquiring bank / card networks
  Attestation of Compliance (AOC) signed by merchant and QSA
```

**Tip for the interview:** At 5 YOE in fintech, PCI-DSS questions are asked as design challenges, not compliance checklists. The signal the examiner is looking for: **scope reduction as the primary architectural goal** (Q3) — the answer that demonstrates you understand that minimizing the CDE is worth more than any other single control. Know the **tokenization vs. encryption distinction** precisely (Q4) — encryption keeps systems in scope because they could decrypt; tokenization removes systems from scope because they cannot detokenize. For the card-on-file scenario (Q14), be ready to draw the full data flow from browser to repayment charge, identifying exactly where the PAN exists and where only the token exists — this is the question that separates engineers who've shipped payment features from those who've only read about it. The **absolute CVV prohibition** (Q7) is a frequent gotcha — know that no form of CVV storage is acceptable under any circumstances, including encrypted. In the RBI/Indian fintech context, connect PCI-DSS to the RBI's card-on-file tokenisation framework (CoFT) — RBI mandated that all merchants replace stored PANs with network tokens by October 2022, making tokenization not just a PCI best practice but a regulatory requirement in India specifically.
